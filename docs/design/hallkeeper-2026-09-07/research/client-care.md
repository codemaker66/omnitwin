# Client care: the hallkeeper as a dependable human host

Research and original design proposals, 7 September 2026. Scope: the hallkeeper's relationship with clients, bookers, guests and venue administrators, including service recovery. No interviews were conducted for this work. The imagined veteran is a design perspective, not a claimed biography or an evidence source. All exchanges below are fictional examples, not venue records or mandatory staff scripts.

The desired outcome is that the organiser can be present at their own event and the hallkeeper can take pride in looking after them. A polished sheet helps only if it gives the hallkeeper time, trustworthy information, authority and a reliable way to finish what they promised.

## What the experienced hallkeeper would wish for

The following are original product hypotheses to validate with working hallkeepers and organisers.

**“Tell me what this person is trying to make happen.”** A layout shows positions; it seldom explains the precious moment. Add one optional, organiser-confirmed line: “What matters most today?” Examples might be that everybody hears a retiring colleague, that the couple get ten quiet minutes before greeting guests, or that relatives can gather without a formal speech. This is useful intent, not an inferred personality profile.

**“Do not make me discover a promise while apologising for it.”** Before the event reaches operations, venue administration should hand over the actual promised experience alongside the latest brief: what is included, what is still conditional, any exceptions, who agreed them and which client decisions are outstanding. A hallkeeper can flag “I cannot deliver this as stated” with a practical alternative. Receipt of the pack means received; it does not mean physically ready or agreement to an impossible promise.

**“Let me remain their person without becoming everybody's technician.”** Distinguish the **client contact** from the **person doing the work**. Nora can own an organiser's update while an AV lead fixes the microphone and Ellis decides any material concession. Changing the technician does not require the client to repeat the story or find a new contact. Cover includes this relationship, not just a list of open tasks.

**“Give me permission for ordinary kindness and support for hard decisions.”** A venue should define modest, practical service discretion and available resources. Show the relevant limit when a decision arises. Do not place every glass of water behind approval, or suggest that a hallkeeper has unlimited money, staffing or permission to change the agreed event.

**“Help me put the screen away.”** Before an arrival, show a brief that can be read in seconds: preferred form of address if supplied, whom to meet, where and when, one meaningful preference, current promises and unresolved concerns. Then let the hallkeeper greet the person with attention. Capture the result afterwards by a short note or a few meaningful actions; avoid asking them to fill a form while somebody is speaking.

## The client care loop

These sample exchanges show the desired relationship; staff should use their own natural voice.

| Moment | Example exchange | What the product must support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before arrival | “I'll meet you at the agreed entrance. Would a short room walk-through help, or would you prefer to settle in first?” | A confirmed meeting arrangement, named host and preference. No invented route or default assumption about assistance. |
| Greeting | “Welcome. I'm Nora, looking after the hall today. I have your latest brief. Is there anything that has changed since you spoke to the events team?” | An accurate arrival brief and one discreet place to capture a new request; do not make the client repeat the whole plan. |
| Understanding the room | “Let's look at where your first speaker will stand. What would you like them to see when they look up?” | A shared view of the current layout with orientation and event intent. Client preference remains distinct from technical feasibility. |
| A small need | “Would you like some water here, or would you prefer I leave you a moment?” | Optional, ordinary hospitality with a quiet “declined” outcome. No repeated prompts or diagnosis of the person's mood. |
| A request outside the plan | “I can check the extra places with our duty manager. I'll come back at 14:25 with the options.” | A bounded update promise, current plan unchanged, decision owner and reminder. Do not turn “I'll check” into “confirmed”. |
| A service failure | “I'm sorry the sound is letting you down. Is being heard at the back the main problem? I'm getting our AV lead; I'll stay your contact.” | The guest's actual problem, immediate next action, specialist owner and person responsible for the next update. |
| A delay | “The replacement is still being tested. I don't have a confirmed finish time yet. Here are the options we have checked.” | Facts, uncertainty and reviewed options. A missed promised update appears before the client needs to chase. |
| Checking recovery | “Can you hear clearly from here now? Is there anything else about this that still isn't working for you?” | Physical fix recorded separately from client outcome: confirmed, still unresolved, or no opportunity to check. |
| Leaving | “Your labelled items are ready with the agreed collection person. Ellis will follow up on the issue we discussed, using your preferred contact method.” | Actual custody/collection state, retained issue owner and agreed follow-up. Do not close the issue merely because the booking ended. |

Keep safety and medical response outside ordinary service recovery: use the venue's verified procedure and appropriate responsible people. Hospitality language must never delay an urgent response or make a technical assurance that has not been established.

## Four small tools that make the care dependable

1. **Promises to keep.** A short list of commitments made to a client, each with words that reflect what was promised, person making it, recipient, due time and outcome. Separate a promise to *update* from a promise to *deliver*. “Nora will return by 14:25” is actionable even when a repair estimate is unknown. The list follows accepted cover; it never silently disappears at shift end.
2. **The thing that matters.** One event-intent line and a few explicit service preferences beside the room brief. Show who supplied each preference and its scope. “Please check with the organiser before moving the memorial photographs” is practical; an invented interpretation of the family's emotions is not.
3. **Stay with the problem.** One issue links the client's words, operational task, decision and follow-up. It can show “Equipment replaced; organiser check due”, preventing a misleading “resolved” badge. The appropriate internal owner can change while the client contact remains clear.
4. **A quiet contact cue.** Show when to approach the organiser, the preferred channel and a deputy for routine queries. Respect ceremony, speech and private-family windows while preserving urgent escalation. This protects meaningful moments without losing requests in a mute mode.

The first three deserve visible space in the event sheet. The detailed history should open on demand; a hallkeeper does not need a customer-management console permanently competing with the room.

## Act, ask, or involve the responsible person

These are proposed categories for the venue to configure, not new authority granted by the software.

| Situation | Expected response | Boundary shown at the moment of need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary comfort or wayfinding within the current brief and the person's role | Offer or act with the guest's agreement | Available resource, applicable room instruction and a way to decline politely |
| A personal preference or physical assistance | Ask before acting; speak to the person concerned | Do not infer preference from appearance, age, disability, title or event category |
| Layout, guest count, timing, cost or supplier change | Capture the request and explain the next confirmed update | Current brief, change owner, consequence review and no unearned commitment |
| A technical fault | Use trained actions within role; involve the specialist | Person doing the work, what is known and the client update owner |
| Complaint requiring compensation or an exception | Understand the impact, use defined discretion or involve the duty manager | The venue's actual discretion and decision limit; no invented budget |
| Threat, abuse, safeguarding concern or urgent hazard | Involve the appropriate duty/security/emergency route | Care for staff and bystanders; no requirement to placate someone at any cost |

## Remember the useful detail; protect the person

This is a privacy-preserving product proposal, not a legal interpretation. Role access, retention and disclosure need the venue's approved policy.

| Information | Appropriate treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Agreed event requirements and ordinary team instructions | Shared with the people who need them to deliver the event; changes remain traceable |
| A guest's requested service adjustment | Store the minimum actionable instruction, source, event scope and permitted audience; re-confirm when appropriate |
| A sensitive explanation | Prefer not to record it when the service instruction suffices. If genuinely needed, use a restricted record with an explicit purpose and retention rule |
| A disputed account or complaint | Separate reported statement from observed fact. Record actions and evidence without insulting labels or speculative motives |
| A lasting preference | Ask whether the organiser wants it remembered; provide review/correction and expiry. Do not silently turn a one-event request into a permanent profile |

For example, the shared instruction can be “Please take routine questions to the nominated organiser during the reception.” It need not expose a family disagreement. Restricted information must not leak into a wall display, notification preview, supplier pack, general printout or offline cache. A private note must also not become the sole repository for an instruction another responsible person needs to deliver.

## Different occasions, without stereotypes

| Occasion | Useful questions and priorities | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wedding or partnership celebration | Who can answer routine questions so the couple can participate? Which moments should remain private? Which names, entrances, photographs or timings must be handled exactly? Who owns personal items at collection? | Assuming gender roles, family relationships or that conspicuous attention is welcome; moving sentimental items as ordinary stock |
| Corporate meeting or conference | Who represents the organiser on site? Who owns presentation content and cues? Which decisions can the floor contact make? Are visible branding, confidential material, speaker preparation or departure deadlines especially important? | Publicly discussing a presenter's fault, broadcasting confidential agenda detail, or promising compatibility without the relevant test |
| Memorial or wake | How would the organiser like the gathering described? Who should receive questions? Is the welcome public or by invitation? Would flexible arrival, private space or a quiet departure help? | Assuming everyone is related, prescribing a mood, repeated explanations of personal circumstances, ceremonial automation or immediate promotional feedback requests |

These are invitations to understand each organiser. An event type must not auto-fill private preferences or decide how people should feel.

## Pressure tests and field validation

- The sound is repaired while Nora is covered for a break: Maya sees that the organiser still needs a check-back, not only that the technical task is done.
- A client says an administrator promised a different layout: preserve their account, find the source and involve the right person privately; do not force the client to referee an internal disagreement.
- The organiser declines a comfort offer: it stays declined for the relevant moment and does not become an overdue hospitality task.
- A memorial organiser requests a private note: the setup crew receives any needed action without the sensitive explanation or notification preview.
- A service update is due while a speech is underway: the contact cue identifies the agreed deputy or next suitable moment, while urgent matters retain their established route.
- The client leaves before a recovery check: show “Client outcome not yet confirmed” and the agreed follow-up owner; do not manufacture satisfaction.

Validate through observation and rehearsal with working hallkeepers and representative organisers. Look for fewer repeated explanations, promises that survive handover, less time looking at the screen during greetings, timely follow-through, and whether staff feel able to use their judgement. Also ask what the system made awkward. Do not judge care by smile counts, inferred emotions, guest sentiment scores or speed alone. Staffing and actual authority remain necessary; software cannot substitute for either.
