### The cap's premise, measured over the geometry

The pull must never reach a solid's own far side. A solid thinner than the pull would draw its far side, in the cut's colour, over its own near side.

- **The first check was wrong (c35e5670).** It took the gap between two faces as the difference of their planes' offsets from the origin. For faces a fraction of a degree out of parallel, far from the origin, that is not their distance: it read the dome's ribs, four-sided tubes 49.5 mm across (a 35 mm radius), as 8.4, 9.9 and 12.8 mm of copper. It compared only parallel faces, so it missed 4.0 mm of stone between two faces 3.5° apart. So c35e5670's message, and this log's draft, were wrong that the front's thinnest solid was the dome's 8.4 mm copper, and mutation B's failure on that copper was a false alarm (the cap's own test did catch it). The second review found it: four dimensions, two skeptics on each finding and a critic for what was missed.
- **The check now (1222b31a, ec719d0c).** For any two faces of one solid within 10° of looking opposite ways (the front's sharpest edge is 16°), with the solid between them and one over the other, the gap is read from one face's own plane, along its normal, at every corner of the area the two share. Only faces near each other are compared, through a grid. A pair whose mean gap is under 1e-5 m counts as touching; that was 0.1 mm, which would have let a sliver that thin pass, until the third review (ec719d0c).
- **What it finds, the same at both detail levels** (`thin-places.log`): 4.0 mm of stone over each pavilion doorway's crown, then the 12 mm glass, and nothing else under 13 mm. The copper is nowhere under 49.2 mm, and the ribs read 49.5 mm.
- **The doorways' crowns stay as surveyed.** The ground floor's wall runs its full depth up to the hall's floor, 3.79 m over the pavement, and each pavilion doorway's arch crowns at 3.786 m (its top, 3.025 m, and its rise, 0.761 m).
