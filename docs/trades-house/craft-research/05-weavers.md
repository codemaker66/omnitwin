# Incorporation of Weavers

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Weavers are the craft of interlacement — the conviction that separate, fragile threads, crossed patiently and honestly, become something none of them could be alone. Everything in their five-hundred-year record repeats that figure: warp and weft, master and prentice, free craft and poor's box, burgh weaver and suburb weaver bound by contract instead of war. Their motto is not "weave cloth"; it is "Weave Trust with Truth" — because a web with one rotten thread is a lie you can see against the light, and a weaver's work, unlike a mason's wall, is inspected by every hand that ever touches it. They were the quietest and most democratic of Glasgow's fourteen crafts — one-year deacons, votes taken by a clerk, canvassing punished as sedition — and the most stubborn about worth: work "not sufficient" earned *no payment at all*. Patience, pattern, mutual burden, and a long memory: that is the weaver.

## History

- **Middle Ages – 1514.** The craft existed long before its charter. A minute of 8 February 1658 records the reading of "the haill old actis extractit out of the buikis for the yeir 1514, and sen syne" — the earliest documented proof of an organised Glasgow weaver craft, and the date the Incorporation counts from ([Old Glasgow Weavers, 1908 records, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **4 June 1528 — Seal of Cause.** Granted by the Provost, Bailies and Council with consent of Archbishop Gavin Dunbar, feudal lord of Glasgow. No copy survives; its wording is known only because the Act of Parliament of 1681 recites it. It fixed five-year apprenticeships, entry fees to maintain "divine service at their alter," booth rights for those "found sufficient and worthy thereto in Practick," and — its most quoted line — the creed that "all encreasement of virtue practick and knowledge stand in good beginning and… persevere to finall end" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Weavers of Glasgow – A Brief History](https://www.weaversofglasgow.org/history)).
- **16 February 1605 — the Reformation re-grant.** The old "Lettere of deikinheid" was renewed as a new Seal of Cause, converting the altar dues of "superstitious usis" into relief of "thair decayit brethereine… and uther godlie and guid workis" — the moment charity replaced candle-wax as the craft's purpose. The parchment survives, seal intact ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **1605 — Letter of Guildry.** The Trades House is founded; the weavers count 30 burgesses of the 363 trades-rank burgesses on Glasgow's first roll of citizens ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)). In the Trades House roll of fourteen the Weavers stand fifth, after Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners and Maltmen ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **8 May 1605 — Gorbals agreement.** First treaty with the weavers of Bridgend/Gorbals, made "for the speciall luife favour and kyndnes" borne to Provost Sir George Elphinstoune, their landlord: 8s yearly per man at Beltane, Lammas, Hallowmas and Candlemas ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **19 July / 17 September 1681.** Charter of confirmation by Archbishop Arthur Ross, ratified by Act of Parliament: old fines "superstitiously bestowed on their blind devotions" converted to the poor of the craft; each pound of wax commuted to £4 Scots ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **1683–1793 — the lost century.** All minute books for this span have vanished; the craft's middle age is "a complete blank" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **23 February 1725 — Calton contract.** Full treaty with the weaver colony of Calton and Blackfaulds: their own yearly-elected "oversman" and six assessors, essays judged jointly, fines split between the two poor funds, appeals to the Glasgow deacon ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **1771–1777 — the equality suit.** Deacon William Bell and the "ten Incorporated Trades last in the order of the Roll" petitioned against the greater representation of the first four trades; a Court of Session declarator of 1777 gave the weavers their standing representation of the deacon and three assistants — the four representatives they still send ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **1846.** Parliament withdraws all exclusive trading privileges; the Incorporation's monopoly ends and its charitable identity takes over entirely ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **10 December 1922.** Arms matriculated with the Lord Lyon ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
- **7 August 1925.** Registered charity (No. SC008691) ([OSCR](https://www.oscr.org.uk/about-charities/search-the-register/charity-details?number=SC008691); [weaversofglasgow.org](https://www.weaversofglasgow.org/)).
- **9 March 2014.** The craft celebrated its 500th anniversary, counted from 1514 ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **Today.** Master Court of 14 plus Deacon, Late Deacon, Collector, Late Collector and Clerk — "our current Clerk is the first woman in our 500 year history." Aims: preservation of the craft, relief of poverty, advancement of education; textile awards, student grants, Stirling Castle tapestries (2002–05) ([weaversofglasgow.org](https://www.weaversofglasgow.org/history); [weavers-today](https://www.weaversofglasgow.org/weavers-today)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **The blank where the saint should be.** The 1681 Parliament transcription of the lost 1528 charter reads: the craft desired "to the honor of St. —— to be their Patron" — and the name is *blank*, twice more in the same deed. Somewhere between 1528 and 1681 the Reformation scraped the patron's very name out of the record, and no one now knows who the Glasgow weavers prayed to. The craft that keeps five hundred years of minutes cannot recover one word ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The wine vow of 1621.** The whole craft, "haifing ane greit regaird of thair awin commoun weill," swore off wine for a full year — because daily drinking had "drawin tha fra thair craftis, spent thair tyme ydillie, abuisit thair bodies, and wraikit thair haill guidis" — with the magnificently human reservation: *except in sickness, or if a stranger pays* ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The apprentice kept in the dark, 1615.** Apprentice William Crawfurd complained his master Archibald Thomesoune was "conceilling fra him diveris poyntis of his craft, specially hyding fra him all playdis he weifes and sea bumbasies." The Master Court ordered the master to give the boy every third pair of plaids that came into the house to warp and weave, and to teach him fustian-work, on pain of £4 per failure. The craft ruled: knowledge withheld is theft ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Canvassing punished as sedition, 1603.** Whoever solicited votes to be made deacon would never be a master again, would pay a new upset, and would "mak his publict repentatioun on the pillar in the kirk as ane persoune seditious and mowear of truble" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The roup of Kirkland's hagbut, 1607.** William Kirkland, fined £6 for wronging the deacon, surrendered a hagbut (gun) in pledge and refused to redeem it — so the craft had it auctioned by three sworn men at the mercat cross ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Meetings in the Bishop's Castle.** In 1665 the craft convened "at the foir toure of the castell of Glasgow," and that summer the masters stood watch "day about" over the masons building the craft's new house in Robertson's land, reporting stone, lime and sand to the deacon each night ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The ride to Edinburgh, 1689.** Late deacon William Boill and six freemen rode eight days to Edinburgh, summoned before the Privy Council over the election of the deacon-convener in Revolution-year Glasgow, £100 Scots spent on man, horse and "horse byres" — and the craft solemnly audited every penny ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Buying the key, 1676.** Finding they paid only £15 yearly to the deacon-convener's house "it being soe small," the weavers voted to *double* their own dues to £30 — in exchange for "ane key of the deacon convineris box" and as many men on the House as any other trade. Dignity, purchased at cost, on their own motion ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **"Like to starve for want of bread," 1701.** With the trade "almost ruined" and its poor starving, every freeman's quarter dues were doubled for seven years — decided by vote of the very men who would pay ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The Calton volley, 1787.** The journeymen weavers of Calton — the *unfree* suburb colony the Incorporation had contracted with in 1725 — struck against a 25% wage cut. Webs were cut from the looms of those weaving at the old rate; on 3 September the magistrates and a detachment of the 39th Regiment under Lieut.-Colonel Kellet met the crowd at Parkhouse in Duke Street; the Riot Act was read and a musket volley killed three weavers outright, six dying in all. Scotland's first working-class martyrs were weavers — outside the Incorporation's walls ([archive.org, quoting M'Gregor](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia: Calton weavers' strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calton_weavers%27_strike)).
- **The Newfoundland dog.** Pagan's History records the first Glasgow power looms, set up in Argyle Street in 1773 and "set in motion by a large Newfoundland dog performing the part of a gin-horse"; a Pollokshaws tenter angrily countered that Adam Kinloch cranked the world's first two power looms by hand in a court off the Gallowgate in 1793. Either way, the hand-loom's doom arrived on a dog's paws ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The radical loom.** In 1820 the weaver James "Purlie" Wilson of Strathaven, aged about 60, was hanged and beheaded before 20,000 on Glasgow Green for his part in the Radical War; weavers Baird and Hardie were executed at Stirling. This is the wider Glasgow weaving world, not the Incorporation itself — but it is why "weaver" and "radical" rhyme in Scots memory ([Maggie Craig, Scotland's Radical Martyrs](https://www.maggiecraig.co.uk/2020/08/30/scotlands-radical-martyrs-james-purlie-wilson/); [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/80283/james-purlie-wilson-1760-1820-radical-reformer)).
- **A Prime Minister on the roll.** In May 1907 the Incorporation admitted as Honorary Member "The Right Honourable Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman… Prime Minister and First Lord of the Treasury, son of a Member" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Weavers at war.** Deacons were bound from 1629 to furnish a musket at their own charge; £200 voted in 1778 to raise the Glasgow Regiment; in 1798, invasion feared, the craft "cheerfully" joined the Trades Battalion of Royal Glasgow Volunteers to defend the city. A Weavers Roll of Honour 1914–18 hangs in the digital museum ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Trades House Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/weavers.html)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Arms.** Matriculated with the Lord Lyon on 10 December 1922 ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)). As borne on the Incorporation's own device: on a blue shield, a silver chevron charged with three thistles, between three leopards' faces each holding a golden weaver's shuttle in its mouth; above, the name-scroll; below, the motto ([Incorporation crest image](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/644a67d7d8c0b42f41b04c27/t/64525930887ff3771279c536/1683118384633/WEVAERS+LOGO+TP.png)). *Note: this description is read from the Incorporation's published crest; the official Lyon Register blazon text was not accessible online.*
- **Motto: "WEAVE TRUST WITH TRUTH."** A deliberate Scottish turn on the London Weavers' Company motto "Weave Truth with Trust" (London: arms granted 1490 — three leopards' faces with shuttles, chevron with *roses*; Glasgow substitutes *thistles* for the roses) ([heraldry-wiki: Worshipful Company of Weavers](https://www.heraldry-wiki.com/heraldrywiki/index.php/Worshipful_Company_of_Weavers)). Aberdeen's weavers likewise bore "three leopards' heads erased Argent having in their mouths a weaver's shuttle Or" (1682) ([heraldry-wiki: Aberdeen Weavers](https://www.heraldry-wiki.com/wiki/Craft_and_Incorporation_of_the_Weavers_of_Aberdeen)) — the leopard-and-shuttle is the shared badge of the British weaver guilds.
- **Symbolism.** The leopard's face holding the shuttle in its jaws: watchfulness carrying the tool of the trade; the spotted pelt itself reads as a woven pattern. The chevron is the weaver's own geometry — the twill line. The thistles plant the English arms in Scottish ground. The motto states the theology of cloth: truth is the warp, trust is the weft; neither holds without the other.
- **Patron saint.** Unknown — and gloriously so. The surviving recital of the 1528 charter leaves the saint's name blank in every place it occurs ("the honor and reparation of the said Alter of St. ——"). The weavers kept an altar (almost certainly in Glasgow Cathedral's era of craft altars), lit by fines paid in pounds of wax and a weekly penny from every booth-holder, "man or woman" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)). [extrapolated] Elsewhere in Scotland the weaver crafts held the altar of **St Severus** (conflated in Scots usage with St Serf) — Dundee's weavers explicitly so ([Nine Trades of Dundee archive](https://archive.ninetradesofdundee.co.uk/download/weavers/weavers_documents_and_pictures/historic/St.%20Severus%20Altar.pdf), PDF, not fetched) — and Glasgow's lost dedication was plausibly the same; but no Glasgow source confirms it. Treat the blank itself as the canonical fact.

## Rituals & Customs

- **Entry and oath.** Admission required being "found sufficient and worthy thereto in Practick and admitted by the sworn masters" (1528), then burgess-ship of the town; entrants "gave thair oathis *de fideli* as use is." Suburb entrants under the 1725 Calton contract had to "give essay to the satisfaction of the deacon… and six of his masteris" — a trial-piece of weaving judged by fourteen men ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Apprenticeship.** Five years minimum under the 1528 charter, *seven* under the 1605 one, "and na les without dispensatioune." Burgess weavers' sons paid 13s 4d where strangers paid 40s; a man who married "ane frieman wobsteris dochter" entered at the kinsman's rate — marriage wove you in ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Upset, banquet and say drink.** New freemen paid their "upset" plus, from 1612, money in lieu of the formerly "sumpteous" entry banquet and "say drink." New deacons paid for their own "heidwasching" (inaugural carousal) "furt of his awin purs" (1616) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Election.** Deacon chosen yearly at Michaelmas "be voittis of the brethrene" with a clerk to take the poll; one year only, "for avoiding of all superioritie and tyrannie" (1595, re-enacted 1611 and 1618); masters chosen by the lyte system — three of the four on the deacon's leet stand as masters, deacon picks two, craft picks two, "quhilk compleitis aucht" (1613). The annual election dinner (with a fee "to the tounes drumeris") was paid proportionally by those who attended — the 1673 minute the craft cites as the origin of its still-independent annual dinner ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Fines in wax.** Pre-Reformation offences — spoiling work, taking another man's warped web, disobeying the deacon — were paid in pounds of wax "to the light of the said alter"; commuted 1681 to £4 Scots per pound. Unfree incomers caught taking work out of town owed a pound of wax *and a free dinner to the masters* — a fine the 1681 charter abolished as "needles expences without good to the poor," substituting £20 for the poor ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Burials and mortcloths.** Every freeman, duly warned, must convoy a brother's coffin from dwelling-house to kirkyard — coming "mid gait, or half gait" counted as absent (1611). The craft owned black velvet mortcloths, lent at fixed rates (dearer to the unfree, dearest outside the burgh, *ad pios usus*), a quiet income for the poor's box. Masters must appear at quarter accounts and burials *with hats on their heads*, £10 fine, "unalterable in all tyme cuming" (1665) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Relics and insignia.** The Deacon's Box; the Deacon's Bell ("Gift of Niel MacBrayne Deacon 1793"); the Deacon's silver snuff-box (1811); a Fly Shuttle marked "Joseph Adkin 1772"; the Old Wynd Society's throwing-shuttle snuff-box bearing the Weavers' arms; the Collector's Box of 1763 inscribed *"The Weavers Art it is renowned so that rich nor poor without it cannot go"*; the Collector's silver-mounted snuff-horn with every collector's name from 1834; an ivory mallet (1834); a chain for measuring warps; the Deacon's gold medal and chain (1861); a silver gong ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The Box Opening.** The annual ceremony — members inspecting the funds to see they are rightly used — survives today as the day the year's donations are paid out to charities ([weaversofglasgow.org](https://www.weaversofglasgow.org/history)).
- **The craft's own song.** "The Band O' Weavers," music by J.C. MacLaurin, sits in the Trades House library ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
- **Precedence and representation.** Fifth of the fourteen crafts on the Trades House roll; four representatives sent yearly to the House; deacon-conveners from the craft include Patrick Bryce (1658), John Buchanan (1659), Archibald Newbigging (1799–1800), John Alston (1829–30) and John Neil (1838–39) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **Communal tools.** The Incorporation bought weaving reeds in bulk in Belgium and Holland and loaned or sold them to members — the guild as tool-library ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).

## Sensory World

The Glasgow weaver's shop was his house: by statute of 1595 "onlie ane wollen lome" per freeman's home, and never "ma loomis in thair hous… nor thai have workeris to work thairin" (1606). The room holds the great frame of the loom; the smell is wool-grease and the flour-paste dressing on the warp, flax dust hanging in the window light, tallow and wax candle smoke in winter. The sounds are a language: the soft thud of treadles, the clack and hiss of the thrown shuttle, the beat of the reed packing the weft home, the creak of the beam as the web winds on. Hands know the difference blind: harden (coarse tow cloth) like sacking at 6d the ell; "gam herdin" at 8d; plaiding — Defoe's "stuff cross-striped with Yellow, Red, and other mixtures" for women's veils; "linchie-winchie," linen warp under woollen weft, ancestor of Glasgow's wincey; "sey bumbasie," the fustian a master once hid from his prentice. Cloth is measured in ells against the law's own inches (an ell and two inches wide for fine linen, by the Act of 1661), bleached "without lime" on pain of fine, sealed for export. Reeds come from the Low Countries; pirns are wound by wives and bairns. On burial days the black velvet mortcloth goes over the coffin on its two poles, and every weaver walks behind it, hat on his head ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).

## Values

1. **Truth in the work.** Work "not sufficient" earned *no payment*; sworn masters could enter and "sicht" (inspect) any web, and compensation to the customer was assessed from the offender's own hands (1605). The whole craft understood that "defective work done by any member injures the whole craft" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
2. **Mutual burden.** From the 1605 charter onward every fee, fine and mortcloth shilling flowed "to the help and confort of thair decayit brethereine"; in 1655 "all sort of wnlawis… sail cum in to the use of the poore"; in 1701 the freemen doubled their own dues for seven years because the poor were "like to starve for want of bread" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
3. **Equality among brethren.** One-year deacons "for avoiding of all superioritie and tyrannie" (1595); canvassing = public repentance as "ane persoune seditious" (1603); no undercutting a brother's price and no touting for another's customer (1598–99); the deacon redistributed "overmeikle work" from the overloaded to "honest men" of the craft (1599) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
4. **Perseverance.** The 1528 charter's own creed: increase of virtue and knowledge "stand in good beginning and… continue in rise and persevere to finall end." A craft where every web is thousands of identical throws of the shuttle canonised patience as its founding text ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
5. **Transmission.** Five- then seven-year apprenticeships; the 1615 ruling that a master *must* teach his prentice the hidden points of the craft; spinning schools backed in 1728, Sunday schools in 1794, and today's scholarships, textile awards and sponsored weavers at Kilbarchan and Paisley ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Trades House Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/weavers.html)).
6. **Negotiated peace.** Where other crafts fought interlopers, the weavers wrote treaties — Gorbals 1605 "for speciall luife favour and kyndnes," Calton 1725 "for mantaining mutual love and good neighbourhood," each with courts, appeals and shared poor funds ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
7. **The popular voice.** "All movements in the way of Burgh Reform were carefully and critically examined… always favourable to popular election"; the craft publicly resolved against the landed interest's corn-law scheme in 1786 and spoke on parliamentary reform, the slave trade and religious liberty ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).

## Shadow

Every weaver virtue has its knot pulled tight into a snarl. *Truth in the work* curdles into the inspection state: sworn men entering your house to finger your web, no payment for honest work an inch narrow. *Mutual burden* curdles into the closed shop — the poor's box fed by fines wrung from "outintownis wobsteris," a pound of wax and a forced dinner from every hungry incomer, charity for *the craft's* poor and poinding for everyone else's. *Equality among brethren* becomes the cartel: price floors ("no ell of plaiding better cheap nor 8d") that made solidarity and price-fixing the same act. *Negotiated peace* becomes tribute — Gorbals and Calton bought their looms' liberty quarterly, forever. And the deepest shadow: the Incorporation's freemen were masters within the walls while the real weaving Glasgow — the journeymen of Calton — starved on piece-rates; when they struck in 1787 it was the burgh's magistrates who brought the muskets, and the craft's own historian records the massacre in a chapter placidly titled "Later History." A guild born to guarantee trust ended defending privilege until Parliament "swept away" its rights in 1846 because "the time had gone by" — the web outlived, the weavers still holding the empty loom ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia: Calton weavers' strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calton_weavers%27_strike)).

## Rivals & Kin

- **The first four trades (Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners, Maltmen).** The structural rivals: they held more seats on the deacon-convener's council "without any reason for it appearing in the Books," until the weavers led the ten lesser trades to the Court of Session and won equal footing (1771–77) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The Tailors.** Immediately upstream in cloth and above them in precedence — the craft that cut what weavers made. No recorded open war in the weavers' minutes, but the roll order itself (Tailors 2nd, Weavers 5th) is the quiet standing grievance ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **Bonnetmakers & Dyers.** Kin trades of the loom and vat, incorporated separately (14th of the roll) — the finishing end of the weaver's world ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/)).
- **Suburb weavers — kin held at contract's length.** Gorbals/Bridgend (treaty 1605), Calton/Blackfaulds (individual licence 1710; treaty 1725; and the 1733 petition of eight Calton men whom their *own* oversman refused to examine, so Glasgow examined and admitted them, holding half the fine in trust "so soon as the said weaveris of Caltoun have ane oversman… regularly chosin"). Edinburgh, Westport and Potterrow weavers were cited in 1725 as the model of such arrangements ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **Rutherglen weavers.** Neighbouring incorporation, rarely mentioned; their deacon John Harvie was made free of Glasgow in 1730 "for his good services to the Toun… in particular at the election of Thos. Smith, M.P." — a vote-for-freedom bargain recorded without blush ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).
- **The friendly-society cousins.** The Weavers Society of Anderston (office-bearers from 1738), the Govan Weavers Society, the Old Wynd Society (whose throwing-shuttle snuff-box the Incorporation's arms adorn), and the Calton Weavers (rules printed 1835) — the halo of weaver brotherhoods around the chartered core ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
- **Paisley.** The Incorporation sponsors the Sma' Shot Cottages in Paisley and formerly a weaver at Kilbarchan's Weaver's Cottage ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-weavers/); [Trades House Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/weavers.html)). [extrapolated] Paisley's own weaver legend — the "sma' shot" binding thread the manufacturers refused to pay for, fought over until the weavers won their Sma' Shot Day holiday — is adopted kin-lore here, not an Incorporation record.
- **The merchants and manufacturers.** The permanent adversary-partner: from "incommers weavers taking out the stuff aff the toun" (1681) to the linen-breadth litigation of the 1680s, when weavers of Glasgow, Brigend and beyond petitioned against a law that confiscated honest narrow cloth and left them unable to "get seall for their cloath abroad" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)).

## Lexicon

Period and craft words a Scots narrator can thread through dialogue — all attested in the Incorporation's own records ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft/oldglasgowweaver00mcewuoft_djvu.txt)):

1. **wobster / webster** — a weaver; the "wobstercraft."
2. **wob / web** — the woven piece on or off the loom.
3. **worklumes / worklooms** — the tools of the trade; entrants must have "guid worklumes."
4. **warpit** — warped; a web set up on the loom ("na man take another man's work that is warped of before").
5. **the deikin** — the deacon, head of the craft, "onlie for ane yeir."
6. **the lyte** — the shortleet of candidates for deacon.
7. **upset** — the fee to set up as a master; "ane new upset" also served as a heavy fine.
8. **unlaw** — a statutory fine.
9. **toties quoties** — "as often as it happens": every single offence, no mercy.
10. **ane pund of wax** — the old altar-fine, paid in candle wax "to the light of the said alter."
11. **buith** — booth; the shop of a freeman (held by "man or woman").
12. **herdin / gam herdin** — harden, coarse tow-cloth; the cheapest weave.
13. **plaiding** — striped/checked homespun woollen cloth.
14. **linchie-winchie** — linen-warp, wool-weft cloth; forebear of wincey.
15. **sey bumbasie** — fustian-type cloth; the "hidden point" a master concealed from his prentice.
16. **the elne** — the Scots ell, the cloth measure; "better chap nor aucht penneis the roundest elne."
17. **heidwasching** — the "head-washing," a new deacon's inaugural drinking, at his own purse.
18. **say drink** — the assay-drink owed by a new entrant with his banquet.
19. **mortcloth** — the craft's black velvet funeral pall, lent for a fee *ad pios usus*.
20. **poynding** — distraint; seizing a defaulter's goods (even his hagbut) for roup at the cross.
21. **overmeikle wark** — more work than a man can honestly finish; the deacon may redistribute it.
22. **decayit brethereine** — brethren fallen into poverty; the charter's own object of charity.

## Question Seeds

1. **The Overmeikle Web.** Trade is roaring and you have promised three customers webs you cannot finish "in dewe seasoun." By the craft's own act, if the deacon learns of it he will take the surplus from your loom and deal it out among your brethren — with the fee. Do you confess your greed to the deacon, or drive your family through the nights to keep what you grabbed? *Choosing confession reveals: Mutual burden over private gain (Value 2/3). Choosing the nights reveals the Shadow: solidarity abandoned for the ledger.*
2. **The Third Plaid.** Your apprentice has served four years and his hands are now quicker than yours. The law of 1615 says every third pair of plaids that enters your house is his to warp and weave, and the fustian-craft is his to learn — the same knowledge he will one day use to underbid you in your own wynd. Do you teach him everything, or keep back the one trick that keeps you master? *Teaching reveals: Transmission — the craft outlives the craftsman (Value 5). Withholding is Thomesoune's sin: the web ends with you.*
3. **The Volley at Parkhouse.** September 1787. You are a free master within the walls; the Calton journeymen — men you licensed, whose quarterly shillings feed your poor's box — are cutting webs from scab looms over a starvation wage. The magistrates ask the honest masters to name the ringleaders before worse comes. Name them and the craft keeps the peace it swore to the town; refuse and you stand with the unfree against your own oath. Where do you stand when the muskets level? *Refusing reveals: the brotherhood of the loom is wider than the charter (Values 2/7). Naming them reveals the Shadow: trust woven only inside the walls.*
4. **The Blank Saint.** You are clerk, copying the old charter for Parliament, and where the patron's name stood the parchment is scraped bare. The deacon says: write in a saint — any saint — for a craft should not pray to a blank. Do you invent a comforting name, or copy the blank faithfully and let the loss stand in the record forever? *Copying the blank reveals: Weave Trust with Truth — truth even when it is a hole in the cloth (Value 1). Inventing reveals the consoling lie, the one thread that rots the web.*
5. **The Pound of Wax.** A landward weaver — skilled, starving, no burgess-fine to his name — is caught taking work out of the town. His fine of wax and a forced dinner will light your altar and load your table; his bairns will eat nothing. The law is the law you swore to keep, and the poor's box you swore to fill is filled exactly thus. Do you exact it? *Exacting reveals: Guardianship of the bounds — the craft survives by its walls (Value 6, honestly owned). Waiving it reveals: charity that reaches past the charter — and undermines the box that feeds your own widows. There is no clean thread here; the choice shows which poor you count as yours.*
6. **The Key to the Box.** 1676. The convener's house treats your craft as small because your dues are small. For double dues — money taken straight from the poor's fund's increase — you can buy a key to the box and equal men on the House. Is dignity worth the poor's silver, or is standing a vanity a weaver should be above? *Paying reveals: Equality among brethren is worth real cost — a craft that lets itself be ranked small will be treated small (Value 3). Refusing reveals: the box exists for the decayit brethereine, not the deacon's pride (Value 2). Both are weaver answers; the question is which thread you make the warp.*

### Gaps and honesty notes

- **The 1528 Seal of Cause does not survive**; everything "1528" rests on the 1681 Act of Parliament's recital. Stated as such above.
- **The patron saint's name is genuinely unrecoverable** — blank in the 1681 recital. The St Severus/Serf suggestion is a tagged extrapolation from other Scottish weaver crafts, not a Glasgow fact.
- **The official Lyon blazon (1922) was not verifiable online**; the arms are described from the Incorporation's own published crest image, and the matriculation date from the Trades House library table.
- **Minute books 1683–1793 are lost**, so the 18th-century record is thinner and leans on the 1908 historian's selections and quoted town histories.
- **Precedence** (Weavers fifth) is taken from the consistent Trades House roll order and the 1771 "first four / last ten" petition language; no separate charter of precedence was found.
- **The Sma' Shot dispute** is Paisley lore adjacent to the craft's modern sponsorship, tagged [extrapolated]; it is not an Incorporation record.
