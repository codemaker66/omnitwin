# Incorporation of Cordiners

> Craft #3 in the order of precedence of the fourteen Incorporations of the Trades House of Glasgow. The leather men: barkers, tanners, curriers and souters (shoemakers) united under one name taken from Cordoba, city of the finest leather in the medieval world. "The Gentle Craft."

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Cordiners are the craft of quiet royalty — the trade whose folklore insists that "a shoemaker's son is a Prince born," and who proved it by crowning their own King Crispin and parading him through Glasgow with champions, pages and a Cossack while real kings looked shabby by comparison. They are the men who touch every foot in the city, from the bishop's slipper to the soldier's brogue, and who therefore believe that all dignity walks on their work. Their seal of cause was the last thing Archbishop Beaton signed before fleeing the Reformation; their patron's altar burned candles paid in fines of wax; their minute books run, almost unbroken, from 1550. Third of the fourteen crafts in precedence and third-largest in number when the Trades House was born, the Cordiners carry a specific temperament: patient, close-guarded, ceremonious, stubborn about boundaries, tender about their own poor — a craft that stitches slowly, waxes every thread, and never forgets that leather outlasts the man who cut it.

## History

- **Before incorporation.** Leather workers served Glasgow from its earliest days as a bishop's burgh. An Act of the Scottish Parliament in 1424–25 directed craftsmen in burghs to elect a "heid man" or deacon; in 1431 James I persuaded "mony nobill Craftsmen out of France, Flandiers, and other parties" to replace Scots craftsmen slain in the English wars, and the Glasgow Cordiners' ranks were later augmented by craftsmen from the Netherlands and France ([Steele, Linnell & Bryce, *A Short History of the Incorporation of Cordiners in Glasgow*, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [Trades House — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-cordiners/)).
- **1550 — the first minute.** The Cordiners' Minute of Michaelmas 1550 records the election of a "Dekin," auditors and officials "after the auld wise and consuetude" — proof the craft was already organised and self-governing before its charter ([Trades House — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-cordiners/); [Trades House Virtual Museum — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/cordiners.html)). The minute books survive for 1550–1682, 1759–1956 and from 1993 — the 1682–1758 volume is lost ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **27 February 1558/59 — the Seal of Cause.** The Cordiners and Barkers were granted their Seal of Cause by the Provost, Bailies, Council and Community, with consent of Archbishop James Beaton — the last seal Beaton confirmed before he fled to Paris ahead of the Reformation, taking the cathedral treasures, diocesan registers and relics with him ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)). The seal gave the craft control of entry by apprenticeship, exclusive trading rights within the burgh, quality regulation, and allocation of market booths ([Trades House — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-cordiners/)).
- **The debunked 1460 claim.** The craft's first historian, Ex-Deacon William Campbell (1883), claimed recognition as early as 27 June 1460; the 2022 official history notes the petitioners' names on that "1460" ratification are identical to those of the second charter of **27 June 1569** — same date, same names — so the older claim is treated as a copying error ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **27 June 1569 — the post-Reformation regrant**, and a ratification of **30 September 1693**, both commemorated on the Deacon's 1881 medal: "Incorporated 27th February, 1558, and 27th June, 1569. Ratified 30th September, 1693" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **6 February 1605 — the Letter of Guildry** created the Trades House and Merchants House. In that first year Glasgow enrolled 363 burgesses of trade rank, of whom **fifty were Cordiners — the third most numerous craft** after the Tailors (65) and Maltmen (55). The order of precedence fixed then has never changed: Hammermen, Tailors, **Cordiners**, Maltmen, Weavers, Bakers, Skinners, Wrights, Coopers, Fleshers, Masons, Gardeners, Barbers, Bonnetmakers & Dyers ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Decline of privilege.** The Reform Act (1832), Burgh Reform Act (1833) and Burgh Trading Act (1846) abolished exclusive craft privileges. The Cordiners considered converting to an annuity society and abandoned the idea in 1848, choosing instead to continue as a charitable incorporation — collecting entry money, enrolling members, and giving ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Royal Charter, 1919** from George V (the official short history gives 1918 in one passage and 1919 in another; the Trades House and the craft's own site say 1919) ([Trades House — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-cordiners/); [cordiners-glasgow.com](https://www.cordiners-glasgow.com/)).
- **16 December 2004 — Supplementary Charter** sealed with the Great Seal of Scotland after a Petition to the Queen in Privy Council, at last permitting women as full members; the Cordiners had voted unanimously for full admission (rejecting a segregated "Ladies Section" as demeaning) at their 2003 Annual Meeting. **Dr Christine Linnell became the first female Deacon in 2013** ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Today.** The Incorporation sends 6 representatives to the Trades House, is Scottish Charity SC016220, and runs the "Body and Sole" footwear charity (founded 2018) ([Trades House — Cordiners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-cordiners/); [cordiners-glasgow.com](https://www.cordiners-glasgow.com/)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **The last seal of the old faith (1558).** The Cordiners' charter was the final act of Archbishop James Beaton before he fled to Paris with Glasgow Cathedral's treasures, registers and relics as the Reformation bore down. The craft's founding document is thus a relic of the vanishing world it outlived ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The crafts save the Cathedral (1579).** When the University's principal, Mr Melville, roused a mob to pull down Glasgow Cathedral, legend says the Crafts "ran immediately to arms" and threatened to **bury Melville under any stones he pulled down**. Summoned before the young James VI, the craft leaders were applauded: too many churches had been destroyed already ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)). Glasgow Cathedral still carries the Cordiners' arms in the Trades House window (1951) and on the pew-ends (1957).
- **Langside (1568).** At Mary Queen of Scots' last battle, the Cordiners fought on the side of the Regent — and Glasgow's crafts were rewarded for their zeal ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf), citing A. M. Scott, *The Battle of Langside*, 1885).
- **Shoes for the army (1639–40).** In the Bishops' Wars, cordiner **John Andelsone was made captain of a Glasgow company** (June 1639, with a disbursement for swan's feathers), and in October–November 1640 the craft was paid £239 12s for **eight score pairs of shoes sent to the camp** — the Cordiners' special war-duty was hose and shoes ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf), citing the Burgh Records).
- **Bonnie Prince Charlie's boots (1745–46).** The Jacobite army occupying Glasgow demanded 6,000 cloth coats, 12,000 linen shirts, **6,000 pairs of shoes**, tartan hose and blue bonnets — delivered at a cost of £5,000. Provost Cochrane recorded that the only recruit the Prince won in all Glasgow was "**Ane drunken shoemaker, who must have fled his country for debt, if he had not for treason**" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The Deacon against the Union (1706).** Glasgow rioted against the proposed Union with England; **John Stevenson, Deacon of the Cordiners**, was one of three men sent to Edinburgh to lay Glasgow's protest before the Scottish Parliament (minuted 17 November 1706). When troops finally came, the rioters surrendered their weapons to the deacons of the crafts — "the only constituted authorities left in the city" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The champion who died in Wallace's armour (1790).** At Glasgow's King Crispin procession of 1790, the man playing the King's Champion hired the reputed **armour of William Wallace from Dumbarton Castle** and was "so chilled and overburdened" wearing it through the streets that he died soon afterwards ([Marwick, *'Sons of Crispin'*, PhD thesis, St Andrews](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y), citing the Glasgow Herald, 5 November 1821).
- **The procession that outshone a coronation (1818).** In *The Steamboat* (1822), novelist John Galt compared George IV's coronation **unfavourably** with Glasgow's King Crispin procession of 1818 — the shoemakers "being in the practice of crowning and processing with King Crispin" had more experience of majesty than Westminster. The Glasgow order of march included the Champion, Indian King, pages, princes — and a Don Cossack ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)). Five hundred Glasgow journeymen shoemakers had already, in 1779–80, described themselves as "distinguished by the name of St Crispin's Society"; a Glasgow lodge of the Royal St Crispin Society was chartered 24 September 1866, and King Crispin walked in Glasgow again for William IV's coronation (1831) and a royal baptism (1864) ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)).
- **Six footballs for a burgess-ship (1589/90).** Cordiner **Johnne Neill** was made Burgess and Guild brother free of charge — on condition he supply "six good and sufficient footballs for the town," or pay 20 shillings ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf), citing the Burgh Records).
- **The man who moved the gallows (1574).** Cordiner **Thomas Craig** was convicted of shifting the town gallows "at his own hand" away from their accustomed place near the town — and ordered to put them back ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Escape with a drawn dagger (1605).** Cordiner **Thomas Neill**, warded in the tolbooth, confessed to breaking out of ward and "coming down the tollbooth stair with one drawn dagger and for wounding and blooding of John Towris upon the head to the great effusion of his blood" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The Bannatyne election scandal (1759–60).** Deacon **John Bannatyne** ran an election so irregular the Trades House annulled it; he declared in open court "that he would not be trampled upon by a sett of prepossessed gentlemen," was barred from House votes for six months — and returned the next April humbly begging to obey ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The cursing of the Deacon Convener (1761).** Cordiner **John Finlay**, at a public craft meeting, repeatedly cursed and called Deacon Convener Daniell Munro "a damned bitch, bougor" — and was sent to the magistrates for fine and imprisonment ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Keepers of the fire-leather (1744).** Cordiner Robert Freebairn was paid £4 12s 2d "for dressing and mending the leather buckets and the pipes of the fire machines" — repairing the city's fire-fighting leather appears repeatedly in the records, always assigned to Cordiners ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The lost box and the stolen chain.** The craft's oak charter box of 1708 ("Queen Anne oak, with carved ends and metal handles, two locks, and brass plate in front"), exhibited at the 1938 Empire Exhibition, was "mislaid" in the late twentieth century and has never been found. The Deacon's chain — possibly the oldest in the Trades House, first minuted 1798, redesigned 1881 after Campbell savaged its bad heraldry — was stolen in the early 1990s; today's is the third incarnation ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Patron: St Crispin** (with his brother St Crispinian) — Roman missionary brothers at Soissons who made shoes by day and preached by night, martyred c. 285–286; their cult reached Scotland via France, and medieval Scottish cordiners maintained altars to both ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y); [Wikipedia — Crispin and Crispinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crispin_and_Crispinian)). The Glasgow Cordiners **paid for the upkeep of St Crispin's altar in Glasgow Cathedral**, visiting on the Saint's Day (25 October) with money for candles and masses for the dead; members who insulted the Deacon were fined **a pound of wax for the Saint's altar** ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)). After the Reformation the saint survived in Scotland, secularised, as "**King Crispin**" ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)).
- **Arms:** the cordiners' emblem across Scotland is the **crescent-shaped cutting knife (half-moon knife) ensigned with a crown**. The Cordiners' section of the ancient crest of the 14 Incorporations shows "a crown above the traditional leather cutter"; the craft applied to the Lord Lyon in 1921 and the Clerk travelled to Edinburgh to accept the Patent of Armorial Bearings (cost £48), used on craft papers ever since ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)). The pre-1921 (unapproved) arms survive on the WWI Belgian refugees' bench in the Trades Hall entrance ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Motto: "God is our Hope."** This is the closing line of the Incorporation's official 2022 short history, set alone as the craft's device; the 1881 medal is described as bearing "the arms of the craft and the motto above" without quoting the words. *[thin evidence: no independent online source spells out the Glasgow blazon or motto text; treat the wording as reported by the craft's own history rather than independently verified]* ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Symbolism.** The knife is the tool that cuts all leather — displayed as a badge of unity among the leather trades since antiquity ([HCC — Arms of the Honourable Cordwainers' Company](https://www.thehcc.org/arms.htm)). The crown above it carries the craft's proudest saying — "**a shoemaker's son is a Prince born**," from Deloney's *The Gentle Craft* (1597), in which princes disguise themselves as shoemakers and the craft feasts forever after ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)). Kin mottoes echo the crown: the Aberdeen Shoemakers' arms bear "Lord Crown us with Glory" ([Doric Columns — Shoemakers 1484](https://doriccolumns.wordpress.com/7-incorporated-trades/incorporated-trades-arms/shoemakers-1484/)).

## Rituals & Customs

- **The oath.** On election, every master swore an oath before the Deacon to uphold the statutes of the guild — an oath with "a strong religious element"; breaches were fined in pounds of wax for St Crispin's altar ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The Essay.** An apprentice (bound five to nine years, unpaid, forbidden to marry, learning the "mysteries") produced a qualifying piece — the **Essay** — to become a journeyman; a journeyman seeking mastership produced a masterpiece "using his own tools and materials and working in his own time, which in effect meant Sundays." In 1757 even the partners of Glasgow's new shoe factory had to send their foremen to "sit the essay" before the work could be lawful ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)). *[No surviving description of the specific Glasgow cordiner essay-piece (e.g. which shoe) was found online; the general mechanism is documented as above.]*
- **Choosing Day.** Office-bearers are elected and sworn on the first Friday after 15 September; the Cordiners are traditionally **the first Incorporation to hold their choosing meeting** that morning (8.45 am in the Saloon of the Trades Hall) ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [Cordiners blog](https://cordiners.blogspot.com/)).
- **The three bells.** Unique in the Trades House, the Cordiners own three bells (gifted 1788 by Deacon John Jenkins; 1794; and 1815 by Col. Charles Walker to thrice-Deacon John Craig). At the Choosing Dinner the **Deacon rings the largest, the Collector the middle, the Goudie the smallest** — in that order — to silence the room before speeches ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The Goudie.** An office **peculiar to the Glasgow Cordiners** (probably from *gawd*/gold): the most junior of the Master Court, who wields the Goudie Box at entertainments collecting donations. Historically there were two — the Trade's Goudie (elected, next in line for Collector) and the Deacon's Goudie, whose legendary duty was "**to ensure that his Deacon returned home safely**" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The box.** The craft's money and charters lived in the crafts box — two locks, two keys, held by Box-maisters ("Kiemaisters," at times split "for the uter" and "for the Inner" box); a Keeper was made personally liable for losses from 1562/63. The surviving customs include an annual **Box-Opening** supper ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [Cordiners blog](https://cordiners.blogspot.com/)).
- **Chain, medal — and Dancing Medal.** The Deacon wears the chain at all formal occasions with one exception: **when dancing, a special Dancing Medal is worn instead**. Retiring Deacons and their Ladies receive replica medals ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Entry at the Near Hand and Far Hand.** Membership is for life: Near Hand for children and children-in-law of members, Far Hand for strangers; entry requires a **Burgess Ticket of Glasgow** and matriculation with the Trades House ([cordiners-glasgow.com — Join](https://www.cordiners-glasgow.com/jointhecordiners.htm)).
- **Civic duties (historical).** Members warded the city, mustered at **wappinshaws** (weapon-shows), and their Deacons lodged arms in the Almshouse — from 1649, each new deacon gave "ane sufficient sword and belt worth the sowme of ten merkis" ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Feasts.** "Upset" (Wpset) dinners marked a new master's entry; today the calendar runs Choosing Day, Choosing Dinner (February), Box-Opening, Old Deacons' Dinner (with ex-Deacon Col. Douglas Davies' silver cup, 1974), and exchanged dinners with the Worshipful Companies of Cordwainers and Pattenmakers in London and the Cordwainers of York ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Relics & insignia.** A silver-mounted buffalo-horn snuff mull (1886); two silver wine coolers (Lamond bequest, 1931); the Deacon's ancient wooden chair, cushioned in 1927 with leather **stamped with the cordiners' cutting knife**; Lord Forte's silver goblet (1970); the 1929 shield by G. Goldie Killin; and the Grand Hall frieze (1903, painted on silk by Belgian artists) in which the Cordiners' panel holds one of only four women depicted in the whole frieze ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).

## Sensory World

The craft splits into two smells: the tannery and the shop. At the barkers' and tanners' end — kept outside the town for the stench — raw hides steeped in pits of oak-bark liquor and lime; the barker's hands were stained brown to the wrist. At the souter's end, the shop smelled of new leather, beeswax, and pitch. *[extrapolated from general tanning/shoemaking practice; the Glasgow minutes record the trades' division — barkers, tanners, curriers, souters — but not their smells.]* The shoemaker sat at a low bench, leather apron over his knees, working entirely by hand and mostly seated — the trade of the thoughtful, the reader, the radical. His tools were "St Hugh's bones": the **half-moon cutting knife** that became the craft's heraldic badge, awls (Scots *elshin*), the **lapstone** on which leather was hammered supple over the thigh, wooden **lasts** (straight, not left-and-right, until c. 1850 — breaking in new shoes was penance), hog-**birse** bristles twisted onto waxed **lingel** thread, pincers, hammers, whangs of leather ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)). The sound of the craft is rhythmic and private: the tap of hammer on lapstone, the hiss of thread drawn through wax, the squeak of new leather — against the public clangour of processions: banners, bells, borrowed armour, and the three bells of the dinner table rung in descending size. The Almshouse painting of the Cordiners, now in the Craftsman's Gallery of the Trades Hall, is simply a still life of these tools ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).

## Values

1. **Dignity in humble work ("a Prince born").** The craft's whole mythology — Deloney's *Gentle Craft*, King Crispin, princes disguised as shoemakers — insists that the lowest-seeming trade carries hidden royalty. Glasgow lived it: crowning King Crispin in 1790, 1818, 1831, 1864, with a champion, pages and a Cossack ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)).
2. **Fairness inside the fold.** In 1657 the shoemakers complained the barkers and tanners were starving them of leather and outvoting them; the ruling forced the masters to be "half shoemakers and half Barkers and Tanners" forever after. In 1765 the Cordiners drove through the Trades House rule that **no person may hold two offices at once** ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
3. **The guarded gate.** Quality and livelihood were defended at the boundary: against "outentown Cordiners and unfreemen" (Deacon Archibald Scott's 1735 mission to Edinburgh), against merchants and saddlers selling boots without freedom (the 1757 House of Lords appeal, costs split with the Hammermen), against cobblers touching new leather ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
4. **Provision under pressure.** When the city needed feet shod, the Cordiners delivered — 160 pairs to the army camp in 1640, the fire-brigade's leather kept sound, even 6,000 pairs for an occupying Jacobite army — service rendered whoever wears the shoe ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
5. **Care for the fallen.** Widows, orphans, "decrepit tradesmen," dowries, funeral costs, a tramping allowance for those walking to find work — and its modern form, Body and Sole, putting sports shoes on children who own one pair ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [cordiners-glasgow.com](https://www.cordiners-glasgow.com/)).
6. **Memory as duty.** Minute books from 1550; a charter box with two locks; bells with donors' names engraved; a medal that recites three founding dates. The craft keeps its own record and mourns its losses (the mislaid box, the stolen chain) like deaths in the family ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
7. **Faith held quietly.** From wax fines for St Crispin's altar, to defending the Cathedral with their bodies in 1579, to the closing device "God is our Hope" — belief expressed through upkeep, not preaching ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).

## Shadow

Every Cordiner virtue has a curdled twin. **Dignity becomes vainglory**: the craft so loved its borrowed majesty that a man died of playing champion in Wallace's armour, and Galt's satire cuts both ways — a guild rehearsing coronations while its trade was dying. **The guarded gate becomes a toll-booth**: the same seal that protected quality prosecuted saddlers to the House of Lords and kept the poor cobbler forbidden by law from making a new shoe — protection of craft sliding into protection of price. **Fairness becomes faction**: the Bannatyne election war, the Finlay cursing scandal, Peter McGrigor's challenge over "country members" that gave one year four Deacons — a court of honour that could not stop litigating itself. **Memory becomes complacency**: clerks who recorded quarterly dues while omitting the plague, the great fires, even the founding of the Trades House itself — "the reader is completely at the mercy of the Clerk." And **care becomes charity-at-home**: the first duty to "our own decayed brethren" could shade into a closed benevolence — a fund for members while the unfree starved outside the gate. The craft's deepest failure mode is the same as its glory: believing the crown above the knife is about the crown ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf); [Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)).

## Rivals & Kin

- **The Hammermen (rival and ally).** February 1661: the Hammermen complained that cordiner Hugh Andersone was making **belts** — beltmakers being "appendices of the Hammermen." The House ruled for the Hammermen; Andersone made more belts anyway, was fined £10 and marched to prison by the House Officer. Yet by 1757 the two crafts jointly sued the merchant-backed saddlers all the way to the House of Lords, each paying a third of costs with the House ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The enemy within: souters vs barkers/tanners.** The Incorporation united the whole leather chain, and the two halves fought — the 1657 leather-supply dispute ended with enforced parity of masters. The Skinners (incorporated 1516, working skins and furs) sat adjacent in trade but held their own charter ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The merchants.** The standing war of 16th-century Glasgow — merchants monopolising the magistracy, crafts demanding office — was settled (barely) by the 1605 Letter of Guildry; it flared again whenever merchants sold boots without freedom of the craft ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **The cobblers.** Beneath the craft's dignity line: cobblers worked only "old leather," were rarely wealthy, and laws were regularly enacted forbidding them to make new shoes ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Kin across Scotland and England.** Edinburgh's Crispin committees lent Glasgow's journeymen the procession robes in 1755 (13s 6d, "the half of £1 7s"); robes and colours circulated among Edinburgh, Glasgow, Dundee and Kelso shoemakers ([Marwick thesis](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/4195/Thesis-Sandra-Marwick-redacted-version.pdf?sequence=6&isAllowed=y)). Today the Deacon exchanges dinners with the Worshipful Companies of **Cordwainers** and **Pattenmakers** in London and the **Cordwainers of York**; cordial links with the London **Curriers** lapsed after 1952 ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).
- **Precedence.** Third of the fourteen, behind the Hammermen and Tailors, ahead of the Maltmen — an order fixed in 1605 by criteria nobody recorded, and never changed since ([Short History, 2022](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/cordiner_s_final_small_book_~_small.pdf)).

## Lexicon

Terms attested in the Glasgow records and craft histories, plus general Scots shoemaker vocabulary a narrator could use (the last five are general Scots trade words, not from the Glasgow minutes specifically):

1. **cordiner** — Scots for shoemaker/leatherworker, from *cordouan*, the fine leather of Cordoba ("cordwain" in England).
2. **souter** — Scots for shoemaker (Burns's "Souter Johnnie"); used alongside cordiner.
3. **barker** — the tanner who "barks" hides in oak-bark liquor.
4. **currier** — the finisher who dresses and supples tanned leather.
5. **the Gentle Craft** — shoemaking's proud nickname, from Deloney's 1597 book of craft legends.
6. **St Hugh's bones** — the shoemaker's tools, from the legend that Sir Hugh's bones were made into them.
7. **"A shoemaker's son is a Prince born"** — the craft's defining proverb.
8. **King Crispin** — the post-Reformation, secularised St Crispin, crowned and processed by Glasgow shoemakers (1790, 1818, 1831, 1864).
9. **Dekin / Deacon** — elected head of the craft; "Kirk Maister & Visitour" in the years deacons were banned.
10. **Goudie** — the Glasgow Cordiners' unique junior office; keeper-of-gold, wielder of the Goudie Box, walker-home of Deacons.
11. **Box-maister / Kiemaister** — keeper of a key to the crafts box ("for the uter" and "for the Inner").
12. **wpset / upset** — the dinner (and fee) marking a new master's entry.
13. **prenteis silver** — apprentice entry money.
14. **leet** — the short-list of candidates for election.
15. **unfreeman / outentown** — the unlicensed interloper; the out-of-town trader the craft existed to repel.
16. **wappinshaw** — the burgh weapon-show at which craftsmen mustered with their own arms.
17. **essay** — the qualifying test-piece of workmanship.
18. **lingel** — the shoemaker's waxed thread *(general Scots)*.
19. **elshin** — the awl *(general Scots)*.
20. **lapstone, last, birse, whang** — the stone on the knee for beating leather; the wooden foot-form; the bristle tip of the thread; a thong or cut strip of leather *(general Scots)*.

## Question Seeds

Dostoyevsky-grade dilemmas rooted in the craft's actual lore. Each reveals a core value when chosen.

1. **Beaton's parchment.** Your charter — the thing that makes your work lawful and your children's future safe — was signed by an archbishop of a faith your whole city is about to overthrow, as he fled with the relics. When the new order asks you to burn everything the old order touched: do you surrender the parchment, or hide it and re-swear it under the new regime, as the Cordiners did in 1569? *Choosing to keep the parchment reveals* **Memory as duty** *(continuity above ideology); choosing to burn it reveals its shadow — conviction that cannot carry anything old.*
2. **The Champion's armour (1790).** The procession is tomorrow. You are chosen Champion of King Crispin, and Wallace's own armour can be hired from Dumbarton Castle — impossibly heavy, freezing, magnificent. The man before you died of wearing it. Do you put it on? *Yes reveals* **Dignity in humble work** *pushed to its glorious, fatal edge — the belief that the craft's honour is worth a life; no reveals* **Care for the fallen** *— the sense that no pageant outweighs a widow.*
3. **The Prince's six thousand pairs (1745).** An occupying army — enemies of your oath, plunderers of your city — demands six thousand pairs of shoes, and the magistrates beg you to comply so Glasgow is spared. Your needle will carry rebellion south on warm feet. Do you stitch? *Stitching reveals* **Provision under pressure** *(the craft shoes all feet; the city's survival is the higher loyalty); refusing reveals* **The guarded gate** *turned to principle — some customers forfeit the craft.*
4. **The starved bench (1657).** You are a shoemaker in an Incorporation ruled by the tanners who sell you leather — your own brethren, throttling your supply and outvoting your complaints. Do you split the craft you swore to, appealing over its head to the Trades House, or endure for unity's sake? *Appealing reveals* **Fairness inside the fold** *(justice within the family is worth a public wound); enduring reveals* **Memory as duty** *shading into its shadow — peace bought with silence.*
5. **The belt on the bench (1661).** A customer begs you for a belt — one strap of leather, work your hands can do in an hour. But belts belong to the Hammermen, and the House has already fined your brother Hugh and walked him to prison for this. Do you cut the leather? *Cutting reveals* **Dignity in humble work** *as defiance — the conviction that skill, not statute, owns the work; refusing reveals* **The guarded gate** *honoured even when the wall runs through your own shop.*
6. **The drunken recruit (1746).** The only man in Glasgow who marched away with the Prince was a shoemaker "who must have fled his country for debt, if he had not for treason." He was your journeyman. He owes the box money; his wife is on the pension roll. When the King's men come asking who he was — do you name him, or does the craft's memory close over him like wax? *Naming him reveals* **The guarded gate** *(the craft's standing with the city above one man); closing over him reveals* **Care for the fallen** *— the box exists precisely for the unworthy.*
