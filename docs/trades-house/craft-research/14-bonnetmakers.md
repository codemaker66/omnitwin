# Incorporation of Bonnetmakers & Dyers

> Craft #14 of the Trades House of Glasgow — the youngest of the fourteen, last in
> precedence, makers of the blue bonnet and keepers of true colour.
>
> Research note (2nd pass): the Incorporation's definitive in-house history —
> *History of the Incorporation of Bonnetmakers and Dyers of Glasgow 1597–1950*
> (6th edition, 1952, ed. ex-Deacon Matthew Lindsay, printed by Robert MacLehose
> & Co.) — **has now been fetched in full (63 pp.) and mined**. It supplies the
> motto, the arms, all three charters verbatim, and minute-book quotations from
> 1592 onward. Where this dossier still leans on general Scottish
> bonnetmaker-guild practice, the claim is tagged **[extrapolated]** or
> attributed to a named kin guild (Stewarton, Kilmarnock, Dundee, Edinburgh).

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Bonnetmakers made the one object by which a Scotsman was known anywhere on
earth — the broad blue bonnet, "even more a mark of Scottish identity than the
plaid" (T.C. Smout, quoted in [This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/)) —
and yet they were the smallest, youngest, and last-ranked of Glasgow's fourteen
crafts. That is the whole psychology: the people who crown the common man do not
themselves wear crowns. Their own charter says why they exist: because coarse
wool and "insufficient colours" meant that "our Sovereign Lord's lieges were
utterly defrauded and prejudiced" (Charter of 1597, in the 1952 History). They
knitted identity out of waste wool, felted it against Scottish rain, and dyed it
a blue that had to be true, because a colour that fades is a small public lie.
When their trade died under factory needles, they refused to die with it: they
took in the vat-stained dyers in 1760, policed false colour, and after 1846
turned the whole apparatus of monopoly into an engine of benevolence — from a
box so poor in 1842 it needed a £50 charity gift, to £81,394 and a thousand
members by 1950. Their two mottoes say it all: **"Give Glory to God"** and
**"Concordia Corroborat"** — harmony gives strength. Last in the procession,
stubbornly present for four centuries — the craft of made identity, true
colour, and quiet endurance.

## History

- **Before 1597 — the voluntary association.** A "voluntary Association" of
  craftsmen supervised the bonnetmakers trading in Glasgow before any charter
  existed. Its minute book is extant **from 17 August 1592** — five years before
  legal recognition — and already shows a Deacon, a Master Court, a Visitor and
  an Officer at work. The first recorded Deacon is **Thomas Nasmyt, "Dekin" in
  1593**; a minute of "ye xxij day of September the year of God 1593" names
  three Masters of Craft, two Visitors, and 13 other members — "against the
  names of 3 of them being the word 'deid'"
  ([1952 History](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/history_of_the_bonnetmakers_and_dyers_1597__~_1950.pdf), ch. I–II;
  [Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
- **29 October 1597 — the Seal of Cause.** The Town Council granted the charter
  "at Glasgw, the tuenty nyne day of October… ane thousand fyve hundreth four
  scoir sevintene yeiris," signed by Provost Mynto and bailies Hector Steuart
  and John Andersoun (full text, Appendix A of the 1952 History; parchment
  "16 inches or thereby square," still legible). It legalised inspection of
  "bonnets, wylicoats, woollen socks, hose and other such like wares," free or
  unfree, with a **twenty-shilling fine for "any faulty or insufficient work in
  the wool, colour, or tradesmanship,"** the money going "to the support of
  their poor decayed brethren." The deacon was to be elected "yearly upon the
  twenty-second day of September."
- **The price of privilege.** The charter bound every entrant to: burgess-ship;
  an essay (proof of "ability to turn out good work"); residence in the burgh;
  entry money graded by relation; and "the triple oath to the King, Town Council
  and Craft." Every freeman paid "one penny weekly… to be put into their common
  box… to the support of their own poor and decayed brethren" — the
  widow-and-orphan duty written into the founding bargain
  (1952 History, ch. I & Appendix A;
  [Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bonnetmakers-dyers/)).
- **1605 — the Letter of Guildry.** Glasgow's constitutional settlement created
  the Trades House under a Deacon Convener; the Bonnetmakers took their seat as
  the fourteenth craft. Precedence among the fourteen was finally "settled in
  1777 after litigation extending over several years!" (1952 History, ch. XII;
  [Wikipedia: Trades House of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trades_House_of_Glasgow)).
- **1608 — two apprentices.** "In the Incorporation of Bonnetmakers two
  apprentices were allowed to each master as early as the year 1608" — most
  Glasgow crafts allowed one until the mid-1700s. The same year entry money was
  fixed at 16 punds "with bankett and say drink" (1952 History, ch. I & III).
- **1640 — the Collector.** The office of Collector (treasurer, later chief
  recruiter) came into existence in the craft (1952 History, ch. I).
- **1650s–1662 — the Stewarton and Kilmarnock licences.** Rather than endless
  prosecution of country rivals, the craft licensed them: the Stewarton
  bonnetmakers under the Corsehill baron court bound themselves "to thankfullie
  pay & delyver to the sd trad of bonnet mackers the sowme of xx. merks money
  yeirlie" for liberty to "haunt the marcats of Glasgow"; a 1662 receipt at the
  "Croftis Hospitall" records the **Broad Penny** — 12 shillings each from
  Kilmarnock men like "David Currie, Bonnetmaker in Kilmarnock" (1952 History,
  ch. I, footnotes). The 1947 craft account dates the licences to 1662; the
  This Man Knits essay gives 1650 for the first Stewarton licence — both kept.
- **1686–1687 — "ye troubles."** The craft's own record states baldly: "No
  election these years, owing to 'ye troubles'" — the only gap in the deacon
  roll, in the convulsed final years of the Stuart crisis
  ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
- **1745 — the Jacobite requisition.** When the retreating rebel army occupied
  Glasgow, "the ragged army demanded 6,000 blue bonnets and 6,000 pairs of
  hose, which had to be supplied" (Ter-centenary address, 1897, in the 1952
  History, ch. IX).
- **29 September 1760 — the Dyers assumed.** On the petition of Deacon
  Alexander Ralph (lodged 25 January 1760, Trades' House permission 19 September
  1759), the Council — persuaded that "false dyes… had a visible tending to
  destroy the value and credite of several kinds of colloured goods both at
  domestic and foreign markets" — erected the combined **Incorporation of
  Bonnetmakers and Dyers**, empowered to inspect dyed goods and fine offenders
  five shillings sterling "for the use of the poor of their said trade." One
  serious defect: dyeing remained open to any burgess who chose not to enter
  (1952 History, ch. I & Appendix A).
- **5 January 1801 — the third charter.** After forty years of watching
  non-entered dyers trade freely, the Council rescinded the open clause: "no
  person shall have right or liberty to exercise the said trade or art of
  Dyeing or Bonnetmaking within the said city and liberties, without having
  first entered with the said Incorporation" — though the new royalty annexed
  under the Police Act of 1800 stayed exempt (1952 History, Appendix A).
- **1816 — "Spartan severity."** Minute of 16 February 1816: the Clerk had
  written to "two gentlemen who had begun the dyeing business desiring them to
  enter," with certification that if they had not entered by 1 March "they will
  be prohibited." They complied (1952 History, ch. I).
- **1846 — privileges swept away; 1842 — the nadir.** The Recissory Act of 1846
  abolished all exclusive trading rights. The craft was so poor that in 1842 it
  had "required a gift of £50 from the Trades' House." Had it wound up in 1846
  "no one would have received much." Instead, "some stout-hearted men… kept it
  going": stock £400 and 20 members in 1870; £2,000 and 170 members by 1881;
  **£81,394 and 999 members by 1950**. The named rescuers: Deacons Wm. F. Shaw
  (1881–2), Wm. Lambert Shaw (1891–2) and Dugald McKechnie (1908–9)
  (1952 History, ch. I & VI).
- **29 October 1897 — the Ter-centenary.** Dinner and dance in the old Windsor
  Hotel, "exactly 300 years from the granting of the first Charter": "sustained
  by a three-fold Charter… that night wore a crown of 300 years" (1952 History,
  ch. IX).
- **14 July 1924 — the arms registered.** Warned by the Lyon King of Arms in
  1923 that its Coat of Arms was unregistered, the Incorporation petitioned and
  registered the design with Lyon on 14 July 1924 (1952 History, ch. V).
- **1925 & 1947 — royal and martial honours.** H.R.H. the Duke of Gloucester
  admitted Honorary Member 2 May 1925; Field Marshal Viscount Montgomery of
  Alamein admitted 29 October 1947 — deliberately the 350th anniversary of the
  first charter, in the Saloon of the Trades' House (1952 History, ch. X).
- **1871 — the dyers' apotheosis.** Sir Archibald Orr Ewing MP — of the great
  Turkey-red dyeing dynasty of the Vale of Leven — served as Deacon (1871 and
  1873), emblem of the craft's second life as the guild of Glasgow's colour
  industry ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
  Five members have risen to Deacon-Convener of the Trades of Glasgow: William
  Brand (1807–8), William McLean (1827–8), Thomas Reid (1881–2), Andrew
  MacDonald (1907–8) and Daniel Duncan (1943–4) (1952 History, Appendix D).
- **2003–present.** Bylaws updated in 2003 opened membership to anyone
  "associated in some way with the textile and dying trades," including women
  ([Trades House Virtual Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/bonnetmakers.html)).
  The deacon roll shows its first woman, Shona Frame, in 2010, followed by
  Karen McKenna-Shand (2011), Janet Pierotti (2015), Mariella MacLeod (2017)
  and Dianne Chapman (2018); the craft is now "the youngest of the fourteen
  Crafts… but one of the largest numerically," distributing grants and student
  prizes across Scottish textile and design institutions
  ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bonnetmakers-dyers/)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **Helen Wylie's tongue (1598).** "On ye xij day of July 1598 yeirs, Helen
  Wylie is decernit in ane wrang of hir awn c'fessioun in bakbyting &
  sclandering of ye dekin & mrs of craft to ye majestrats of ye toun" — fined
  four pounds, and if she did the like again she was, by her own confession,
  "nevir to work walk or weif with bonatts or wyr airt of ye craft of
  bonatmaker craft in tyme c'ming." A woman working in the craft, threatened
  with perpetual expulsion — not for bad work, but for taking a complaint about
  the deacon *outside*, to the magistrates (1952 History, ch. II).
- **The capful of ale (1628).** "The haill craft all in ane voice hes decerneit
  & ordaneit Andro Jaix to pay to ye puir & weill of ye craft ye soume of
  fourtie schillings for ye casting of capfull of ale in Mitschellis Spens face
  & to mak ye said Mitschell houmadge at ye deikin & brethreins sicht." The
  history adds, deadpan: "And Andro Jaix became Deacon in 1645 and again in
  1652!!" — public penance, then the chair (1952 History, ch. II).
- **The wife's words (1664).** John Miller was held answerable when his spouse
  Kathrein Hernsoune "scandilizit" Deacon Jon Walker "in calling him ane dam
  boger and ane brought in beggar to byt the bairnes"; Miller bound himself to
  pay "ten schillings sterling for ilk fault toties quoties" if she did the
  like again — the deacon having "quatt & past the said wrong… upon her
  amendment" (1952 History, ch. II).
- **The bonnet kept on (1612).** William Watson was fined ten pounds Scots for
  "contempt and misbehaviour done by him to the Magistrates in uttring
  disdaynful speiches to theme, **with his bonet on his heid**" — the craft's
  own product as an instrument of insolence (1952 History, ch. II, from
  Macgeorge's *Old Glasgow*).
- **The Stewarton whisper (1617).** In St Enoch's kirkyard, Andrew Shiells,
  servant, was "fund in ane wrang for gevin counsall to James Robesoun… to gang
  to Stewartoun and yair he will be better interteneit & get ane gooder fie nor
  he gat in this toun" — fined 26s 8d plus 10s to the bailies, "dischairgit
  labour" until he submitted to the deacon's will. Telling a fellow worker
  wages were better elsewhere was an offence against the craft (1952 History,
  Appendix F).
- **The deacon-breaker's threat (1617).** John Smith, twice found "in ye wrang
  for blaspheming," told William Govan "that gif he were handillit as he wos he
  suld find ye way wtin ane yeir that yai suld not have ane dekyn and yairupone
  suld spend xx s." — twenty shillings of his own money to destroy the
  deaconship itself (1952 History, Appendix F).
- **The Kilmarnock contraband and the Broad Penny.** The severest punishment in
  the craft's code — "a fine of £50 Scots and permanent expulsion from the
  Incorporation — was reserved for those who bought bonnets in Kilmarnock and
  sold them in Glasgow" (craft history text indexed from
  [Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bonnetmakers-dyers/) via
  search snippet). Yet the same craft then *licensed* the rivals: Stewarton's
  guild paid twenty merks yearly, Kilmarnock men their twelve-shilling "Broad
  Penny," for an inferior stand on market day only — "if you cannot beat the
  country, tax its road" (1952 History, ch. I). The crime was never the country
  bonnet; it was passing one off under Glasgow's name.
- **The 6,000 bonnets of the '45.** Prince Charlie's retreating army exacted
  6,000 blue bonnets and 6,000 pairs of hose from Glasgow — the city's
  signature object requisitioned at sword-point by the rebellion whose own
  badge was a white cockade pinned to blue wool (1952 History, ch. IX;
  [Wikipedia: Blue bonnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_bonnet)).
- **The poor-money and the war (1809).** When the Trades' House voted £500 to
  raise a battalion against Napoleon, this Incorporation objected "that as the
  Trades' stock had been gathered for the maintenance of the poor, it could not
  be validly applied to any other purpose" — recommending members subscribe
  personally instead. The 1834 Municipal Corporations Commissioners later
  vindicated them (1952 History, ch. II).
- **The empty box (1882).** At the first recorded Deacon's Dinner, 24 January
  1882, "the Box of the Incorporation was opened by the Key Masters and was
  found to be empty!" Still empty in 1892. Only on 6 April 1905 were the
  Charter of 1597 and the minute books beginning 17 August 1592 ceremonially
  re-deposited — reviving "an old custom which had fallen into desuetude": the
  annual opening, examination and certification of the chest's contents, with
  the day's *Glasgow Herald* and *Daily Mail* laid in "of interest to future
  historians" (1952 History, ch. V & VIII).
- **The reversed medal (1877–78).** The Deacon's gold medal was inscribed "The
  Incorporation of Dyers and Bonnetmakers" — the order reversed, "through there
  being in the Master Court when the Medal was made a number of dyers actually
  engaged in the industry." By mid-century the craft was "known as 'Dyers'
  only," and when the full title was revived the Town Clerk had to be shown
  the original 1597 Charter before he would accept it (1952 History, ch. V & IX).
- **The two years with no deacon.** 1686–1687, "no election… owing to 'ye
  troubles'" — the craft simply held its breath through the storm around
  James VII and resumed electing as if nothing had happened
  ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
- **The dynasties.** Nasmyths from 1593 into the 1700s; Govanes, Spences and
  Burnsydes through the 1600s; then the Wyllie family holding the deaconship
  almost without interruption from 1704 to 1751
  ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
  A tiny craft kept alight by a handful of hearths.
- **The relieved Prince (1925).** The Duke of Gloucester, on his admission:
  "I am somewhat relieved to find that you do not expect me to knit or even
  dye one." He was presented with a Kilmarnock bonnet made by "one of the
  oldest of the Stewarton Weavers," and left quoting the Border song: "Ye hae
  no' seen the last o' my bonnet and me" — the hall answering with "Will ye
  no' come back again?" (1952 History, ch. X).
- **Montgomery's second beret (1947).** On the charter's 350th anniversary the
  Field Marshal received a bonnet from Late Deacon T. Muir Wilson, who — citing
  the eternal rivalry over whether it is a "Kilmarnock" or a "Stewarton"
  bonnet (this one was called Kilmarnock but made in Stewarton) — advised him
  to call it simply "a Scotch Bonnet," and hoped it would become "as well known
  a headgear of the Field Marshal as his famous beret" (1952 History, ch. X).
- **The Red Society's heirs (1939).** The Wrights handed over the Charter Box,
  Minute Book and Seals of the Red Society (founded Gorbals, 1733) to the
  Bonnetmakers and Dyers "as the natural inheritors of the Red Society" — a
  dead society's relics entrusted to the craft that would not die
  (1952 History, ch. V).
- **A Far Far-Hander (1946).** Joseph Michael Mahon, dyer, "who left the city a
  poor boy and achieved success" in New Zealand, returned and joined; presented
  with a Kilmarnock Bonnet, he vowed to "wear the Bonnet on every appropriate
  occasion" in his adopted land. Emigrant parties had once assembled in the
  Trades' House itself before sailing (1952 History, ch. XI).
- **The pensioner's offer.** An unmarried daughter of a once-prosperous
  craftsman, 79 and frail: "Please thank the Master Court for all they do for
  me, but if they know of any one needing help more than I do, I will willingly
  give up my pension and manage somehow." The offer was not accepted
  (1952 History, ch. VII).
- **The indigo mill that never was (1800).** With funds earning only 4%, the
  Deacon proposed buying ground "fit for grinding indigo and chipping wood, and
  a waulk mill for making cloth and blankets." The meeting approved; "no
  investment of that nature, however, seems ever to have been made"
  (1952 History, ch. VI).
- **The wet caps and the burned bonnets** *(kin lore — Stewarton/Ayrshire, not
  Glasgow record)*. Stewarton's inspectors ("sichters") fined makers who sold
  "wet caps (bonnets soaked in water to make them heavier)"; substandard goods
  "were reportedly forfeited and burned"
  ([This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/);
  [Future Museum](https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/life-work/key-industries/textiles/bonnet-making)).
  Glasgow's own 1617 statute is kin to it: bonnets hawked through the town by
  servants were "to be escheit" — confiscated (1952 History, Appendix F).
- **988 bonnets for privates, 71 for sergeants** *(kin lore — Stewarton)*. A
  1798 dispatch by John Wylie of Stewarton for a single military order shows
  the scale the trade reached supplying the army
  ([This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/)).
- **The Turkey-red deacon.** Sir Archibald Orr Ewing MP (Deacon 1871, 1873),
  whose Vale of Leven works dyed cotton a red that would not wash out, joined a
  line of Victorian notables in the chair — an ex-Lord Provost, an MP, knights
  and magnates — proof that the "smallest" craft became a magnet for Glasgow's
  textile aristocracy ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **No patron saint on record.** Chartered in 1597 — a generation after the
  Reformation — the Glasgow craft never had a pre-Reformation altar or saint,
  unlike older crafts. (Contrast the Edinburgh kin craft, whose bonnetmakers
  joined the Waulkers in 1520 and won their own seal of cause in 1530, in the
  age of altars — [Incorporated Trades of Edinburgh](https://edinburghtrades.co.uk/trades/bonnetmakers-and-dyers/).)
  But the craft's early meetings were held in kirkyards — St Thenew's
  (St Enoch's), the Laigh Kirk door, the Hie Kirk door — "and maybe sometimes
  in more convivial places!" (1952 History, ch. XII).
- **Motto — CONFIRMED: "Give Glory to God."** Listed as the craft's motto in
  the official order-of-precedence table of all fourteen Incorporations, and
  invoked by the editor of the 1952 History: "Our motto: 'Give Glory to God'"
  (1952 History, Editor's Note & ch. XII).
- **Second motto — "Concordia Corroborat" ("Harmony gives strength").** Carved
  on the mahogany Charter Chest (c. 1715) beneath two clasped hands, flanked by
  a carved Tam o' Shanter on the left panel and a pair of shears on the right;
  the 1930 cement cast of the Coat of Arms "carries the Craft's motto: 'Give
  Glory to God' and the words 'Concordia Corroborat'" (1952 History, ch. V).
- **Arms.** Registered with the Lyon King of Arms on **14 July 1924** (after
  Lyon's 1923 warning that the design was unrecorded). The old Kilmarnock
  bonnet "is emblazoned on our Coat of Arms"; in Deacon R. Allan Ogg's 1934
  words to new members: "The fine indigo symbolises the Dyers' bit and the
  dicing recalls a fearless and romantic figure in our Scottish History" — the
  dicing being, by legend, the "fesse-checky" of the Stewart arms rendered in
  military colours (1952 History, ch. V, XI & Appendix G). A formal blazon text
  is still not published in accessible sources; the elements above are as the
  craft itself describes them.
- **Symbolism.** Blue itself is the craft's true heraldry: "The product of the
  most famous dyers of antiquity — the Tyrian purple — was an emblem of royalty…
  The distinctive colour of the Scots, however, was not purple but blue — the
  colour of the blue blanket (the eldest national flag of Scotland), the colour
  of the blue bonnet" (Sir John Reid, 1925, in the 1952 History, ch. X). Among
  the Romans the felt cap was "the emblem of liberty"; James V minted the gold
  "bonnet piece" showing the King wearing a bonnet instead of a crown
  (1952 History, Appendix G). The bonnet was the commoner's crown — a flat,
  rain-proof halo of felted wool.

## Rituals & Customs

- **The bonnet at the oath (living ritual).** "Each new member of the
  Incorporation at his introduction, takes the Oath and is then presented with
  a blue bonnet, with a diced border in red and white and crowned with a bright
  red tuft or 'toorie'. Thereafter the new Craftsman wearing this headgear is
  introduced to the Master Court." The bonnets are bought from the **Bonnet
  Fund** founded by Deacon R. Allan Ogg in 1935 (£120 of railway stock, plus a
  £200 legacy after he died on war service) — pointedly, *Stewarton* bonnets
  for new Glasgow members (1952 History, ch. XI).
- **The Deacon's Dinner and the opening of the chest.** Annually the Deacon
  entertains the Master Court and ex-Deacons; "The Charter Box is opened and
  its contents are inspected" — charters, the 1592 minute book, and each year's
  deposited newspapers. Revived 6 April 1905 after long desuetude and "observed
  to the present day" (1952 History, ch. V & VIII).
- **The deacon's election.** Fixed by the 1597 charter "yearly upon the
  twenty-second day of September by the votes of the whole freemen of the
  craft." Early deacons evaded the one-year rule by "two or three leading
  Craftsmen holding office in alternate years for quite a long period." Half
  the Masters were nominated by the Deacon ("Deacon's Masters"), half elected
  by leets ("Trades Masters") — "so that the Deacon was always in power"
  (1952 History, ch. I & Appendix A).
- **Court secrecy.** "In caice ony speik any thing yat is spokin in court tyme
  to ony p'soun… ye speiker yrof saIl pay xl sh moy toties quoties" — forty
  shillings, every time, for revealing what was said in court (1952 History,
  ch. II).
- **The mark.** On entry each craftsman declared the indelible mark by which
  his bonnets could be known: "And the said John hes retanneit twa blakis in
  the ye lip of ye neck for his mark" (1952 History, ch. I).
- **Entry by service and proof.** Apprenticeship "usually five years… with an
  additional two years for meat and fee," no wages for the first five; the
  indenture written by the craft's Clerk (a Notary), booked in the craft's
  books and — for Guild-Brotherhood — in the Trades' House books. The unbooked
  apprentice "was neither recognised by the Craft nor the Guild." Entrants paid
  entry money, "the cost of a banquet and Clerk's and Officers' dues," and "an
  Essay drink" after passing the essay — the History notes drily that since
  there would otherwise be "neither free dinner nor drink," hungry and
  "drouthy" examiners had a temptation "not to be too severe on the new
  applicant!" (1952 History, ch. I & III).
- **Far Hand and Near Hand.** The two gates of entry, still in force: "Far
  Hand" for strangers, "Near Hand" for sons and sons-in-law of members at
  greatly reduced rates — originally, marrying a freeman's daughter meant
  entering *without payment* (1952 History, ch. III & Appendix E).
- **Inspection and censure.** Searching Masters seized and confiscated bad
  work; the Masters "fixed the mill days, regulated prices and wages of
  servants, fixed the weight of caps, and the Craftsman's marks… and regulated
  the division of wool cargoes." Against unfreemen they needed the Trades
  Bailie and the Red Coat Officer to "interpone authority" and poind goods
  (1952 History, ch. I).
- **Market order.** Strangers might not stand "betwixt a freeman's stand and
  the Cross in market time of day"; by minute of 19 May 1653, "everie man's
  stand sall be set as they come in friemen and the first frieman nixt the
  score" — placement by seniority of entry, 40s Scots for breach. At Fair time
  all monopoly was suspended (1952 History, ch. I).
- **Watch and ward.** Every bonnetmaker kept "in readiness, within his booth, a
  hag-butt, a jack and a steel bonnet"; the Deacon "sichted" his craft and
  handed up the Ticket of men ready for service, headed the craft "in all
  public wapinschaws and musterings," lodged a new musket in the Trades' Alms
  House on election, and convoyed Lords of Session, the Bishop, and
  distinguished visitors in and out of town (1952 History, ch. I).
- **The 'deid' bell.** Members "summoned by the 'deid' bell to attend funerals
  of deceased brethren" — with fines for absence (1952 History, ch. I).
- **The qualified roll.** Payment of quarter accounts (1d weekly / 1s quarterly)
  was the voting qualification. "Another disqualification for voting was the
  receipt of alms. Anyone who had received assistance from the Craft at any
  time had to repay all money received by him before his name was again
  entered on the qualified roll" (1952 History, ch. I).
- **The Officer.** The most recently admitted member served as Officer —
  warning members to meetings, carrying "the box and candles" — and "could not
  escape office until another member joined." He was provided with shoes,
  stockings, a wylie coat, and sometimes a hat and suit (1952 History, ch. I).
- **Pension visitation.** Every pensioner is visited yearly by two members of
  the Master Court; "No one in need and entitled to assistance is turned empty
  away." A recent Deacon's custom on welcoming new members: to express the hope
  "that neither they nor any of their dependents would ever receive one single
  penny from the Incorporation" — a picturesque blessing of prosperity
  (1952 History, ch. I & VII).
- **St. Mungo Goose and Turkey Club.** Since 1891 every member of the Master
  Court automatically joins this club; they dine before Christmas "and
  thereafter pass round 'the hat' — it should really be a bonnet!" — sending
  parcels to fifty or more old folks, most of them the craft's pensioners
  (1952 History, ch. VII).
- **The Stewarton pilgrimage.** Since the 1935 revival of the Stewarton Bonnet
  Guild Festival ("after a lapse of 345 years"), the Incorporation attends the
  crowning of the "Corsehill Queen" each June, the Deacon and friends "wearing
  their 'Tam o' Shanter' Bonnets," and the Deacon lays a wreath on Stewarton's
  War Memorial (1952 History, ch. XI).
- **Modern rites.** Annual prizes to textile and design students at up to eight
  institutions — pointedly including **Stewarton Academy**, in the old rival
  "Bonnet Toun" — plus Glasgow School of Art, Glasgow Kelvin College and the
  University of Strathclyde ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/bonnetmakers.html);
  [Trades House Virtual Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/bonnetmakers.html)).
- **Kin-guild court customs** *(Ayrshire/Dundee, for flavour, clearly not
  Glasgow record)*: "idlesetts" — enforced production pauses to stop price
  collapse, breached at "five to 15 shillings for each day's offence"; the
  Dundee guild's "Lockit Book containing its trade secrets and the names of its
  masters" ([This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/);
  [Future Museum](https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/life-work/key-industries/textiles/bonnet-making)).

## Sensory World

Skin wool — fleece pulled from slaughtered sheep, greasy with lanolin, bought
cheap so as not to compete with the clip of living flocks. A thick leather belt
at the waist with a fixed wooden needle, the **bonnet brod**, and two more
16-inch needles working the round — a man knitting standing up, walking,
gossiping, the bonnet growing like a coiled basket from headband to flat crown,
deliberately enormous, because everything shrinks in the waulk mill. "The
genuine bonnet of the Lowland Scots was of a broad round flat shape of a dark
blue colour and with a red tuft or 'toorie' at the top… without seam or lining
and it was exceedingly durable. No headgear ever invented could stand so much
rough usage. It is warm, fits snugly to the head and can be worn at a jaunty
angle suggestive of the independence of the Scot" (1952 History, Appendix G).
Then **dighting** — wetting and beating into shape (no servant might "dicht
bonatts" till three years apprenticed, 1593) — and the thunder of the
water-powered waulk mill on the appointed **mill days**: "Michaell Spence,
Gavan Nasmyt, Marioun Thomsoun… to pass to ye mylne wiklie of satday at thrie
hors eft none." The pile raised with teasels, then sheared dense and close.
And over everything, the dye-house reek: woad from Bordeaux and Dieppe, indigo
from the West Indies, both "ground down into powder, then steeped in urine
before the yarn was boiled in it"; black coaxed from oak galls over days;
scarlet from cochineal; and in the Victorian city the sulphurous majesty of
Turkey red. The craft's own historian reached for Macbeth to describe the
ancient dye-croft — "Double, double toil and trouble, / Fire burn and cauldron
bubble" — clouds of vapour, the stirred pot, the magic of heat: "secret recipes
and rules of thumb, handed down from generation to generation, merely guided
our ancestors as the walls of the cave guided the blinded Cyclops… in no other
branch of the textile industry are the penalties of error so severe." The
finished object: a broad, flat, twelve-inch circle of blue felt that sheds
Scottish rain off a working face — and the dyer "still performs the miracle of
giving life to dead things, and joy is the consequence."
(Sources: 1952 History, Appendix G & ch. II;
[Selvedge Magazine](https://www.selvedge.org/blogs/selvedge/the-scots-blue-bonnet-1);
[This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/);
[Future Museum](https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/life-work/key-industries/textiles/bonnet-making).)

## Values

1. **Identity-making.** They manufactured the badge of a whole people — the
   bonnet was "the sign by which a Scotsman was everywhere identified"
   ([Selvedge](https://www.selvedge.org/blogs/selvedge/the-scots-blue-bonnet-1));
   even the coinage showed a king crowned with one (the James V "bonnet piece,"
   1952 History, Appendix G). The craft's deepest pride is giving others
   something to *be*.
2. **Truth in colour.** The 1597 charter existed because false stuff and
   colour left the King's lieges "utterly defrauded and prejudiced"; the 1760
   merger existed to kill false dyes that were destroying "the value and
   credite" of Scottish goods "both at domestic and foreign markets"
   (1952 History, Appendix A). Integrity here is literally visible.
3. **Guarded quality.** Searching Masters, wool-thickness and weight standards,
   indelible maker's marks, confiscation, escheat of hawked bonnets — quality
   enforced as a public covenant, not a private preference (1952 History,
   ch. I & Appendix F).
4. **Mutual care.** The penny-a-week for "poor decayed brethren" was in the
   founding charter and outlived the trade itself: by 1950 the craft's whole
   revenue after expenses went to pensioners — "No one in need and entitled to
   assistance is turned empty away" (1952 History, Appendix A & ch. VII).
5. **Adaptive survival.** Youngest craft; absorbed the dyers (1760); closed the
   loophole (1801); nearly died (1842–46) and chose benevolence over
   dissolution; was rescued by named stout-hearted men; opened its doors by
   bylaw (2003). In Dr Harry Lumsden's words: "From the ashes of their effete
   trade organisations they re-erected on a surer foundation those houses of
   refuge — the fourteen Incorporations of Glasgow" (1952 History, ch. I).
   The craft treats extinction as a design problem.
6. **Democratic humility.** Their product crowned ploughmen, not princes; the
   makers were "humble folk who earned at most a modest living," and the craft
   stands last in every procession without complaint
   ([This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/)).
   Its anthem, quoted by its own historian: "He merits praise / Who will not to
   misfortune bow, / But cocks his bonnet on his brow / And fights and fights
   he kens na' how / Through long dark days" (1952 History, ch. I).
7. **Dynastic loyalty.** Nasmyths for a century, Wyllies for half of one — the
   flame kept by families who would not let a small craft gutter out
   ([Past Deacons list](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_bonnetmakers__~_2018.pdf)).
8. **Harmony as strength.** "Concordia Corroborat" — the clasped hands on the
   Charter Chest. The craft that fined ale-throwers and slanderers into
   "houmadge" believed concord was not a nicety but the survival strategy of
   the smallest guild (1952 History, ch. V).

## Shadow

Every virtue above has a curdled twin, and the record shows each one:

- **Identity-making → tribal gatekeeping.** The same craft that gave Scotland
  its badge reserved its harshest penalty for selling a Kilmarnock-made bonnet
  under Glasgow's name — identity policed until it becomes exclusion
  ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bonnetmakers-dyers/)).
- **Truth in colour → censoriousness.** "Drastic powers of censuring bad work
  and punishing offenders" is inquisition machinery; Glasgow escheated hawked
  bonnets, kin guilds burned substandard ones. Righteous inspection slides into
  zealotry against any work not one's own.
- **Harmony → enforced silence.** Forty shillings for repeating anything said
  in court; four pounds and threatened perpetual expulsion for Helen Wylie's
  "bakbyting" to the magistrates; a husband bonded for his wife's tongue.
  "Concordia" kept by fines is not concord — it is quiet (1952 History, ch. II).
- **Guarded quality → cartel.** The Masters "regulated prices and wages of
  servants"; a servant was fined for merely *telling* another that Stewarton
  paid better; kin courts enacted "idlesetts" to prop up prices. Quality
  language becomes cover for restraint of trade (1952 History, ch. I &
  Appendix F; [This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/)).
- **Mutual care → conditional care.** The widow-money flowed — but receiving
  alms struck you from the qualified roll until every penny was repaid: the
  craft's charity cost the poor their vote (1952 History, ch. I). Daughters in
  kin guilds could practise but were kept out of the Lockit Book (Dundee);
  Glasgow admitted no women members until 2003, a fact its own 1952 historian
  jokes about rather than questions (1952 History, ch. IV).
- **Adaptive survival → dilution.** A craft of bonnetmakers that contains no
  bonnetmakers: by the mid-1800s it was "known as 'Dyers' only," and its name
  had to be rescued by waving the original parchment at the Town Clerk;
  survival by absorbing dyers, then merchants, then anyone "associated in some
  way" with textiles risks becoming a dining club wearing a dead trade's name
  (1952 History, ch. IX).
- **Humility → invisibility.** Last in precedence can become last in ambition:
  the craft contributed just £25 10s 1d to the £7,927 cost of Robert Adam's
  Trades Hall — a 0.3% stake in the house it sat in — and until 1920 held only
  two of the House's 64 seats (1952 History, ch. XII).

## Rivals & Kin

- **Kilmarnock and Stewarton — the country rivals made tributary.**
  Unincorporated-town makers (Stewarton's guild under the Baron Court of
  Corsehill, Kilmarnock from 1646) undercut the Glasgow monopoly; Glasgow
  answered first with the expulsion rule, then made peace profitably — the
  Corsehill agreement bound Stewarton to "xx. merks money yeirlie," and the
  Broad Penny receipts of 1662 show Kilmarnock men paying 12 shillings each for
  an inferior market stand (1952 History, ch. I;
  [Future Museum](https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/life-work/key-industries/textiles/bonnet-making)).
  The rivalry endures as comedy: is it a "Kilmarnock Bonnet" or a "Stewarton
  Bonnet"? Official craft advice (1947): call it "a Scotch Bonnet"
  (1952 History, ch. X). And as friendship: the craft attends Stewarton's
  festival and endows prizes at Stewarton Academy.
- **The Dyers — the marriage.** "Intimately associated" always; legally nobody
  until assumed in a body in 1760 at their own petition — "the better class of
  Dyers" seeking order for a trade "rife" with malpractice. The union renamed
  the incorporation forever, and within a century the junior partner's name had
  swallowed the senior's (1952 History, ch. I & IX).
- **Within the Trades House.** Natural adjacency to the **Weavers** (cloth),
  **Tailors** (garments) and **Skinners** (wool and skins) in the textile
  chain; no recorded demarcation wars with them were found in either the web
  sources or the 1952 History — the Bonnetmakers' real demarcation fight was
  always with *unfree* makers outside the walls (the History's demarcation
  example is other crafts': "No Hammerman shall make the Woodwork of Clocks
  and no Wright shall make the Ironwork").
- **The Wrights — unexpected kin.** It was the Wrights' Master Court that in
  1939 handed the Bonnetmakers and Dyers the relics of the Gorbals Red Society
  "as the natural inheritors" (1952 History, ch. V).
- **Elder cousins.** Dundee's proud bonnetmaker guild (Seal of Cause 1496,
  dominated by the Hogg, Langlands and Gib families) and Edinburgh's
  (bonnetmakers under the Waulkers 1520, own seal 1530, dyers joined 1684,
  motto "We dye to live") — the Glasgow craft is the *youngest sibling* in a
  national family, just as it is the youngest craft in its own city
  ([This Man Knits](https://www.thismanknits.com/early-male-hand-knitters-scotland/);
  [Incorporated Trades of Edinburgh](https://edinburghtrades.co.uk/trades/bonnetmakers-and-dyers/)).
- **Merchants vs crafts.** The background feud of all fourteen crafts, settled
  by the 1605 Letter of Guildry that split civic power between Merchants House
  and Trades House. A ghost sibling haunts the story: the **Mariners and
  Fishers**, once a fifteenth incorporation, "went out of existence though it
  provided the first Deacon-Convener" (1952 History, ch. XII).

## Lexicon

Period and craft words a Scots narrator can wear naturally:

1. **bonnet brod** — the fixed wooden needle on the knitter's belt
2. **braid bonnet** — the broad flat blue bonnet, the commoner's crown
3. **toorie** — the bright red tuft crowning the bonnet
4. **dicing** — the red-and-white checked border; by legend the Stewart "fesse-checky"
5. **wylie coat** — flannel undervest or petticoat, part of the craft's charter goods
6. **litster** — Scots for dyer
7. **waulking** — felting/fulling cloth or bonnets by beating and shrinking
8. **dighting** — wetting and beating a bonnet into shape ("na servand agane saIl dicht bonatts…")
9. **mill days** — the rostered Saturdays each maker might "pass to ye mylne"
10. **sichting** — inspecting; the Deacon "sichted" both bonnets and armed men
11. **essay / essay drink** — the proof-piece a would-be master must make, and the drink he owed the court after passing
12. **upset** — the freedom-fine paid at entry ("for his vpsett the sowme of fyve pundis")
13. **Far Hand / Near Hand** — entry as a stranger vs as a member's son or son-in-law
14. **quarter accounts** — the 1d-a-week dues; paying them put you on the **qualified roll**
15. **Broad Penny** — the licence fee paid by Kilmarnock and Stewarton men for a market-day stand
16. **escheat** — confiscation; the fate of bonnets hawked through the town
17. **poind** — to seize goods for a fine, by the Trades Bailie's **Red Coat Officer**
18. **roup** — to auction; the forbidden private "rouping of servands"
19. **toties quoties** — "as often as it happens": the repeat-offence formula of the minutes
20. **in ane wrang** — the minute-book verdict: found in the wrong
21. **wapinschaw** — the armed muster the Deacon led, hag-butt, jack and steel bonnet
22. **deid bell** — the bell that summoned the craft to a brother's funeral
23. **Key Masters / Box Masters** — keepers of the (three-locked) chest's keys
24. **Deacon's Goudie** — the office-bearer attending the Deacon (still listed 1951–52)
25. **unfreeman** — a craftsman outside the incorporation, forbidden to trade in the burgh
26. **Seal of Cause** — the town council charter that makes a craft legal
27. **bonnet piece** — James V's gold coin: the King in a bonnet, not a crown
28. **bonnet laird** — a small landed proprietor who kept wearing the blue bonnet
29. **woad** — the blue dye plant, ground and steeped in urine before boiling
30. **skin wool** — wool from slaughtered sheep, the bonnetmaker's raw stuff
31. **Turkey red** — the fast, brilliant industrial red of the Victorian dye trade
32. **"ye troubles"** — the craft record's own phrase for 1686–87, when no deacon could be chosen
33. **idlesett / sichter / wet caps / Lockit Book** — kin-guild terms (Ayrshire/Dundee): enforced production pauses; the bonnet inspector; water-heavied bonnets; the locked register of masters and secrets

## Question Seeds

Dostoyevsky-grade dilemmas rooted in this craft's documented lore. Each states
the dilemma and the value the answer reveals.

1. **The Kilmarnock cart.** It is a hard winter. Across the moor, Kilmarnock
   bonnets sell for half your price and no buyer alive can tell them from
   yours. One cartload, sold quietly at the Glasgow market, feeds your children
   until spring. The penalty if discovered: fine and expulsion *forever* —
   civic death. Do you drive the cart? — Choosing to starve rather than sell
   reveals **loyalty to the brotherhood's name over personal survival**;
   choosing the cart reveals that you believe **care for your own outranks any
   covenant**.
2. **The Norway order.** A merchant orders a hundred dozen blue caps "for our
   friends in Norway," sailing in a month, never to return. Logwood dye costs a
   third of indigo and fades in a season — but the fading will happen on a
   fjord you will never see, on heads that will never know your name. Your
   craft exists, by charter, because false colour left the lieges "utterly
   defrauded and prejudiced." Do you dye true for strangers who cannot check? —
   Dyeing true reveals **truth in colour as an absolute** (integrity when
   unobserved); dyeing false reveals that quality, for you, was always only
   reputation management.
3. **Helen Wylie's choice.** You have seen the deacon and masters do a wrong in
   court, and the court's own law fines anyone forty shillings for repeating
   what was said there. Taking it to the magistrates is "bakbyting and
   sclandering" — Helen Wylie was fined four pounds and promised perpetual
   expulsion for it. Do you carry the truth outside the craft's walls, knowing
   the craft will call the carrying itself the crime? — Going outside reveals
   **justice above brotherhood**; staying silent reveals **the belief that a
   brotherhood that washes its linen in public is already dead**.
4. **Ye troubles.** 1686. Soldiers in the town, elections forbidden, no deacon
   in the chair. The craft's box holds the widow-money — behind three locks,
   held by separate Key Masters, precisely so no one man can open it. Old
   Marion Burnsyde, a member's widow, is starving *now*. Do you break the box's
   law to honour the box's purpose? — Opening it reveals **mutual care above
   institutional form**; keeping it sealed reveals **faith that the form itself
   is what protects the widows of the next century**.
5. **The price of alms.** The rule is written: receive the craft's help and
   your name comes off the qualified roll until every penny is repaid. Your
   family is hungry; your vote is the last thing that makes you a freeman and
   not a beggar. Do you take the pension and lose your voice, or keep your
   voice and let your children feel the winter? — Taking it reveals **bread
   before standing**; refusing reveals **that dignity of membership can matter
   more than the body it belongs to** — and forces the question of whether a
   charity that disenfranchises is charity at all.
6. **The Stewarton whisper.** You are a servant. Your friend, another man's
   servant, is ill-used and underpaid, and you know Stewarton would treat him
   better — Andrew Shiells was fined and barred from labour in 1617 for saying
   exactly that. Do you tell him? — Telling reveals **loyalty to the brother
   beside you over the craft above you**; keeping silent reveals **that you
   accept the craft's peace must be bought with other men's ignorance**.
7. **The husband's bond.** 1664. Your wife has called the deacon "a brought-in
   beggar" — and she may be right about him. The court will end the matter if
   you bind yourself to pay ten shillings for every future word she speaks
   against him. Do you sign, purchasing peace with a bond over another person's
   tongue? — Signing reveals **household survival above household truth**;
   refusing reveals **that you will not be made the instrument of silencing
   your own** — at the cost of the craft's mercy.
8. **The £500 battalion.** 1809. Napoleon threatens; the Trades' House votes
   money for a battalion; every other craft cheers. The stock in your box was
   gathered penny by penny "for the maintenance of the poor." Do you vote to
   spend the widows' money on the nation's war? — Refusing (as the craft did)
   reveals **the trust as sacred even against patriotism**; yielding reveals
   **that all covenants bend before the survival of the commonwealth that
   shelters them**.
9. **The dyers at the door.** 1760. The dyers — vat-stained, urine-reeking,
   legally nobody — ask to be taken into your ancient name. Absorbing them
   doubles your strength and lets you police every false vat in Glasgow; it
   also means the Incorporation of Bonnetmakers ends, and something hyphenated
   begins — and within a century the world will call you "the Dyers" and the
   Town Clerk will need to see your parchment before he believes your name.
   Do you dilute the name to enlarge the good? — Yes reveals **pragmatic
   stewardship over purity**; no reveals **the conviction that a name kept
   narrow is the only thing time cannot fade**.
10. **The nightcap heresy.** Bonnet demand is dying; gentlemen want tall hats,
    and your son says the future is striped nightcaps for sailors. Your father
    and his father made the braid blue bonnet — the crown of Scotland's common
    man. Do you retool the shop for nightcaps and live, or keep faith with the
    bonnet and gutter out with it? — Retooling reveals **adaptive survival**
    (the craft outlives the product); refusing reveals **identity held holier
    than existence**.
11. **The wet caps.** Your own journeyman — your dead brother's son — has been
    soaking bonnets to cheat the weight before the searchers' visit. The fine
    would break your house; silence would break the seal your craft puts on
    every honest ounce of wool. Do you report your own blood? — Reporting
    reveals **guarded quality as covenant, above kin**; shielding him reveals
    **dynastic loyalty as the true first law**, whatever the charter says.
12. **The empty box.** 1882. You are a Key Master. Before the assembled court,
    at the revived old ceremony, the Charter Chest is opened — and it is
    empty: the relics lost, mislaid, or quietly kept elsewhere on some earlier
    generation's watch. The minute-book is open before you. Do you record the
    emptiness, or find soft words to spare the dead and the craft's dignity? —
    Recording it (as the craft did: "was found to be empty!") reveals **the
    record above the reputation**; softening it reveals **piety toward the
    institution above the truth that institution exists to keep**.
