# Incorporation of Tailors

> Research dossier for the Trades House "sorting hat" quiz. Sources cited inline; anything not directly evidenced is tagged **[extrapolated]** (from general Scottish guild practice) or **[unverified]** (seen in one source excerpt only). Compiled 2026-08-05.

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Tailors are the craft of the measured body and the guarded door. Second in precedence among Glasgow's fourteen trades, they clothed every rank of the city "according to the fashion used at the time," and they proved a person's worth the hardest way there is: alone, in a locked room, with cloth, shears and needle, and an oath that no other hand helped. Their god was fit — of garment to body, of member to rule, of conduct to office — and their fury was reserved for whatever came in unmeasured: the unfreeman stitching in the Gorbals, the journeyman poaching a master's customers, the slander flung at a sitting Deacon. Yet the same hands that barred the door kept the most open purse in the city: in a single year they spent nearly four-fifths of their income on their poor, and their black velvet mortcloths covered the dead of thirty-two parishes. They dressed you for the kirk, the court, and the coffin. Precision, propriety, protection — and the price of all three was exclusion.

## History (founding, seal of cause date, key episodes)

- **October 1527** — the Tailors' earliest charter; the Trades House dates the formal incorporation to this year ([Trades House of Glasgow — Tailors](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-tailors/); Lumsden's charter table lists Tailors at "AD 1527, AD 1546, AD 1569" — [Lumsden 1928, Appendix A](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)). The Incorporation's own 1902 rule-book is more austere: "The period at which the TAILORS IN GLASGOW were Incorporated… prior to the middle of the Sixteenth century, are alike unknown" ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **3 February 1546** — Seal of Cause granted by the magistrates and council with the consent of the Archbishop of Glasgow (Glasgow was then a burgh of regality, so the bishop's assent, not the king's, made it law). It empowered the craft to choose a deacon annually, bind apprentices for four years, fine the disobedient eight shillings, and search out unlicensed practitioners; each entering apprentice paid half a merk "for the upkeep of divine service at the craft's altar of St. Anne," and every booth-holding master a penny weekly "for the repair and adornment of the altar" ([British History Online — Glasgow Charters, Historical Preface 1508–1546](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/xlviii-lxx)).
- **22 May 1546** — the Tailors reportedly banded with the only other crafts then recognised — Hammermen, Baxters and Cordiners — "to concur under penalty in all manner of actions, disorderis, or debates to the defence of their rights" **[unverified — reported in a search excerpt of the British History Online preface; passage not re-verified verbatim]**.
- **1556** — charter confirmed by Queen Mary; **11 May 1569** — constitution renewed by charter of the magistrates and council ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **1605** — the Letter of Guildry ends the merchants-vs-crafts war by arbitration and creates the Trades House; each craft keeps its own Master Court (Deacon, late Deacon, Collector, late Collector, six Masters, six Deacon's Masters) ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **1650** — the Tailors take six thirty-first parts of the Gorbals and Brigend lands (equal with the Maltmen; more than any other single craft), the foundation of a property empire — West Parson's Croft, North Parson's Croft, St Rollox Croft, Deanside Yard, Taylor Street, Clayknowes — that made them "one of the richest of the Glasgow Guilds" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)). The trades' ownership survives in the district name **Tradeston**.
- **28 February 1777** — the **Grand Decerniture** of the Court of Session fixes the order of precedence: "the Incorporation of Tailors is second, in point of rank or precedence, among the Incorporated Trades of the City," entitled to six members in the Trades House ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **1846** — the Burgh Reform Act ends exclusive trading privileges; the Incorporation continues as a charitable and civic body, today sending 6 representatives to the Trades House and keeping ties to Glasgow's garment industry ([Trades House — Tailors](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-tailors/)); it is a registered Scottish charity ([OSCR SC007955](https://www.oscr.org.uk/about-charities/search-the-register/charity-details?number=SC007955)).
- **14 May 1924** — arms matriculated with the Lord Lyon ([Trades House Digital Library — Incorporations](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).

## Legends & Episodes (the memorable stuff a storyteller would steal)

- **The locked room.** From 1725, every entrant's essay had to be sewn "in a locked room whereof the Essay-maker shall keep the key during the time of making thereof," after which the candidate swore to the Deacon "that what he has made, he has done himself without private assistance of any other" — because men had been admitted "on too slender proof of their skill" with "private help and assistance of other tradesmen" ([Lumsden 1928, Minute of 23 Nov 1725](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)). The craft literally rented an "Essay Room" — a line item in the 1749–50 accounts pays Deacon Luke a year's rent for it.
- **"The dearest hog in the Mercat."** In December 1705 Benjamin McIntyre mocked Deacon Robert Hogg's name to his face, adding that "if he had not used guile he had never been an Deacon." Banned from voting for seven years — pardoned only after he publicly craved the Deacon's pardon ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **"Saulless dogs and beggar bitches."** 1712: William Robin, in the Deacon's own house, cried "the devil take all these that had been chosen," repeated "Divel rive the present Deacon his womb," and called the Masters "saulless dogs and beggar bitches." Seven years' loss of vote and 48 hours in the Tolbooth — "a high price for losing one's temper" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The Jacobite slander that wasn't the crime.** 1716: David Biskett called Deacon Cornelius Luke "a Deacon illegally chosen and no Deacon" — and a great Jacobite besides. Witnesses proved "illegally chosen"; he lost his vote for five years. Lumsden notes drily that the accusation of illegal election mattered far more than any sympathy for the Old Pretender ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The election overturned by eleven unpaid pennies.** 1724: Gabriel Crosbie beat John Minto 73–71, but eleven of his voters owed quarter-dues to the Box. The Convener's Court unseated him — the only time in the century an elected Deacon of the Tailors was forced to stand down ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The Collector who ran.** July 1769: Collector John Wood's "affairs went into great disorder" and he absconded with the trade's money and papers. The Tailors got a warrant, seized their mortcloths from his house, and paid John Rowan, jailor of the Tolbooth, five shillings at a time to keep Wood locked up — ten entries' worth — plus lawyers as far as Edinburgh. The last entry is for Rowan's expenses "when John Wood was liberated." Not a penny was ever recovered ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The journeymen's newspaper war.** 4 March 1771: the journeymen walked out for one hour off before dark. The masters advertised in the *Glasgow Journal* for strike-breakers; directly beneath, the journeymen printed their own notice describing "the slavish hardship of working so many hours a day for the small wages" and begging the public to shun masters who imported country tailors "incapable of finishing work with any sort of neatness or sufficiency." Strikes followed in 1776 (13d to 15d a day — refused) and 1787 ("the shops of all the masters in Glasgow lie empty"), until magistrates' regulations fixed wages, hours and the "houses of call" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The slatehouses.** Unemployed journeymen chalked their names on the slate at two public houses — "houses of call" — which the masters denounced as "only jaunts of drunkenness and idleness" and sought to move to "the house of some honest sober freeman" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The hose test case.** 1758: Robert White sold inadequate hose, gave work out of his own house, and employed women — all against regulation. The Deacon-Convener backed the Tailors on quality (hose must be wool, "sufficient so as the same may be brought into character and reputation abroad") but rebuked them for "arresting his work": a craft may fine and debar, but not stop a man trading ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Darien and other burnt fingers.** 1696: £200 advanced to "the African Trade" — the Company of Scotland — "This proved an unprofitable investment." 1768: £50 for coal pits and a "fire machine" on the Gorbals muir (came to nothing); 1770: they refused, "by a great majority," to subscribe to the Monkland Canal — it would compete with their own coal. Ardrossan Canal shares eventually returned one-twentieth of par ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf); [Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Famine years.** 1752, 1756, 1765, 1799: the Tailors helped buy thousands of bolls of meal — 1756's alarm even priced Danzig oats — moving over the century "from pure self-interest to a sense of civil responsibility," buying at first for their own poor and at last for the whole city; in 1799 they subscribed £300 of their own on top of the House's £500 ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); the 1756 meal-and-grain distribution is the episode the Trades House still tells — [Trades House — Tailors](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-tailors/)).
- **The Sabbath post.** 1760: ordered to receive a mail packet on a Sunday, the Tailors declared "our detestation and abhorrence of the above profanation of the Lord's Day," lest "God may be provoked to pour out his righteous judgement upon the City" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Fighting the Police Bill.** 1783–1790: the Tailors hired lawyers, sent money to London, and printed resolutions in the *Glasgow Mercury* against rating powers — then campaigned against the Corn Bill, writing to Home Secretary Dundas and to the playwright-MP Richard Brinsley Sheridan ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Named men worth stealing:** John Gilchrist, tailor and Deacon-Convener of the Trades House 1689–90, whose 1700 mortification apprenticed a poor member's son every two years (Gilchrists, Bryces and Boyds preferred); Marjory Stephen, a Deacon's widow, who mortified 500 merks in 1717 on like terms; John Clark, thrice Deacon, Deacon-Convener 1729–30 ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto (heraldry, patron saint, motto with translation, symbolism)

- **Patron saint: St Anne**, mother of the Virgin — the 1546 Seal of Cause funds "divine service at the craft's altar of St. Anne," maintained by apprentices' half-merks and masters' weekly pennies ([British History Online](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/xlviii-lxx)). St Anne is the classic Scottish tailors' matron: Edinburgh's tailors kept an altar to her in St Giles' from 1473 ([Edinburgh Trades — Tailors](https://edinburghtrades.org/tailors/)). Symbolically apt: Anne, who clothed and raised Mary, stands for the domestic, protective making that tailoring is. (Continental tailors favoured St Homobonus of Cremona; there is no evidence Glasgow used him.)
- **Arms:** matriculated at the Court of the Lord Lyon on **14 May 1924** ([Trades House Digital Library — Incorporations](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html); [Lord Lyon Court Facebook post](https://www.facebook.com/LordLyonCourt/posts/in-may-1924-the-incorporation-of-tailors-in-glasgow-matriculated-its-arms/902977715189751/), inaccessible to fetch). **The exact 1924 blazon and motto could not be recovered from open web sources; the Lyon Register entry is needed.** The Tailors' shield appears in the 1888 stained glass of the 14 Incorporations on the Trades Hall stair and among the arms of the 14 in the Grand Hall dome ([Trades House Virtual Museum — Stained Glass Window](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/stained-glass-window.html); [Grand Hall](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/grand-hall.html)).
- **[extrapolated] Heraldic tradition of the trade:** British tailor guilds blazon a **royal tent (pavilion) between two parliament robes** — shelter and raiment, the two things sewn cloth gives mankind — as in the Merchant Taylors of London (motto *Concordia parvae res crescunt*, "in harmony small things grow"); Scottish tailor imagery leans on the **open shears**. Any quiz art should use tent + robes + shears with St Anne's colours rather than a fabricated blazon.

## Rituals & Customs (oaths, essays, feast days, precedence, relics, insignia)

- **The essay (masterpiece).** Pre-1713: "wide-kneed breeches and doublet"; updated because fashion had left them behind — essays were to be "work in current fashion as appointed by the Deacon and Masters." From 1723/25, fixed as: for a gentlemen's tailor, "one upper coat, one waistcoat and a pair of breeches according to fashion used at the time"; for a ladies' tailor "a gown and a petticoat"; "or a pair of stays if he is a staymaker" — made alone in the locked essay room, sworn to on oath ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)). By 1902 the upholsterers' essay was to "stuff and finish an Easy Chair in hair-cloth or morocco, or square-stuff a Footstool" ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **The oath (1757):** "I do solemnly swear that I shall obey, implement and fulfil the Acts, Rules and regulations… for the sustenance and maintenance of the poor thereof; that I shall not pack or deal with unfreemen… and that so oft as I shall violate any part of this my oath, shall forfeit and pay a new upset for the use of the poor of the Trade, so help me God" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)). Its descendant declaration ("true and faithful member… haill Acts, Rules and Regulations") was still subscribed in 1902.
- **The year's rhythm:** courts at **Hallowday, Candlemas, Beltane and Lammas**; the Lammas Court (August, in the evening) booked indentures, took quarter accounts (one shilling), and nominated Deacon, Collector and Masters; elections the first Friday after 15 September, 9 a.m., with the "Deacon's farewell" and alms to supplicants at each court ([Lumsden 1928, Appendix C](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **The Trades Box** — the treasury chest with **three keys** held by Deacon, Collector and Clerk; the Officer carried "the Box and candles" to every meeting and was clothed by the trade (accounts buy him shirts, German serge breeches, "Hat, Wig, Shoes & Stockings as usual") ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Precedence:** second of the fourteen, by the Grand Decerniture of 1777; six seats in the Trades House ([Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **The mortcloths.** The Incorporation rented funeral palls — including a child's mortcloth of velvet with a dyed fringe — to 32 parishes from Airdrie to Strathblane; 235 hires in 1731 alone. Income from grief, paid out again as pensions ([Lumsden 1928, Appendix E](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Entry money by blood and distance (1743):** stranger £12 sterling; booked man 102 pounds Scots; apprentice 42; freeman's son or son-in-law 21 — and from 1734 a son could enter "albeit he be neither a working tailor nor cappable of making an essay" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Deacon term limits (1719):** one year, then three years out — "none of the trade who shall be elected Deacon thereof shall continue in that office longer at one time then for the space of one year" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).

## Sensory World (materials, tools, sounds, smells, the physical texture of the work)

- **Materials in the records:** wool hose, yarn for shirts, German serge, holland (fine linen) for vests, velvet for mortcloths, whalebone-stiffened stays, gowns, petticoats, hoops (a "hoopmaker" paid dues), wide-kneed breeches and doublets of the old fashion; hair-cloth and morocco for the upholsterers ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **[extrapolated] The shop:** shears biting through wool on the cutting board; the hiss and hot-wool smell of the "goose" (the tailor's pressing iron); chalk lines and paper measures; needles, thimbles, bodkins; men sitting cross-legged on the board by the window for light — the standard furniture of an 18th-century tailor's shop, not specifically documented in the Glasgow minutes.
- **The hours tell the body's story:** documented working days of 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., later 6-to-8, with one hour for dinner — fifteen hours of close needlework, much of it by candlelight in a Scottish winter; the strikes were fought over a single evening hour ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Sounds of the institution:** the Officer's warning knock, the drummer at the rouping of an acre at Deanside, the clink of quarter-shillings into the three-locked Box, the reading of Acts "publically in the presence of the Trades Bailie" ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The frieze image:** in the Trades Hall Grand Hall, a 64-metre painted frieze (Belgian artists, c. 1903) romanticises all fourteen crafts at work — the Tailors among them — beneath a dome bearing the fourteen arms ([Virtual Museum — Frieze](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/frieze-in-the-grand-hall.html); [Grand Hall](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/grand-hall.html)).

## Values (5–7 core values, each with WHY rooted in evidence)

1. **Proof by your own hand.** The locked essay room, the key with the Essaymaster, the oath of unassisted work (1725) — worth is demonstrated alone or not at all ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
2. **Fit — to the time and to the body.** Essays had to be "according to the fashion used at the time," and were *changed* in 1713 when breeches and doublets went out; quality rulings demanded work "brought into character and reputation abroad" (1758). Excellence meant current, exact, fitting ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
3. **The guarded threshold.** The 1757 oath against "packing or dealing with unfreemen," prosecutions from the Gorbals to Port Glasgow, the pendicle agreements — the craft's livelihood was its boundary ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
4. **Care for the fallen.** In 1743–44 charity (£484) consumed most of a £616 income: monthly pensioners, supplicants at every court, "verbal orders" for anyone who came to beg, funeral money, mortifications to apprentice poor members' sons; famine meal in 1756 for the whole city's poor ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Trades House — Tailors](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-tailors/)).
5. **Lawful order above persons.** A Deacon unseated over eleven unpaid pennies (1724); term limits against "careermen" (1719); no man both Deacon and Collector (1710); a fine for merely *accusing* a Deacon of pocketing a fine (1717). Legitimacy of process outranked every personality ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
6. **Stewardship.** The three-keyed Box; accounts from 1713 "beautifully kept and very detailed"; Collectors bonded for £295 and fined 10% per day for money not banked; the property empire prudently feued — thrift as a moral practice ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
7. **Civic voice.** From Sabbath-post outrage (1760) through Police Bill and Corn Law campaigns to burgh-reform delegations (1784–88): the needle trade repeatedly hired lawyers, wrote to Parliament and printed its conscience in the newspapers ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).

## Shadow (the virtue curdled)

The Tailors' shadow is **the fitted world become a closed one**. The guarded threshold curdles into a shop shut against everyone unmeasured: women banned from learning the trade on pain of fine (1744) and confined to stitching stays and buttonholes; journeymen held to fifteen-hour days at fixed wages while masters fixed penalties for paying more; old soldiers ("King's Freemen") litigated all the way to the Court of Session for daring to cut cloth; and — the purest corruption of "proof by your own hand" — freemen's sons admitted "albeit he be neither a working tailor nor cappable of making an essay," while a skilled stranger paid £100. Reverence for office curdles into tyranny over speech: two days in the Tolbooth for an insult. Thrift curdles into avarice that votes against a canal because it competes with the craft's own coal. The value revealed in every case is the same one inverted: a craft that worshipped *fit* could not abide anything it had not measured — and mistook its tape for justice. ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Trades House — Tailors](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-tailors/))

## Rivals & Kin (clashes, marriages, demarcation)

- **Kin by bond:** Hammermen, Baxters and Cordiners — the 1546 mutual-defence band of the four earliest recognised crafts **[unverified excerpt]**; the **Maltmen**, equal 6/31 co-heirs of the Gorbals lands — the two richest crafts yoked in property ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Absorbed trades:** **staymakers and upholsterers** came "under the protection of the tailor's craft" — separate essays, lower journeymen's wages, same Box ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf); [Rights and Privileges, 1902](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/rights_and_privelages_of_the_incorporation_of_tailors_1902___001.pdf)).
- **Vassals:** the **Tailors of Port Glasgow** (1731) and of the **Gorbals** (1740) became *pendicles* — Glasgow's Deacon picked their oversman from a leet of three and they could pass no act without Glasgow's leave ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Enemies within:** their own **journeymen**, from "sinistrus ways and contrivances" (1710) to the organised Society of Journeymen Tailors fighting through newspapers and courts ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Enemies at the edge:** unfreemen who took measurements in the city and sewed beyond the boundary; **mantuamakers** — the women's dressmakers — tolerated for dues (Mrs Rebecca Wallace paid £6 Scots a year, 1736–62; never more than six women, none after 1762) but forbidden to train other women ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **The old grand rivalry:** merchants vs craftsmen city-wide, settled by the 1605 Letter of Guildry — the tailors' cloth came *through* merchant hands, and no craftsman could be Lord Provost ([Lumsden 1928](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/tailors_in_18c_~_by_lumsden.pdf)).
- **Cousins elsewhere:** Edinburgh's Incorporation of Tailors (St Ann's altar in St Giles', 1473; their 1621 Tailors' Hall in the Cowgate hosted the drafting of the National Covenant in 1638 — [Gazetteer for Scotland](https://www.scottish-places.info/features/featurefirst7777.html)); the Tailors of Rutherglen (1657), the last surviving craft of that burgh ([tailorsofrutherglen.co.uk](https://tailorsofrutherglen.co.uk/)).

## Lexicon (period/craft words a Scots narrator could use)

1. **Seal of Cause** — the charter that made a craft a legal incorporation.
2. **The Box** — the three-keyed chest holding the trade's money; "owing to the Box" could cost you your vote.
3. **Essay / essay-piece** — the masterpiece proving skill; **Essaymaster** — its invigilator; **the Essay Room** — the locked chamber.
4. **Freedom fine** — entry money; **new upset** — the fresh entry-fee levied as penalty for oath-breaking.
5. **Quarter accounts** — the shilling dues paid at Lammas Court.
6. **Booked** — enrolled in the craft's books (apprentice or journeyman); **indenture** — the notarised bond of service.
7. **Unfreeman** — one practising the trade without membership; **packing and peeling** — partnering or trading with unfreemen (a fineable sin).
8. **At the far hand** — entering as a total stranger, at the dearest rate.
9. **Pendicle** — a subordinate craft body (Port Glasgow, Gorbals) under Glasgow's deacon.
10. **Mortcloth** — the velvet funeral pall rented over coffins; a steady income of the craft.
11. **Mortification** — a charitable endowment (Gilchrist's, Stephen's) for apprenticing poor members' sons.
12. **Lammas Court / Hallowday / Candlemas / Beltane** — the quarter-day courts of the tailors' year.
13. **Deacon; late Deacon; Collector; Deacon's Masters** — the officers of the Master Court; **Deacon's farewell** — the outgoing court with alms.
14. **Guildbrother; burgess ticket; oath *de fideli*** — the citizenship ladder every entrant climbed.
15. **Mantuamaker** — a woman dressmaker outside full membership; **staymaker**, **hoopmaker** — the corset and hoop trades.
16. **House of call / slatehouse** — the pub where out-of-work journeymen chalked their names on the slate.
17. **Haill** — whole ("the haill Acts, Rules and Regulations"); **anent** — concerning ("Act anent proof of work").
18. **Wide-kneed breeches and doublet** — the antique essay, retired 1713 when fashion moved on.
19. **Relict** — widow (the accounts are full of relicts on the pension roll).
20. **Whip the cat** — Scots for the travelling tailor sewing in farm kitchens for bed and board **[extrapolated — general Scots usage, not in the Glasgow minutes]**.

## Question Seeds (Dostoyevsky-grade dilemmas rooted in this craft's lore)

1. **The locked room, twice.** Years ago a friend passed work through the essay-room window for you; you swore the oath anyway, and the door of your life opened. Tonight, as Essaymaster, you hold the key and hear the same soft knock for another man. To expose him is to confess yourself; to let it pass is to make the oath cloth-cheap. *Choosing exposure reveals* **Proof by your own hand** *(integrity worth self-destruction); choosing silence reveals* **Care for the fallen** *curdling toward complicity.*
2. **Eleven pennies.** You have won the deaconship 73–71 — and learn that eleven of your voters owed a quarter's pennies to the poor's Box, as Crosbie's did in 1724. No one else has counted. Do you stand down over farthings, or serve, and serve well? *Standing down reveals* **Lawful order above persons**; *staying reveals belief that* **works outweigh forms** *(and the first thread of the Shadow).*
3. **The hour between seven and eight.** It is March 1771. The journeymen ask for one evening hour; you remember your own fifteen-hour days, your ruined eyes. The masters offer you country hands "incapable of finishing work with any sort of neatness" to break the strike. Bad cloth or broken men — which will you sew your name into? *Yielding the hour reveals* **Care for the fallen** *over profit; breaking the strike reveals* **Fit and reputation** *held above persons.*
4. **The jailor's five shillings.** John Wood ran with the Box's money; the trade pays the Tolbooth jailor week after week to keep him, and lawyers besides, while his family falls onto the very poor-roll he robbed. Every shilling spent on vengeance is a shilling off a widow's pension. Keep paying? *Pressing on reveals* **Stewardship as justice** *(the poor's money is sacred); relenting reveals* **Mercy** *— and an acceptance that some debts are dressed, not repaid.*
5. **The son who cannot sew.** The Act of 1734 lets your boy enter "albeit he be neither a working tailor nor cappable of making an essay," while a skilled stranger pays £100 at the far hand. Your son's name would keep the pension rights in your blood. Enrol him? *Refusing reveals* **Proof by your own hand** *as the craft's true blood; enrolling reveals* **Kin-loyalty and the guarded threshold** *— the craft as inheritance, its Shadow smiling.*
6. **The child's mortcloth.** You are Collector. Tonight Sarah Paterson brings back the child's mortcloth, its fringe new-dyed, and the fee goes into the Box beside the famine-meal accounts. A supplicant at the door cannot pay the hire for her own bairn's burial. The cloth is the trade's; the grief is hers. Waive the fee unminuted, or minute it and let the Court decide come Candlemas? *Waiving reveals* **Care for the fallen** *above the ledger; minuting reveals* **Stewardship and lawful order** *— compassion that must still balance at year's end.*
