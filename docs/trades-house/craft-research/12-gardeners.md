# Incorporation of Gardeners

> Craft #12 of the 14 Incorporations of the Trades House of Glasgow. Researched 2026-08-05, enriched in a second pass the same day. Every claim is cited inline; anything inferred from general Scottish/Glasgow guild practice rather than Gardeners-specific evidence is tagged **[extrapolated]**.
>
> **Evidence note, read first — this dossier got much stronger on the second pass.** The craft's own 325-page history — *The History of the Incorporation of Gardeners of Glasgow: From 18th November 1626 to 1st September 1903* (J. Smith and Son, 1903) — is **still not digitised as a whole**. But three things broke open on the second pass:
> 1. **Roughly fifty pages of that 1903 book are online after all**, as scans on the Trades House Digital Library. The "members" and "genealogy" downloads are photographed book pages — their running heads read `LIST OF MEMBERS 179` … `226 THE INCORPORATION OF GARDENERS` and `PRESENT MEMBERS, WITH CRAFT GENEALOGY 243`. They are pp. ~178–227 and ~243+ of the 1903 volume. Everything quoted below from "the 1903 roll" is **verbatim primary text from the craft's own book**.
> 2. **The Lord Lyon's PDF that defeated the first pass has been read.** `pdftotext` produced garbage; `pypdf` extracted it cleanly. It contains the **official blazon and the 1924 grant date** — Gap #2 is closed.
> 3. **Crawfurd's 1858 *Sketch of the Rise and Progress of the Trades' House of Glasgow*** (the House's own Clerk, in office 1831–1875) and the **transcribed *Records of the Trades House of Glasgow, A.D. 1605–1678*** are both fully digitised on Archive.org and were mined line by line. Between them they **solve the 1789 land exclusion**, **contradict the craft's own plague legend**, and supply a dozen new episodes.
>

Glasgow City Archives at the Mitchell hold the manuscript records of all fourteen incorporations ([Glasgow Life — City Archives](https://www.glasgowlife.org.uk/libraries/city-archives/archive-collections)). **Photographing `338.6 GAR 1903` remains the single highest-value action available.**

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Gardeners are the only craft in Glasgow whose coat of arms depicts the moment everything went wrong. Adam on the right, Eve on the left, the tree between them, the serpent in its branches — and over that scene, on a blue chief, the sun, the moon, and a spade crossed with a rake. London's gardeners chose the same Eden and read it as a sentence: *In the sweat of thy brows shalt thow eate thy bread* ([Worshipful Company of Gardeners](https://www.gardenerscompany.org.uk/company/history/)). Glasgow's gardeners looked at exactly the same picture and read it as a *claim*: **Gardening — the First of Arts** ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)). That inversion is the whole craft. And the claim was made from the bottom of the room: twelfth of fourteen by decree of the Court of Session; two assistants where the Hammermen had five; £50 18s 8d toward the Trades Hall where the Weavers put up £834; left out of the land settlement that enriched eleven other crafts; a founding charter destroyed in a plague year; yards rented from the Weavers. Glasgow's own constitution, the Letter of Guildry of 1605, listed their entire product — *"pears, apples, corn... onions, kail"* — among the "small things" it was beneath a guild brother's honour to sell ([Crawfurd, 1858, appendix](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). They answered that with a motto saying they came before everybody. They are the craft that works with time instead of against it: you cannot hurry a tree, you cannot argue with a season, and the only thing you truly own is the black earth you made out of other people's muck. Sun and moon on the shield, because the work is governed by things no deacon can vote on.

## History

- **c.1605 — the lost charter.** "About the year 1605, on an application from a number of 'practical gardeners', the Craft obtained a charter from the Burgh of Glasgow, conferring the usual rights and privileges," granted by the Lord Provost, Magistrates and Town Council ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/); [Incorporation of Gardeners — History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)). Note the phrase the craft chose to preserve about itself: **practical** gardeners. Not gentlemen with a garden — men who dug.
- **6 February 1605 — the constitution that despised their goods.** The Letter of Guildry, made "At the burgh and city of Glasgow, the 6th February, 1605 years," created the Trades House. Its **article 23** forbids any new gild brother "to tapp tar, oil, butter, or to tapp eggs, green herring, pears, apples, corn, candle, onions, **kail**, straw, bread... milk, and such like small things, **which is not agreeable to the honour of the calling of a gild brother**" ([Letter of Guildry, printed in Crawfurd, 1858, appendix](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). The founding document of the House the Gardeners belong to declares the retail of a gardener's produce dishonourable. *This is the taproot of the motto.*
- **c.1600 — a food trade, incorporated with the food trades.** "The increasing importance of food and drink products is reflected in the incorporation of trades such as the Coopers, the Fleshers and, around 1600, the Maltmen... and the Gardeners" ([TheGlasgowStory: Industry and Technology](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/story/?id=TGSBE03)); "Fresh vegetables were supplied from nearby nurseries by 'gardeners'" ([TheGlasgowStory: Shopping](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/story/?id=TGSBA02)).
- **13 November 1616 — the roll opens.** The craft's own membership list is headed "**LIST OF THE MEMBERS OF THE INCORPORATION FROM 13TH NOVEMBER 1616 TO 18TH SEPTEMBER 1903**," and the first name on it is **Gawain Wilsoun** ([1903 history, p.179, scanned](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). The roll therefore begins ten years *before* the date in the book's title.
- **18 November 1626 — where the minutes begin.** The book is titled *From 18th November 1626*; the clerks' list shows why. The first clerk, "**Johne Blair, Notar**," took office **18th Nov 1626** ([Incorporation of Gardeners ~ Clerks](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_~_clerks_1.docx)). The first deacon on the craft's own list, **John Govane**, is elected the same year ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). **1626 is the day the craft started keeping a notary — not the day it started.**
- **22 August 1632 — the visitor arms the House.** "Adame Tod deacone of ye tailzo's and **Dauid Jack visito' of ye gairdneris** gav in y' muskatts bandaleiris and stafts qlkis war hung vp with ye rest" ([*Records of the Trades House of Glasgow 1605–1678*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). Every new head of a craft gave a musket to the Trades Hospital armoury. David Jack was deacon of the Gardeners in 1630–32, 1634, 1636 and 1641 ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)).
- **14 May 1634 — the first pensioned gardener.** "The said day ordanis ye collecto' to pay to **Androw Harvie gardner** quarterlie fyve merks money and ane laid of coillis" ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). Five merks a quarter and a load of coals — the House's charity to a broken gardener, 392 years ago, and the direct ancestor of the pensions the Incorporation still pays.
- **1638–1656 — the craft in the accounts, always near the foot.** In the House's stent rolls the "Gairdneris" are assessed **iiij li** where others pay eight and twelve; toward the new craft school (1649) "the deikine and craft of hemermen" gave £13 6s 8d, "the deikin of talzo's" £22 4s 6d, and "**be the gairdners fourtie schilling**." The quarter accounts repeatedly list "ye haill deikines, **litsters and gardners**" — the Gardeners and the Dyers booked *after* and *apart from* the thirteen deacons ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). **Their later exclusions are prefigured here, in the bookkeeping.**
- **28 September 1648 — a gardener in the room.** The House convenes with the deacons of eight crafts, "Alex' Parker for the litsters and **Johne Woddrow for the gairdneris**" ([*Records*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)) — again, named separately from the deacons.
- **1646 or 1649 — the charter is destroyed. Two irreconcilable accounts.** *(See Legends.)* The craft says 1646, plague, the Deacon sent to the "Foull Moor," charter lost among his possessions ([Incorporation of Gardeners — History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)). The Trades House's own Clerk, writing in 1858, says: "The deacon of this incorporation **died, in 1649**, of the plague, then prevalent in Glasgow, and the original charter incorporating this trade, **being in the house at the time of his death, was burned along with all his furniture and papers**" ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Scotland's final plague outbreak ran 1644–1649, with evidence for plague in Glasgow from late 1646 into 1647 ([PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0306432)).
- **9 March 1650 — the day the Gardeners were written out.** A contract between the town council and "the deacon convener and **twelve** deacons of crafts" divided the Trades House's quarter of the Gorbals lands into **thirty-one parts**: the hospital 8, tailors 6, hammermen 2, cordiners 2, skinners 2, weavers 1, wrights 1, fleshers 1, coopers 1, bakers ½, masons ½ — with the maltmen's 6 appearing in the 1792 implementing disposition ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). **The Gardeners, Barbers and Dyers are in neither list.** The 1789–92 division that the first pass could not explain simply *executed the 1650 shares*. **The exclusion is 139 years older than the event that made it visible.** **[extrapolated — that the 1789 exclusion inherits from the 1650 contract is my inference; Crawfurd prints both instruments but never says so.]**
- **10 September 1649 — assault on the visitor.** *(See Legends.)*
- **22 November 1690 — the Seal of Cause, exactly dated.** "The magistrates and town council, **on 22d November, 1690**, granted a seal of cause to the incorporation, **referring to the original one**" ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)) — confirming, and precisely dating, the craft's own account that the Seal of Cause "with the rules and byelaws, remains the basis of the Constitution of the Incorporation," conferring power "to prevent persons, other than members of the Incorporation, from carrying on their trade within the Burgh" ([Incorporation of Gardeners — History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)). **Still the constitutional foundation, 336 years on.**
- **22 January 1745 — the craft wins the right to choose its own head.** The magistrates "authorized them to **elect their deacon without a leet of two**" ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). The craft's own deacons list carries a note against the year **1745** (William Smith): "**First Electyion of Deacon by Craft**" ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). Two independent sources, one date, one fact: until 1745 the Gardeners submitted two names and the Town Council picked.
- **1771–1777 — the Grand Decerniture, and the Gardeners are now *documented* among the ten.** Crawfurd names the pre-1777 representation: hammermen, tailors, cordiners, maltmen five assistants each; weavers three; "the bakers, skinners, wrights, coopers, fleshers, masons, **gardeners**, and barbers, two; and the dyers one" — the ten smaller crafts, who "made an effort... to obtain an equal representation." The Court of Session's decree of **28 February 1777** found "that the rank and precedency of the several trades and incorporations in the Trades' House is, and ought to be as follows... Hammermen, tailors, cordiners, maltmen, weavers, baxters, skinners, wrights, coopers, fleshers, masons, **gardeners**, barbers, bonnetmakers," fixing the House at **fifty-four** members ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt); corroborated in [Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). **This closes the first pass's Gap #8** — the Gardeners' membership of the ten claimants is no longer arithmetic, it is on the page. They sued for equality and were given a number instead: **twelfth**.
- **1789–90 — a Gardener chairs the House that divides the land.** "**John M'Aslan, Gardener, 1789-1790**" is Deacon Convener of the Trades House ([Crawfurd's list of conveners, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)) — in the very years the House drew lot third of the Gorbals lands (decree arbitral 15 Oct / 11 Nov 1789) and prepared the disposition of **78 acres, 3 roods, 14 falls** to the eleven crafts **his own was not among**. The 1903 history names him among only four Gardeners ever to hold the office in a century: "**John M'Auslin in 1789-90, Robert Austin in 1805-6, Andrew Fowler in 1842-43, and Matthew White in 1901-2**" ([1903 history, p.178](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)).
- **1791–1802 — the Trades Hall, and what the Gardeners paid for it.** Foundation stone **9 September 1791**; Robert Adam's design; first meeting of the House in the new hall **17 September 1794**; a hall "of seventy feet by thirty-five, and twenty-three feet in height to the ceiling, and **thirty-one feet to the top of the dome**." Total charges 1791–1802: £7,927 18s 6d. Contributions: the House £4,700 9s 7d, the Weavers £834 6s 4d, the Maltmen £611 4s 2d, the Tailors £509 8s 8d… and **the Gardeners, £50 18s 8d** — second smallest of the fourteen, ahead only of the Dyers' £25 19s 1d ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). *The Gardeners bought roughly two-thirds of one percent of the building whose stair window still carries their arms.*
- **1798 — a gardener among the almoners.** In November 1798 the House named a committee "to look after the Boarded in the Alms House and put them in order" — first named: "Messrs. **Robert Austin, Gardener**" ([Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). Crawfurd confirms a Robert Austin, Gardener, was Deacon Convener 1805–6; the same name founded the Austin & McAslan nursery partnership in 1803 ([Bygone Bungo](https://bygone.bungoblog.com/wp/horticultural-strathbungo-and-the-austin-mcaslan-nursery/)). *Same man is very likely; still not proven.*
- **1833 — the country craft.** Bye-law 8 of the House, under the Burgh Reform Act: "**Members of the incorporations of maltmen and gardeners shall continue to be eligible as representatives of their respective incorporations, although not resident in, or in the occupancy of a place of business within that district**" — and this is expressly "**the ancient law of the House**," older than the 1833 bye-law that granted the same to dyers and bonnetmakers ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Only two crafts in Glasgow were structurally assumed to live outside the town. The Gardeners were one. **Their ground was always beyond the wall.**
- **14 May 1846 — the end of monopoly.** The Burgh Trading Act enacted "that all such exclusive privileges and rights shall cease" ([quoted in Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). The Gardeners' privileges "were enjoyed down to the passing of the Act in 1846" ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)). They did not wind up: "the yearly income is expended mainly in **pensions and precepts**" ([Incorporation of Gardeners — History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)).
- **25 March 1924 — the arms are matriculated.** Lyon Register **volume 26, folio 29** ([Court of the Lord Lyon, *Practical Heraldry for the Self-Isolator, Week 6a — The Incorporated Trades*](http://courtofthelordlyon.scot/IsolationHeraldryWeek6A.pdf)). *(See Patron, Arms & Motto — Gap #2 of the first pass is closed.)*
- **16 December 1960** — registered as a Scottish charity, SC004606 ([OSCR](https://www.oscr.org.uk/about-charities/search-the-register/charity-details?number=SC004606)).
- **1979 →** the travelling scholarships leave a paper trail: student reports on Italian gardens (1979), soilless culture at Wageningen (1980), the Loire nursery-stock industry (1982), the Westland glasshouses (1986), interior landscaping in New York (1986), the heather industry (1988), horticulture in modern Czechoslovakia (1990), Madeira (1992), clubroot disease and *Plasmodiophora brassicae* (2004, 2005), RHS Wisley (2012) — about forty of them, shelved at `338.6 GAR R<year>` ([Trades House Library Books, June 2023](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/trades_house_library_books_june_2023.pdf)). **A craft with no trade left, still sending apprentices abroad and making them write it up.**
- **1983 →** the *Let Glasgow Flourish* awards, founded by Ex Deacon John B. Park — who was himself Deacon in 1982 ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/); [Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)).
- **1987–88** — supported the Trades House Expedition to Papua New Guinea and the 1988 botanical exhibition ([Virtual Museum — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/gardeners.html)).
- **2000s** — funded the Millennium Garden at Strathclyde's Rottenrow campus ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)).
- **2017 / 2023 — women in the chair.** **Pauline Bell**, Deacon 2017, is the first woman on the published list of Deacons running back to 1626; **Janet Pierotti** followed in 2023 ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). **[extrapolated: "first" is my reading of the list, which is complete from 1830 and patchy before.]** The most recent Deacon listed is **Stephen Docherty, 2024**.
- **Today** — approximately **750 members**; Master Court in October, February, May and August; King Charles III "the most distinguished member" and "an Honorary Master" ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/); [Virtual Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/gardeners.html)). Income for the year to 31 May 2025 £48,984 against expenditure £54,950 ([OSCR](https://www.oscr.org.uk/about-charities/search-the-register/charity-details?number=SC004606)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **The Deacon and the charter — two stories, and the craft tells the gentler one.** This is now the richest thing in the dossier, because there are **two incompatible accounts and both are respectable**.
  - *The craft's own version, told on its website:* "This Charter was lost, it is believed, in the year **1646**... the Deacon of the Incorporation was infected with the plague, which was raging in the Burgh at that time, and he was sent to the '**Foull Moor**' where he was **cleansed**," the charter believed lost among his possessions and never recovered ([Incorporation of Gardeners — History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)). In this version the Deacon *survives* — he is cleansed — and only the paper is lost.
  - *The Trades House Clerk's version, printed 45 years before the craft's own history:* "The deacon of this incorporation **died**, in **1649**, of the plague, then prevalent in Glasgow, and the original charter incorporating this trade, **being in the house at the time of his death, was burned along with all his furniture and papers**" ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). In this version the Deacon dies in his own house and the charter burns in the fumigation of his goods.
  - **Who was he?** The deacons list gives **Johne Woddrow** for 1648 *and* 1649, and he vanishes from the roll after 1649; Thomas Lowrie succeeds in 1650 ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). The last documentary sighting of Woddrow alive is a Trades House court of **10 September 1649**. **[extrapolated: identifying Crawfurd's plague-dead deacon as John Woddrow is my inference from the chronology of the deacons list — no source names him.]**
  - Whichever is true, note what the craft did next: **nothing, for forty-one to forty-four years.** They kept trading, kept admitting, kept holding courts, on nothing but collective memory, until in 1690 they went back to the Council and asked for it all in writing again. *(The location of Glasgow's "Foull Moor" is still not established in any source I could reach. Do not place it on a map.)*
- **The wronging of John Woddrow, 10 September 1649.** Weeks before the plague year closed over him, the visitor of the gardeners was assaulted by one of his own. The Trades House minute: "**Thomas Lourie gairdner is vnlawit in the sowme of twentie merks money for the wronging and injureing of Johne Woddrow visito' of the gardners sundrie wayes suiiiUe provin** befoir the deikin conveiner deikins and y' counsall, and ordaines him to **entir his persoune in ward in the tolbooth of this brugh** q'' he pay samyne and give satisfactioune for the sd wrong, and that the sd Johne Woddrow declair he hes gottin satisfactioun" ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). Fined twenty merks, jailed in the Tolbooth until he paid **and** until the injured man himself declared satisfaction. **And in 1650 — the very next election — Thomas Lowrie is chosen Deacon of the Gardeners** ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). He had already been deacon in 1645 and 1657, and he was still taking apprentices in 1656. *The man who was jailed for injuring the head of the craft became the head of the craft within the year.* Nobody has ever written this down as a story. It is the best one they have.
- **The room the Gardeners were not in (9 March 1650).** While that quarrel was being settled, the deacon convener and **twelve** deacons were dividing the Trades House's quarter of the Gorbals lands into thirty-one shares. Tailors six. Hammermen two. Bakers a half. Masons a half. **Gardeners: none** ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). A century and a half later, when that ground became Tradeston and started paying, the shares were copied out unaltered into the disposition of 1 June 1792 — and eleven crafts drew feu duties from it into the 21st century ([Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). **The Gardeners were not robbed in 1789. They were forgotten in 1650, and the forgetting was notarised.**
- **The King's deacon, 1686.** Against that year the craft's own list reads: "**James Cross ~ nominated by his Majestie visitor for a year. His Majestie having nominant this year both Magistrats Counsell and Deacons**" ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). James VII appointed the head of the gardeners of Glasgow. And then the list **goes blank for fourteen years** — no deacon recorded from 1687 until Thomas Nicoll in 1700, a hole that swallows the Revolution *and the 1690 Seal of Cause itself*. Yet the burgh records place **Patrick Tennent, visitor of gardeners, on 12 December 1688** ([Glasgow burgh records, 1688](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol3/pp407-419)). **The craft's own memory of the years in which it recovered its charter is missing, and has to be reconstructed from the town's paperwork.**
- **The manure money (1792).** When the Weavers sold the Rottenrow ground out from under them, the gardeners were compensated not for the crop but for the *soil*: **£6 10s 6d** to the widow of John Allan, gardener, "in name of damages for **manure laid on the ground**," and **£9 12s** to John Dougal, gardener, likewise (20 March and 14 February 1792) ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)). A craft that spent decades enriching another incorporation's dirt, paid off in pounds for the black earth it had made. Still the emotional centre of the dossier.
- **The gardeners of the Rottenrow, 1682–1792 — tenants of the Weavers.** The Weavers' charter chest holds a century of leases: a tack to **James M'Indoe, gardener**, of the house and yard "commonly called **Bowastie**, on the north side of Rottenrow" (29 Nov 1682); **Robert Dougald, gardener** (19 Oct 1703); **Janet Anderson, relict of Patrick Tennent, gardener**, of "a large yard, and a 'little yard at the head of it,' newly taken in by the Weavers" (20 Dec 1717); tacks 1731–1792 to **Hugh Tennent, late visitor of the gardeners; James M'Lom; Archibald M'Cousland; Thomas Barton; John Jamieson;** and **John Allan**, all gardeners; and a 1754 tack to **Archibald M'Auley, gardener**, of ground "**only to be used as garden grounds**" ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **The Tennent dynasty of the yards.** One family holds the headship again and again: **Robert Tennent** (deacon 1658, 1668, 1672, 1676, 1678, 1682, 1683), **Patrick Tennent** (visitor 1688), **Hugh Tennent** (deacon 1723, 1724, 1746, and "late visitor of the gardeners" in the Weavers' tacks) ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf); [burgh records vol. 3](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol3/pp555-574); [*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)). The 1903 roll shows the mechanism: "**Patrik Tenent, son of Robert Tenent, and Prentise to Robert Tenent**" — son and apprentice to the same man, the craft reproducing itself inside one household ([1903 history, p.181](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)).
- **The stallengers — freedom on the instalment plan.** The roll of the 1680s and 90s is thick with a category the first pass never saw: "**William Aikine, Stallenger, till he be admitted Burgess**"; "Alexander Grahame, Stallenger"; "Robert Laurie, Stallenger"; "Johne Craig, Stallenger"; "William Stewart, Stallenger"; and a bracketed pair, "Johne Allane / Johne Hatrick — **Stallengers**" ([1903 history, pp.182–183](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). A stallenger paid for licence to keep a stall without being free of the burgh — a man tolerated at the edge of the market until he could afford to belong. *The 1903 book carries a footnote, "Stallenger. See note, p. 29," which is on a page nobody has scanned.*
- **The charity apprentice (c.1742).** "**John Rodger**, son of John Rodger, Booked Apprentice with John Jamieson, being a **charity Apprentice from the Town Hospital, paid only clerk and officer's dues**" ([1903 history, p.192](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). A pauper child out of the workhouse, indentured to a freeman gardener, every fee waived but the two men's wages who wrote it down.
- **Gilbert Wilson's three pounds.** "**Gilbert Wilson (entry money £3 scots, quarterly, as being very poor, old and infirm)**" ([1903 history, p.186](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). In the same decade a stranger paid £60 Scots to enter. The craft cut a poor old man's entry to a twentieth and let him pay it by the quarter. **This single line is the best evidence in the whole dossier that the Gardeners' benevolence predates the loss of the monopoly by 130 years.**
- **The Duke's gardener buys in (c.1717).** "**Hugh Ronald, late Gardener to the Duke of Montrose, now to Daniel Campbell of Shawfield (paid 60 pounds scots as freedom fyne)**"; and later "**Robert Faulds, a stranger, servitor to the Laird of Jordanhill, paid £60 scots as freedom fyne**"; and "**Andrew Clark, servitor to Richard Cockburn of Clarkeson Toun (pays 100 merks scots as freedom fyne, and officer's fee, and clerk and officer's dues)**"; and "**James Miller, late servant to Duke of Montrose**" ([1903 history, pp.185–192](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). The great estate gardeners came into town and paid the full stranger's rate to trade in it. *Daniel Campbell of Shawfield's Glasgow mansion was sacked by the mob in the Malt Tax riot of 1725 — his gardener was already a Glasgow freeman.* **[extrapolated: the 1725 connection is context, not a Gardeners record.]**
- **The essay — it existed.** Buried in the 1745 entries: "**John Muir, Weaver, son of Thomas Muir, Weaver, produced his essay**" ([1903 history, p.192](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). The first pass could find no Gardeners essay and warned storytellers not to invent one. **We now know the Incorporation of Gardeners took essays.** We still do not know what a gardener's essay *was*. That is now a far better question than it was, because it is a real one.
- **Three women in the earliest roll.** Among the first pages of names — between 1628 and 1661 — stand **Margat Fynne**, **Margat Oswall** and **Marie Wilsoun**, entered without qualification, husband or father ([1903 history, p.180](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). *The printed year-column did not survive the scan at that point, so I cannot date them exactly; they sit in the stretch before the roll's next legible year, 1661.* Widows plainly traded — "Janet Anderson, **relict** of Patrick Tennent, gardener," held a Weavers' tack in her own name in 1717 ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)) — but three women on the members' roll of a seventeenth-century Glasgow craft is a bigger fact than that, and nobody has written about it.
- **The gardener of the Gorbals, 17 April 1650.** "**Robert Pollok sone to vmq'll James Pollok gardner in Gorballs** is buikit prenteis with James Jape skinner" ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). A dead gardener's son from the Gorbals, apprenticed out of the trade into skinning — five weeks after the Gorbals lands were divided among crafts that did not include his father's.
- **The apprentice who crosses the sources.** The Trades House records book Richard Glene prentise "with **Thomas Cowrie** gairdner" for five years and two years for meat and fee, 14 April 1656 ([*Records*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)); the Gardeners' own roll has "**Ritchard Glen, Prentise to Thomas Lourie**" ([1903 history, p.181](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). The same boy in two books, and the OCR's "Cowrie" is Lourie — the same Thomas Lourie who went to the Tolbooth in 1649.
- **The thirteen poor men, and what a gardener's pauper cost.** Under the House's bye-laws of 1729–38 each craft installed a poor man in the almshouse at a set rate: hammermen, tailors, cordiners and maltmen **one hundred marks Scots**; weavers, baxters, wrights and skinners **£60 Scots**; "coopers, fleshers, masons, **gardeners**, and barbers, **forty-eight pounds**." The gardeners' man was **Thomas Morrison, gardener** ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Cheapest tier, and named.
- **The craft with no gardeners in it.** The men entering the Incorporation in its final documented year: coalmasters, stockbrokers, a drysalter, a wine merchant, a laundry proprietor, a brush manufacturer, a surgeon dentist, a chartered accountant named **Menotti Garibaldi M'Adam**, an "Editorial Assistant," an artist, a biscuit baker, a boiler maker, medical students ([1903 history, pp.225–227](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). Fifty-seven years after the monopoly died, the Incorporation of Gardeners contained almost nobody who gardened — and was about to start funding travelling scholarships for people who did.
- **Five generations of Wilsons, and the Austin line.** The 1903 craft genealogies run like pedigrees: Alex. Campbell (1861) entered as son-in-law of William Wilson (1819), son of James Wilson (1772), son of James Wilson (1734), son of James Wilson (1728), son of **Mathew Wilson, entered 7th May 1692**. And twice over: "**James Austin, entered 17th Nov. 1821 as Apprentice to John M'Aslan**" ([1903 history, pp.243–244](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_genealogy_comlplete_1.pdf)). **The Austin & McAslan nursery partnership is inside the craft's own membership tree, as master and apprentice, two years before it appears in any horticultural history.**
- **The lost minute book, 1774–1835.** The clerks' list runs Johne Blair 1626, Johne Selkrig 1635, John Andersone 1650, William Selkrig 1664, Joannes Lees 1674, Robert Allane 1677, **Robert Robertson, March 1690** (the man who drew up the new Seal of Cause year), Johne Syme 1699, Matthew Gilmour 1774 — and then a single line: "**Minute book missing**." The next clerk is John Paul, 16 October 1835 ([Clerks](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_~_clerks_1.docx)). **This craft lost its records twice: the charter in the plague, and sixty-one years of minutes to nothing anybody has recorded at all.**
- **Not the Free Gardeners.** A storyteller will conflate these and must not. The **Order of Free Gardeners** was a separate, non-incorporated fraternity: oldest record the Haddington lodge minute book of **16 August 1676**, opening with fifteen rules called *Interjunctions for ye Fraternity of Gardiners of East Lothian*; a Dunfermline lodge c.1715 admitting many non-gardeners; three degrees built on **Adam** as "the first Free Gardener," **Noah** the second, **Solomon** the third; regalia adding "the pruning knife, presented as 'the simplest tool of gardening'" to the compass and square. Crucially, Scotland's rural gardeners "could not obtain the status of an incorporation... and modelled their organisation on that of the masons." The Haddington lodge died **22 February 1953** ([Order of Free Gardeners, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Free_Gardeners)). Glasgow City Archives hold a minute book of the **West of Scotland Grand Lodge of the Ancient Order of Free Gardeners from 1859**, and in 1879 the Western Order opened the St Andrew Order of Ancient Free Gardeners Friendly Society ([Glasgow Life — City Archives](https://www.glasgowlife.org.uk/libraries/city-archives/archive-collections)) — so the two bodies were operating in the same city, on the same iconography, for at least seventy years. **And the reason the Free Gardeners existed is the 1833 bye-law in reverse: gardeners "lived outside burghs on nearby landed estates or market gardens and so could not become burgesses, the essential qualification to join incorporations."** Glasgow's Gardeners got the thing the rest could not — and got a special dispensation to keep it while living outside the town.
- **The shield on the throne.** The Deacon Convener's Chair of State, designed by David Hamilton in 1819, carries fourteen silver shields with the arms of the Incorporated Trades — Adam, Eve and the serpent on the seat of the head of the whole House ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/who-we-are/)). The Trades Hall stairwell window shows the arms of all fourteen "as they appeared in 1888," under the inscription "Instituted by Letter of Guildry 1605" ([Virtual Museum — Stained Glass Window](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/stained-glass-window.html)).
- **The living legend: Let Glasgow Flourish.** Founded 1983 by Ex Deacon John B. Park, judged every August by members of the Master Court walking the city centre, east end, west end, south side and Merchant City, awarding Bronze, Silver and Gold to "commercial or institutional organizations... which create and maintain arboreal or floral displays of high standard for public benefit, ranging from hanging baskets to large ambitious plantings," the overall winner taking the **Robertson Silver Spade** (named for Ex Deacon T. Iain Robertson, Deacon 1978) at a Trades Hall ceremony the following spring. In February 2026, 72 organisations were honoured — 16 Gold, 43 Silver ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/); [Glasgow Chamber of Commerce](https://www.glasgowchamberofcommerce.com/news/news/2026/february/26/72-businesses-honoured-at-this-year-s-incorporation-of-gardeners-let-glasgow-flourish-awards/)). A craft with no monopoly left still sends its officers out to *inspect the burgh's work* every August — **the visitation outlived the trade by 180 years.** *(Sources disagree on the Spade's first award: 1994 per the Incorporation, 1992 per the Chamber. Unresolved.)*

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Arms — the official blazon, at last.** Matriculated in the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in Scotland, **grant dated 25 March 1924, volume 26 folio 29**:
  > "**Argent on a mount in base, the Tree of Paradise, environed with a serpent, between Adam and Eve all proper, on a chief Azure a spade and rake in saltire between in the dexter the sun in his splendour and in the sinister the moon in her increment all proper.**"
  > — [Court of the Lord Lyon, *Practical Heraldry for the Self-Isolator, Week 6a — The Incorporated Trades*](http://courtofthelordlyon.scot/IsolationHeraldryWeek6A.pdf)
  - Read the words. Not "an apple tree" — **the Tree of Paradise**. Not "with a serpent" — **environed with** a serpent, encircled, wrapped. Not "a crescent moon" — **the moon in her increment**, the *waxing* moon, the moon on her way up. The craft's own herald chose a growing moon over a full one. **The only increasing thing on the shield is in the sky.**
  - The Gardeners have **no crest**: "Only the Hammermen and the Cordiners have a crest" ([Lord Lyon, Week 6a](http://courtofthelordlyon.scot/IsolationHeraldryWeek6A.pdf)).
  - **The 1917–1924 matriculation wave.** All fourteen crafts registered within seven years: Masons first (19 Feb 1917), then Cordiners (1921), Weavers (1922), Barbers and Hammermen and Bakers (1923), and in 1924 Skinners (14 Feb), Fleshers (25 Feb), **Wrights (17 March)**, **Gardeners (25 March)**, Tailors (14 May), Coopers (26 June), Bonnetmakers & Dyers (14 July), Maltmen (21 July) ([Lord Lyon, Week 6a](http://courtofthelordlyon.scot/IsolationHeraldryWeek6A.pdf)). The Gardeners went to the Lyon eight days after the Wrights. Their arms had been painted on boards in the almshouse and repainted for the new Trades Hall in the 1790s ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)) — **borne for at least three centuries before they were lawful.**
  - Read it as the craft reads it: the **shield** is the first garden and the first gardeners; the **chief** is everything the gardener cannot control (sun, moon, day, night, season) set over the only two things he can (spade, rake). Human tools under celestial time. Every gardener's actual theology in one badge.
- **Motto: "Gardening — the First of Arts."** English, not Latin; a claim of primacy, not a prayer ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)). Compare the sister trade in London, chartered 9 November 1616, whose arms show **Adam delving with a spade** and whose motto is the curse of Genesis 3:19 — *"In the sweat of thy brows shalt thow eate thy bread"* ([Worshipful Company of Gardeners](https://www.gardenerscompany.org.uk/company/history/)). **Same Eden, opposite reading.** London remembers the punishment; Glasgow remembers that the job existed before the punishment did. And now read the motto against the Letter of Guildry of 1605, which ranked kail and onions among the "small things" beneath a guild brother's honour ([Crawfurd, 1858, appendix](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). **Glasgow's motto is not a boast into empty air. It is an answer to a specific insult written into the city's constitution.**
- **Patron saint: none evidenced, and probably none ever.** Glasgow's pre-Reformation crafts kept altars in the Cathedral — the Hammermen's St Eloi aisle still carries their emblem in its floor ([Hammermen of Glasgow](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/our-history/)); the Masons' 1551 charter directs fines to St Thomas's altar. The Gardeners incorporated **c.1605/1626 — two generations after the Reformation** — so there was no altar to endow and no saint to keep. Their patron, functionally, is **Adam**, and he is on the shield, and the Lyon Court calls his tree the Tree of Paradise. **[extrapolated: the "therefore no patron saint" inference is mine from the chronology; no source states the Gardeners lacked one.]**
  - For colour only, and **not to be attributed to Glasgow**: the European patron of gardeners is **St Fiacre** (c.600–670), an Irish hermit near Meaux who built "a hermitage for his dwelling, a vegetable and herb garden, an oratory... and a hospice," depicted with a **spade and a basket of vegetables**, feast **30 August / 1 September**. Legend: Bishop Faro offered him as much land as he could entrench in one day with a furrow, and Fiacre "turned up the earth with the end of his staff, toppling trees and uprooting briers and weeds" — after which he barred women from the precincts on pain of infirmity ([Saint Fiacre, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Fiacre)). A saint who took land by digging faster than anyone believed possible, and then fenced it. **Note the accidental rhyme worth using: Fiacre's feast is 1 September, and the craft's own history closes on 1 September 1903.** Useful as an *echo*; there is no Glasgow evidence, and the Free Gardeners' Adam-Noah-Solomon degrees show the Scottish tradition went to the Old Testament, not to the saints.
- **The House above them.** The Trades House "simply uses the arms of the City of Glasgow but with the lower half of the shield **GULES**. The crest is 14 arrows representative of the Trades" ([Lord Lyon, Week 6a](http://courtofthelordlyon.scot/IsolationHeraldryWeek6A.pdf)); registered 1911, motto **"Union is strength"** ([TheGlasgowStory](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/image/?inum=TGSA05258)). The Gardeners are one arrow in that sheaf — and the tree on the city's own arms is, of course, a tree.
- **"Let Glasgow Flourish."** The city's motto, cut down from "Lord, let Glasgow flourish by the preaching of thy word" ([Coat of arms of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Glasgow)). The Gardeners took the horticultural half of Glasgow's own prayer and made it the name of their awards. No other craft can claim the city's motto is a gardening word.

## Rituals & Customs

- **Entry, as it still runs.** Four steps: "**Apply to become a Burgess of the City of Glasgow**" (£30); "**Have your name matriculated onto the roll at Trades House**" (£25, or £5 if under 16); "**Apply for membership of the Incorporation**"; "**Your application is considered at the next quarterly meeting of the Master Court**" ([Membership](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/membership/)). Burgess-ship first, craft second — the medieval sequence, intact. The old restriction to men with a gardening business in Glasgow is gone.
- **Near Hand and Far Hand — and now the craft's *own* attestation.** Entry money is **£75** for "the spouse, child or son/daughter-in-law of an existing member" (*Near Hand*) and **£125** for everyone else (*Far Hand*) ([Membership](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/membership/)). The first pass could only source the phrase from a Wrights minute of 1786. It is now attested **in the Gardeners' own book**, used as a bare technical term hundreds of times in the 1903 craft genealogy: "entered 26th Jan. 1903 (**far-hand**)"; "John Macintyre, entered 10th Sept. 1777 (**far-hand**)" ([1903 history, pp.243–](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_genealogy_comlplete_1.pdf)). **A distinction between kin and stranger, in continuous use in this craft for at least 249 years, still on the price list, still exactly two-fifths cheaper.**
- **The eight doors into the craft.** The 1903 roll records every entrant's route, in a fixed vocabulary ([1903 history, pp.179–227](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)):
  1. **a Freeman's son** — "Johne Bartoun, a Freeman's son"; fee typically **10 merks**.
  2. **Booked Prentise / Booked Apprentice to** a named freeman — the seven-year road.
  3. **Booked Journeyman with** — "John Norie... Booked Journeyman with David Robb."
  4. **by marriage** — "as having married Janet Dougall, daughter to Thomas Dougall"; "as marrying Marion Allan, daughter of John Allan." Roughly a third of all entries. Sons-in-law count as near hand to this day.
  5. **a stranger** — "Arthur Hamilton, Gardener, **a stranger**"; **£60 Scots**.
  6. **Stallenger** — licensed to a stall "till he be admitted Burgess."
  7. **Burgess gratis** — "William Finlay, a stranger (**Burgess gratis**)"; "William Scott (Burgess gratis)."
  8. **by the grandfather's line** — "**Andrew Ralstoun**, son of the deceased William Ralstoun, Cordinier. *This member is admitted through paying his father's freedom fine, his grandfather having been a member*."
  *And the ninth, unnamed: the essay. "John Muir, Weaver, son of Thomas Muir, Weaver, **produced his essay**."*
- **The price of belonging, in Scots money.** A freeman's son, **10 merks** of fine plus £4 Scots to clerk and officer. An apprentice, **54 shillings Scots**. A stranger, **£60 Scots** — about 120 times the son's rate. Andrew Clark, servant to a landowner, **100 merks**. And Gilbert Wilson, poor, old and infirm, **£3 Scots, payable quarterly** ([1903 history, pp.185–192](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). **The craft priced membership by what it thought your presence was worth to it, and then made exceptions in the other direction.**
- **The musket, then the sword.** Every new deacon of every craft gave a weapon into the Trades Hospital armoury; "David Jack visito' of ye gairdneris gav in y' muskatts bandaleiris and stafts" on 22 August 1632. On **10 August 1649** the House enacted "that everie new elected deikin sall giwe in to ye hospitall **in place and sted of his muskett ane sufficient sword and belt worth the sowme of ten merkis money**, and that everie new electit deikine conveaner the first zeir of his electioune sall giwe in... **ane pick and sword and ane belt**" in place of his corslet ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). Forty-two muskets hung in the hospital by 1638. **The head of the gardeners armed the poorhouse.**
- **The leet of two, and its abolition.** Until **22 January 1745** the craft submitted two names and the magistrates chose the Deacon; thereafter it elected its own ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt); "First Electyion of Deacon by Craft," [Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). And in 1686 the King had dispensed with even the leet and simply named their man.
- **The Lammas Court.** The Master Court meets in **October, February, May and August** ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)) — Martinmas, Candlemas, Whitsunday, Lammas ([Quarter days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_days)) — and the August sitting is still called the **Lammas Court**, at 7pm after the Master Court at 6pm ([Events](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/events/)). Lammas, 1 August, is the loaf-mass, the first-fruits day. **A gardeners' court sitting at first-fruits is the calendar telling the truth about the trade.** The seventeenth-century roll bears this out: entries cluster hard in **August and September**, the two months after harvest, when a gardener knew what his year had been worth.
- **The Choosing Dinner.** A Friday evening in late October — the Deacon's choosing, the craft's own new year ([Events](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/events/)). The AGM falls in September. Ceremonial gifts persist: at the 1901–2 Deacon Convener's dinner "the Chairman presented the Deacon-Convener with **an ivory mallet**" ([1903 history, p.178](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)).
- **The Deacon and the Visitor.** The Deacon "is appointed annually and attends a number of events as the Incorporation's representative," leading the Master Court, with a Clerk and an Officer beneath ([Incorporation of Gardeners](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/)). But through the seventeenth century the head of this craft was styled **visitor** as often as deacon — *the man who goes round and looks* ([burgh records vol. 3](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol3/pp555-574); [*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). For a trade whose whole quality control is walking the yards and judging what has grown, "visitor" is the truer title — **and the August judging walk of the Let Glasgow Flourish awards is the same act, four centuries on.**
- **In the House.** The Gardeners send their **Deacon, Late Deacon and two ordinary members** — four of sixty-four trustees today ([Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)); under the 1777 decree it was **the deacon with two assistants**, three of fifty-four ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Deacons wear the historic chains of office — collectively "the Chain Gang" ([Virtual Museum — Regalia](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/regalia.html)).
- **The modern relic: the Robertson Silver Spade.** A silver spade, awarded annually to the best floral display in Glasgow ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/)). The tool from the chief of their own arms, rendered in silver, given away every year.
- **Charity as the surviving rite.** "Care of its pensioners, providing annual prizes and travelling scholarships for gardening apprentices and students of horticulture" ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)). Recent grantees: Castlemilk High School, Horatio's Garden, the Maggie's Centre at Queen Elizabeth University Hospital ([Incorporation of Gardeners](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/)); an annual prize for new plotholders with the Glasgow Allotments Forum ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/)). **The line runs unbroken from five merks and a load of coals to Androw Harvie in 1634.**

## Sensory World

*Sourced texture first; inferences tagged.*

- **The yards.** Not fields — **yairds**: walled or dyked enclosures on the north side of the Rottenrow, a "large yard" and a "little yard at the head of it," one named **Bowastie**, leased house-and-yard together so the gardener lived on his ground; let "only to be used as garden grounds" ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)). A lease clause that tells you the town was hungry for building plots and the gardener was the last man holding soil inside the burgh.
- **Outside the wall.** The craft's ground was assumed to be beyond the burgh: only the maltmen and the gardeners could sit in the House "although not resident in, or in the occupancy of a place of business within that district," and this by "the ancient law of the House" ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Gardeners in the Gorbals ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)); in Partick, Govan, Rutherglen, Whiteinch, Camlachie Toll, Scotstoun Mill, Shettleston, Tollcross ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). **The map of this craft is a ring around Glasgow, not a street in it.**
- **The market basket of 1605.** What a gardener actually sold, in the words of the city's own constitution: "**eggs, green herring, pears, apples, corn, candle, onions, kail, straw, bread... milk, and such like small things**" ([Letter of Guildry, in Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). Pears and apples and onions and kail, on a board, beside herring.
- **Manure, constantly.** The defining smell and the defining asset — enough of it, over enough years, that when the ground was sold the Weavers paid damages **for the manure alone** ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)). The **midden** is in the language: "A begun turn is half ended; quoth the good Wife when she stuck the Grape in the Midding" (1721) ([DSL, *graip*](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/graip_n_v1)).
- **Coal.** Not incidental. The Gorbals lands the craft was cut out of were bought for their **coal-heughs**, worked under lease by Patrick Bryce, who "**maliciously drowned the coal-work**" of a neighbour and threatened "to cut the cords" of anyone sent down to inspect (parliamentary minute, 3 April 1661) ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). And coal is what charity looked like: "fyve merks money **and ane laid of coillis**" ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)).
- **Tools, named.** The **spade** and the **rake** on the shield — crossed in saltire, per the blazon. The **graip** — "an iron-pronged fork used in farming and gardening," which Burns puts in a drunk man's hands: "The *graip* he for a *harrow* taks, An' haurls at his curpan" (1786) ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/graip_n_v1)). The Free Gardeners added the **pruning knife** to compass and square as "the simplest tool of gardening" ([Order of Free Gardeners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Free_Gardeners)).
- **The crop.** **Kail** above all — attested 1662: "Remember when ye delvit your kail yard that Bessie Neil got two spaidful of the *kailyard*" ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/results/kailyard)). **Syboes** (spring onions): "To Jamie Gray that he gave for ane unce of sybaes seed" (1701); "Plantin' syboes roond his cottage home" (1904); "If mair sybies were eaten there would be fewer doctors" (1894) ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/sybow)). **Grosert** bushes — "Sax grosset-busses" (1868) ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/results/grosert)). **Neeps** and **taties**: "He saw me howkin' taties fornent our back shop window" (1860) ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/howk_v_n1)).
- **Shelter.** The **plantie-cruive** — "an inclosure for raising cabbage plants" (Orkney, 1845) ([DSL](https://dsl.ac.uk/results/plantie-cruive)). *(Northern-isles usage; flavour, not Glasgow vocabulary.)*
- **The commercial nursery, later.** Forest-trees, fruit-trees, shrubs, greenhouse plants and roses, on ground running most of a mile at Coplawhill ([Bygone Bungo](https://bygone.bungoblog.com/wp/horticultural-strathbungo-and-the-austin-mcaslan-nursery/)). Glass, heat, imported stock, a brother trained at Kew.
- **The modern sensorium.** Hanging baskets on a pub front; a Gold-award planting outside a Gurdwara; Master Court members with clipboards walking the Merchant City in the first fortnight of August ([Glasgow Chamber of Commerce](https://www.glasgowchamberofcommerce.com/news/news/2026/february/26/72-businesses-honoured-at-this-year-s-incorporation-of-gardeners-let-glasgow-flourish-awards/)).
- **[extrapolated]** Sounds and smells entailed but not directly recorded: the scrape of a spade on stony Glasgow clay, the ring of a graip's tines on a stone, wet leaf-mould, the ammonia of a fresh midden and the sweetness of an old one, rain on a dyke, and the particular silence of a walled yard inside a noisy burgh.

## Values

1. **Patience — the craft that plants for someone else's eyes.** Trees, apprentices and scholarships are the same investment: "annual prizes and travelling scholarships for gardening apprentices and students of horticulture" ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/)), a paper trail of them from 1979 to 2012 on the library shelf ([library catalogue](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/trades_house_library_books_june_2023.pdf)). *Why:* no gardener has ever seen the mature form of most of what he plants, and the craft's whole institutional behaviour since 1846 has been to keep planting anyway.
2. **Tending over making.** Every other craft in the House *makes* a thing: a shoe, a barrel, a wall. The Gardeners' arms show a man and a woman who made nothing — they were *put in* a garden that already existed. The motto claims that keeping is the first art, older than any making. *Why:* it is the one craft whose masterpiece grows itself if you are good enough at not being in the way.
3. **Stewardship of ground you do not own.** A century of Rottenrow leases; compensation for manure rather than land; **and, now documented, absence from the thirty-one-part division of 9 March 1650 that eleven other crafts drew income from for three hundred and fifty years** ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt); [*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt); [Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). *Why:* this craft's documented economic history is improving other people's property and being paid off when they sell.
4. **Provision — feeding the burgh before beautifying it.** Incorporated among the food trades; Glasgow's vegetables came "from nearby nurseries by 'gardeners'" ([TheGlasgowStory](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/story/?id=TGSBE03)). *Why:* the kailyard was a larder centuries before it was an aesthetic — and the city's constitution filed it under "small things."
5. **Mercy inside the price list.** £60 Scots from a duke's gardener; £3 Scots, by the quarter, from a poor old man; nothing at all from a workhouse child ([1903 history](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). *Why:* the same body that charged a stranger a hundred and twenty times the son's rate wrote down, in the same ledger, that Gilbert Wilson was very poor, old and infirm. **Benevolence is not a post-1846 pivot in this craft. It is in the seventeenth-century books.**
6. **Beauty as a public utility.** Let Glasgow Flourish awards displays maintained "for public benefit," judged in the street, free to anyone walking past ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/)). *Why:* the only trade whose product cannot be locked in a shop; a flourishing garden is consumed by strangers at no charge and this craft calls that success.
7. **Continuity without paper.** Charter gone in 1646 or 1649; craft still functioning in 1690 to petition for a new one; **sixty-one years of minutes lost between 1774 and 1835 and the craft never missed an election** ([Clerks](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_~_clerks_1.docx); [Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). *Why:* an institution is not a document, it is a continuous practice — and this craft has proved that twice.
8. **Renewal — the belief that anything can be brought back.** The post-1846 pivot from monopoly to benevolence; grants to Horatio's Garden and a Maggie's Centre, gardens made for the sick; the millennium garden built on the site of a demolished maternity hospital at Rottenrow ([Trades House — Gardeners](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-gardeners/); [Rottenrow Gardens](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/rottenrow-gardens)). *Why:* pruning, grafting and composting are the same conviction — that what looks finished is raw material.

## Shadow

Every value above, curdled. This is the craft's own dark twin, not a generic vice list.

- **Patience becomes fatalism.** "It'll come right in its own time" is a gardener's wisdom and a coward's alibi. The craft that lost its charter and did not go back for it for **forty-four years** is the same craft that says wait. Somewhere in that gap is a Deacon who could have petitioned and chose the season after next. The Gardener's failure mode is not haste; it is the endlessly deferred spring.
- **Tending becomes control — and weeding becomes a moral category.** A gardener decides, daily, which living things belong and pulls out the rest. Scale that up and you have an incorporation with legal power "to prevent persons, other than members of the Incorporation, from carrying on their trade within the Burgh" ([History](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/history/)) — a licence to root out the unfree man feeding his family from a yard outside the rules. **The word for a self-seeded plant in the wrong place and the word for a working man without a freedom are, structurally, the same word.** Horticultural tidiness as a theory of who may live where.
- **Primacy becomes grandiosity.** Twelfth of fourteen; two assistants where the seniors had five; £50 18s 8d of a £7,908 building; forty shillings toward the school where the tailors gave twenty-two pounds — and the motto says *the First of Arts*. Precedence-envy dressed as cosmology. **The junior craft with the largest claim about itself is a recognisable human type, and it is not always a happy one.**
- **Stewardship becomes martyrdom, then resentment.** Decades of manure in another man's ground; £6 10s 6d and get off. Forgotten in 1650 and never once, in any surviving paper, recorded as having complained. A craft can build an identity out of being the one who improves and is never given title — and that identity quietly poisons every collaboration afterwards. **The Gardeners' shadow-question is whether the humility is real or is a grievance kept warm for three hundred and seventy-six years.**
- **Deference becomes collaboration.** In 1686 James VII abolished their election and named their visitor, and the craft's own list records it without a word of protest — then falls silent for fourteen years ([Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). A body that has been told it is twelfth for long enough learns to take the man it is given.
- **Fraternity becomes a closed shop with a violent temper.** Thomas Lourie beat or defamed the head of his own craft "sundrie wayes," went to the Tolbooth for it, and was elected Deacon the next year ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt); [Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). The Tennents, Ralstouns, Macaulays, Wilsons and Hatridges hold the chair generation on generation. **A craft that admits the stranger at a hundred and twenty times the son's price, and forgives its own anything.**
- **Beauty becomes façade.** Gold medals for hanging baskets while the ground behind the wall is poisoned. A craft that judges the city on what can be seen from the pavement in the first fortnight of August has invented a system gameable by anyone with a watering can and good timing. Flourishing and *appearing to flourish* are, for two weeks a year, indistinguishable — and this craft's awards cannot tell them apart.
- **Vocation becomes costume.** By 1903 the Incorporation of Gardeners was coalmasters, stockbrokers, drysalters and dentists, awarding prizes to the people who actually dug ([1903 history, pp.225–227](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). The craft that once fined a man for trading without freedom now exists to hand certificates to gardeners from a body containing none. **Patronage is what a trade becomes when it stops being one, and it is not obvious that this is a fall — but it is not obvious that it isn't.**
- **Eden becomes nostalgia.** Put the Fall on your shield and you have declared that the best day already happened and everything since is management of decline. The gardener who believes the garden was perfect before he arrived will spend his life restoring an imaginary condition and calling it care.
- **Renewal becomes refusal to let anything die.** Compost, graft, cut back, try again — the same instinct that saves an orchard keeps a dying institution, practice or relationship on life support long past the point where the honest act is to pull it up and plant something else.

## Rivals & Kin

- **Landlord and near-kin: the WEAVERS.** From at least 1682 to 1792 the Gardeners held the Weavers' Rottenrow yards on tack — house and yard, large yard and little yard — and when the Weavers sold up in 1792 they paid the gardeners off for their muck ([*Old Glasgow Weavers*](https://archive.org/stream/oldglasgowweaver00inco/oldglasgowweaver00inco_djvu.txt)). Not a rivalry: a century of rent, then an eviction with compensation. And the Weavers put £834 6s 4d into the Trades Hall to the Gardeners' £50 18s 8d ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)) — landlord in every sense. *Weavers also marry into the craft constantly: John Muir, Robert Winning, James Sym, John Bryson, Walter Wilson, Robert M'Ellroy, all weavers, all admitted Gardeners.*
- **The rank above: the MERCHANTS.** The real antagonist, and the first pass missed it. Article 23 of the Letter of Guildry names kail, onions, pears and apples among the "small things" a guild brother may not sell, "**which is not agreeable to the honour of the calling of a gild brother**" ([Crawfurd, 1858, appendix](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)). **The merchant rank wrote the Gardeners' produce into the city's constitution as beneath dignity, and the Gardeners answered with a motto claiming they were first.**
- **Fellow food trades: MALTMEN, BAKERS, FLESHERS, and the greengrocers.** Incorporated in the same wave around 1600 ([TheGlasgowStory](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/story/?id=TGSBE03)). The Maltmen are the Gardeners' true twin: the only other craft whose members were assumed to live outside the burgh, protected by the same ancient law ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)) — barley and kail, the two halves of the plant kingdom, both grown beyond the wall. Maltmen, fleshers, bakers and coopers all marry into the Gardeners in the roll. The recurring pairing "gardeners and greengrocers" points to the trade's real fault line — **grower versus seller** — but *I still found no minute of an actual Glasgow demarcation case.*
- **The two crafts that share their exclusion: BARBERS and BONNETMAKERS & DYERS.** These three alone are absent from the 1650 division and its 1792 implementation ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt); [Bryce, *Wrights*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). The Dyers ("litsters") are the closer ally: in the Trades House accounts of the 1640s and 50s the litsters and gardners are repeatedly booked together, apart from the thirteen deacons ([*Records of the Trades House*](https://archive.org/stream/recordsoftradesh00glas/recordsoftradesh00glas_djvu.txt)). **A natural alliance of the landless three — and the beginnings of an explanation: in 1650 the Gardeners and Dyers were not counted among the deacons at all.**
- **The seniors: HAMMERMEN, TAILORS, CORDINERS, MALTMEN.** The four with five assistants each, against whom the ten junior crafts — **Gardeners now documented among them** — litigated from 1771, winning a court-fixed ranking in 1777 rather than equality ([Crawfurd, 1858](https://archive.org/stream/asketchriseandp01crawgoog/asketchriseandp01crawgoog_djvu.txt)).
- **The parallel that is not a relative: the FREE GARDENERS.** Lodges, not an incorporation; Haddington 1676, Dunfermline c.1715; Adam/Noah/Solomon degrees; compass, square and pruning knife; formed precisely *because* rural gardeners "could not obtain the status of an incorporation" ([Order of Free Gardeners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Free_Gardeners)). A West of Scotland Grand Lodge minute book survives from **1859** in Glasgow City Archives, and the St Andrew Order opened in **1879** ([Glasgow Life](https://www.glasgowlife.org.uk/libraries/city-archives/archive-collections)) — **two bodies of Glasgow gardeners, one chartered and one fraternal, using the same Adam, in the same city, at the same time.** An unwritten sibling rivalry.
- **Kin by trade, not by charter: the NURSERYMEN and SEEDSMEN.** Austin & McAslan from 1717 ([Bygone Bungo](https://bygone.bungoblog.com/wp/horticultural-strathbungo-and-the-austin-mcaslan-nursery/)) — and now placed *inside* the craft: "James Austin, entered 17th Nov. 1821 **as Apprentice to John M'Aslan**," with Austins as Deacon in 1861 and 1881 and Hunters and M'Donalds marrying into the line ([1903 history, genealogy](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_genealogy_comlplete_1.pdf); [Past Deacons](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_gardeners_of_glasgow.pdf)). The commercial growers were the craft, and eventually outgrew it.
- **The great houses: MONTROSE, SHAWFIELD, JORDANHILL, WILLIAMWOOD, CLARKESTON.** The estate gardeners who bought into the burgh craft at the stranger's rate ([1903 history, pp.185–190](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/gardeners_members001_1.pdf)). **The Incorporation is the interface between the aristocratic garden and the city market, and it charged for the passage.**
- **Modern kin: the ALLOTMENT HOLDERS.** The Incorporation sponsors an annual prize for new plotholders with the Glasgow Allotments Forum ([Awards](https://www.gardenersofglasgow.org/awards/)) — the freemen of the yards funding the amateurs of the yards, four centuries after the monopoly that would have made them criminals.
- **Not kin, despite appearances: the NECROPOLIS.** Glasgow's great garden-cemetery is the **Merchants House's** ground, bought as Wester Craigs in 1650 for its quarry, planted as the Fir Park, and laid out under a head gardener and superintendent named **George Mylne** ([Glasgow Necropolis — history](https://www.glasgownecropolis.org/history/); [Glasgow Life — Times Past](https://www.glasgowlife.org.uk/libraries/family-history/stories-and-blogs-from-the-mitchell/times-past-blogs/the-glasgow-necropolis-times-past)). **I found no Incorporation of Gardeners involvement in the Necropolis whatsoever.** The city's most famous designed landscape belongs to the other House. *Recording this as a negative finding so nobody assumes otherwise.*

## Question Seeds

Twelve dilemmas, each rooted in a documented episode of *this* craft. Each names the value the choice exposes.

1. **The manure (Rottenrow, 1792).**
   For nine years you have carted muck onto a yard you rent from the Weavers. You made that earth. It was pale clay when you took it and it is black now. The Weavers sell the ground for building and offer you six pounds, ten shillings and sixpence — "in name of damages for manure laid on the ground." Your neighbour John Dougal took nine pounds twelve and said nothing. **Do you take the money and agree that what you made was a cost, or refuse it and insist the soil itself is your work and cannot be bought at the price of dung?**
   *Reveals:* **Stewardship of ground you do not own** — and whether your humility about ownership is genuine or a grievance you have been saving. Accepting cleanly is stewardship. Refusing is pride wearing stewardship's coat. Haggling reveals a third thing: you believe value is negotiable, which no gardener who has watched a frost believes.

2. **The two charters (1646 / 1649).**
   Your craft's founding charter is gone. There are two stories. In the first, the Deacon caught the plague, was carted to the Foull Moor, was **cleansed**, and the charter was simply lost among his things. In the second — the one the Trades House's own Clerk wrote down — the Deacon **died in his own house**, and the charter was **burned with his furniture and his papers** while the town cleaned out the infection. Both are in print. The gentler one is on your website. **Which story do you teach the apprentices, knowing you cannot know, and knowing that the one you choose will be true in a hundred years?**
   *Reveals:* **Continuity without paper — and its cost.** The craft that governs by memory must eventually decide what the memory says. This asks whether you can hold "I do not know" as an institution, or whether every body of people eventually chooses the version of its origin it can bear, and calls that history.

3. **The Foull Moor (the night of it).**
   The Deacon is dying. His kist goes to the cleansing ground tonight, and every privilege, every rule, the whole legal existence of the gardeners of Glasgow is in it. You may send a man in after the kist and risk him, or let it burn with the shirts and govern on memory. **Which do you choose — and if you choose memory, what do you do when, forty-four years on, nobody alive remembers what the charter actually said?**
   *Reveals:* **Whether an institution is a document or a practice.** The craft's real answer was memory, for two generations. Then in 1690 it went back and asked the Council to write down what it now merely *believed* it had always been allowed to do.

4. **The room you were not in (9 March 1650).**
   The deacon convener and twelve deacons of crafts are dividing the Gorbals lands into thirty-one shares. The tailors take six. The masons take a half. You are the head of the gardeners and you are not one of the twelve, because your craft has a *visitor*, not a deacon, and is booked in the accounts after the deacons, with the dyers. You could go in and demand a share, and be told, correctly, that you are not entitled to be in the room. Or you could let it pass as a technicality of bookkeeping. **What do you do — and how would you know, standing there in 1650, that this is the single most consequential hour in your craft's history?**
   *Reveals:* **The difference between an injustice and an omission.** Nobody wronged the Gardeners in 1789; they were simply never written in, in 1650, and the paperwork was faithful for three hundred and fifty years. This asks whether you can recognise the moment that will matter when it presents itself as an administrative detail — and whether "I was not entitled to object" is a fact or an excuse.

5. **John M'Aslan's chair (1789).**
   You are a gardener, and you are **Deacon Convener of the Trades House** — the fourth of your craft in a hundred years to hold it. This year the House draws its lot of the Gorbals lands: seventy-eight acres, to be divided among eleven incorporations in thirty-one shares. **You will chair the meeting.** Your own craft gets nothing, by an instrument signed a hundred and thirty-nine years before you were born. You can rule from the chair that the division be reopened — and be the convener who used the whole House's highest office to enrich his own trade — or you can hold the gavel steady and hand your successors two centuries of quiet resentment they will inherit without knowing why.
   *Reveals:* **Whether office is a trust or an opportunity, when the trust is the thing that wronged you.** There is no clean answer. Reopening it is self-dealing that happens to be just; holding steady is integrity that happens to be a betrayal of the men who elected you.

6. **The weed you did not plant.**
   Something has come up that you did not sow — vigorous, healthy, thriving, in the wrong place. Your hand goes to it automatically. Now the same question at human scale: a man is working a yard inside the burgh without freedom of the Incorporation, undercutting your members, feeding four children. Your Seal of Cause gives you power to stop him. **Do you pull him out?**
   *Reveals:* **Tending over making — and its shadow.** The craft's whole competence is deciding what belongs. This asks whether you can tell the difference between a garden and a purge, and whether "it was in the wrong place" has ever been a sufficient reason for anything.

7. **Gilbert Wilson's three pounds.**
   A stranger stands in front of you and the fee is sixty pounds Scots. Behind him is an old man, poor and infirm, who has gardened all his life and cannot pay a twentieth of it. Your books say a freeman's son pays ten merks and a stranger pays sixty pounds, and the difference is not a scale of ability — it is a statement about blood. You can hold the price and be fair to every stranger who ever paid it, or write "**entry money £3 scots, quarterly, as being very poor, old and infirm**" and admit that your fee table was never about money. **And if you make the exception, what do you say to the stranger who paid in full last month?**
   *Reveals:* **Mercy inside the price list — and whether justice survives it.** The Gardeners did both, in the same ledger, in the same decade. This asks whether an institution can be merciful case by case without becoming arbitrary, and whether "we made an exception for him" is charity or just a nicer word for favouritism.

8. **The essay that takes a season.**
   Every craft in Glasgow makes its entrants prove themselves with an essay — a masterpiece, made under supervision. The Wrights lock theirs in an Essay House with a four-throw lock and one key to each of four Essay Masters, "to prevent fraudulent Essays in time coming." A wright makes a window in a day. **What do you set a gardener?** Anything he can finish in a day proves nothing; anything that proves something takes a year, during which he cannot trade and his family does not eat. **Do you set the true test and starve him, set an easy one and admit the craft cannot be examined, or admit him on his word and let the yard find him out?**
   *Reveals:* **Patience, colliding with the institution's need to certify.** Your craft's masterpiece is the one that cannot be inspected on demand — and it is the only craft in the House with that problem. *(The Gardeners did take essays: "John Muir, Weaver... produced his essay," 1745. What it consisted of, nobody now knows.)*

9. **The King's man (1686).**
   His Majesty has this year nominated the magistrates, the council, **and the deacons** — and the man he has named as visitor of the gardeners is James Cross, who is one of your own and has held the chair before by free election. He is a good choice. He is also the King's choice, and if you accept him you have conceded that the craft's head is a royal appointment. **Do you take the man you would have elected anyway, from the hand that had no right to offer him?**
   *Reveals:* **Deference, and how a liberty is actually lost** — not by being seized, but by being exercised on your behalf, correctly, once. Note what the record does: it writes the fact down without comment, and then goes silent for fourteen years.

10. **Thomas Lourie's election (1650).**
    Last September he was fined twenty merks and jailed in the Tolbooth for "wronging and injuring" the head of your craft "sundrie wayes," and was not released until the injured man himself declared he had been satisfied. That man is now dead. Thomas Lourie is the ablest gardener in Glasgow, he has been Deacon before, and he is the name on the most votes. **Do you seat him?**
    *Reveals:* **Whether a craft forgives or merely forgets — and whether they are the same act at a distance of one year.** The Gardeners seated him. The record does not say whether the dead man's family was in the room.

11. **The Silver Spade and the slow tree.**
    One season's budget, one pair of hands. Plant the bedding that will win the Robertson Silver Spade this August — judged from the pavement, photographed, applauded in the Trades Hall, seen by fifty thousand people — or put in an avenue of limes worth looking at in 2085, when nobody will know your name and the Incorporation may not exist. The limes will look like bare sticks in a muddy verge for eleven years. **Which do you plant, and what do you tell the Deacon in August?**
    *Reveals:* **Patience versus Beauty-as-public-utility.** Both are real values of this craft and here they are genuinely incompatible. The bedding is not vanity — the city gets something now. The limes are not virtue — you have spent public money on a benefit you arranged never to be judged on.

12. **The arms.**
    Every other craft chose a badge of competence: a hammer, a fleece, a pair of compasses, three castles. Your forebears chose Adam, Eve, the serpent and the fruit — **the exact moment the world broke** — and wrote underneath it *Gardening, the First of Arts*. London's gardeners chose the same picture and wrote the curse under it: *in the sweat of thy brows*. And the city's own founding constitution had already ruled that selling what you grow — "pears, apples... onions, kail" — is "not agreeable to the honour of the calling of a gild brother." **Which motto would you have put on the scroll — the boast or the curse — knowing the boast is an answer to an insult and the curse is an agreement with it?**
    *Reveals:* **The craft's whole theology, and which shadow you carry.** The boast risks grandiosity in a craft ranked twelfth of fourteen. The curse risks a lifetime of penitential labour and Eden-nostalgia. A third answer — that Adam gardened *before* the Fall as well as after, and the work is therefore neither reward nor punishment but simply the oldest thing people do — is the mature Gardener, and it is available to anyone who looks closely at the shield. *And note what the herald actually wrote in 1924: the moon is not a crescent, it is **the moon in her increment**. Waxing. Even on the badge of the Fall, this craft put something that was getting bigger.*
