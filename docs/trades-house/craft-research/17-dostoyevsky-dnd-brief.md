# 17 — Dostoyevsky Writes a D&D Campaign: the Literary Craft Brief

> Governing brief for the sorting-quiz authoring session. The 14 craft dossiers (01–14 in this
> directory) supply the *lore*; this brief supplies the *literary machinery* — what a question is,
> how it is built, what voice delivers it, and what is banned. Sources are cited inline. Dostoevsky
> scene references use the book/part/chapter divisions shared by the standard Garnett and
> Pevear–Volokhonsky translations; where a detail could not be verified against a text, it is
> tagged `[verify]`. Nothing in this brief ties an example to a specific craft — that mapping is
> the authoring session's job, and doing it here would bake in exactly the telegraphy this brief
> exists to prevent (see Anti-pattern X7).

---

## 0. The thesis

The quiz must feel like **Dostoyevsky wrote a D&D campaign**: every question a small, concrete,
second-person scene at a moral threshold, with an irreversible choice between options a serious
person could each defend — delivered by a 17th-century Scots narrator who kens ye better than ye
ken yersel, and who never, ever tells ye what yer answer meant.

The two traditions repair each other's failure modes:

- Dostoevsky supplies **moral pressure without a correct answer**. His novels are, in Bakhtin's
  famous formulation, polyphonic — "a plurality of independent and unmerged voices," none of them
  the author's mouthpiece, none finally refuted
  ([Bakhtin, *Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problems_of_Dostoevsky%27s_Poetics)).
  Left alone, though, this tradition drifts toward abstraction and gloom.
- The tabletop supplies **concreteness and agency**. The fundamental loop of D&D is the DM
  describing a specific environment and the player saying what *you* do
  ([D&D Basic Rules, "How to Play"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/basic-rules-2014)); the
  craft of good prompts is the craft of *situations, not plots* — pressure without a
  predetermined outcome ([The Alexandrian, "Don't Prep Plots"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots)).
  Left alone, though, this tradition drifts toward optimisation puzzles with a best answer.
- The forced-choice quiz supplies **the sorting mechanism** — and psychometrics confirms the
  instinct: pairing options *matched in desirability* is precisely how forced-choice instruments
  defeat the urge to pick the answer that makes you look good
  ([forced-choice item design](https://neurolaunch.com/forced-choice-questions-psychology/);
  [Thurstonian IRT forced-choice inventory development, *Behav. Sci.* 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs14121118);
  [normative vs ipsative selection](https://www.bryq.com/blog/likert-scale-vs-forced-choice-for-effective-employee-selection)).

A question that passes this brief asks *who ye are when it costs ye* — never what ye like, never
what ye do on a Saturday.

## 1. What makes Dostoyevsky's moral scenes work

### 1.1 The seven devices

**The confession.** Marmeladov, five days drunk, seizes a stranger in a tavern and narrates his
own degradation — the sold stockings, Sonya's yellow ticket — not to be helped but to be *heard*
(*Crime and Punishment*, Part I, ch. 2). Stavrogin brings Tikhon a printed confession of the worst
act in Dostoevsky, and what he cannot bear is not the guilt but that Tikhon foresees he will
confess *for the wrong reason* — to be admired for confessing (*Demons*, the suppressed chapter
"At Tikhon's"). The device: **a confession is a transfer of weight.** The listener becomes the
scene's protagonist; the moral question is never "was the confessor wrong?" (obviously yes) but
"what do you now do with what you carry?" Nothing sorts people faster.

**The temptation.** Raskolnikov, already sick with his idea, overhears a student in a tavern make
the whole utilitarian case for killing the pawnbroker — thousands helped, one "louse" removed —
and is shaken because the argument arrives *from outside*, in someone else's mouth (*C&P*, Part I,
ch. 6). Dostoevskian temptation is never a devil with horns; it is a *reasonable voice* plus an
*open door* plus a *clock*. The victim supplies the rationalisation himself; the scene merely
declines to stop him.

**The trial.** Book XII of *The Brothers Karamazov* is titled "A Judicial Error": every fact
presented at Mitya's trial is individually true-ish and the verdict is wrong. The rhetoric of
prosecutor and defender are both brilliant and both beside the point. Compare the miniature trial
when Luzhin plants a hundred-rouble note on Sonya and "proves" her a thief before a room of
mourners — undone only because one honest witness happened to be watching (*C&P*, Part V, ch. 3).
The device: **judgment under incomplete evidence, with a crowd that has already decided.** What it
reveals is how a person ranks law, mercy, truth, and their own safety when these split apart.

**The unearned gift.** Grushenka's fable of the onion: a wicked woman's guardian angel finds one
good deed — she once gave a beggar an onion — and it is almost enough to pull her out of the lake
of fire, until she kicks at the other sinners clinging to her, and the onion breaks (*BK*, Book
VII, ch. 3). Myshkin and Rogozhin, rivals to the death, exchange crosses as brothers (*The Idiot*,
Part II, ch. 4). Grace in Dostoevsky is always *disproportionate* and slightly *humiliating*: it
cannot be repaid, and the receiver's response to that impossibility — acceptance, repayment,
resentment, transmission — is a complete portrait of the soul
(cf. [Rowan Williams, *Dostoevsky: Language, Faith and Fiction*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Williams), 2008, ch. 1
on grace and exchange `[verify chapter]`).

**The Grand Inquisitor's challenge.** The single most important structural lesson in the corpus
(*BK*, Book V, ch. 5). The Inquisitor is not a strawman: he loves mankind, his indictment of
freedom is the strongest speech in the novel, and Dostoevsky *gives the devil the best lines* —
bread, miracle, mystery, authority, versus a freedom most people experience as abandonment. And
the answer is not a counter-argument. It is a kiss. The device: **an eloquent, kind antagonist
offering a genuinely good deal — comfort, order, certainty — priced in freedom or truth.** The
respondent cannot refute him; they can only answer him *differently*. Any quiz option that makes
the tempter stupid has failed this scene.

**Shame vs guilt.** Guilt is a debt (private, owed, payable); shame is an exposure (public,
seen, unpayable). Dostoevsky splits them constantly: Raskolnikov can bear guilt for months but
nearly dies of the *shame* of kissing the earth at the Haymarket crossroads (*C&P*, Part VI,
ch. 8); Captain Snegiryov *needs* the two hundred roubles Alyosha offers, and grinds the notes
under his heel because taking them before a witness would price his honour (*BK*, Book IV, ch. 7);
Nastasya Filippovna orchestrates her own public shaming rather than let others hold the whip
(*The Idiot*, Part I). See [Deborah Martinsen, *Surprised by Shame: Dostoevsky's Liars and
Narrative Exposure*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv17vf5vw) (Ohio State UP, 2003). For the
quiz: scenes where **a private thing becomes public in a crowded room** sort people by what they
protect — the person, the truth, the order of the room, or their own skin.

**The double.** Golyadkin meets a second Golyadkin who succeeds by every shameless means the
first only dreamed of (*The Double*, 1846). Svidrigailov keeps telling Raskolnikov they are
berries off the same field (*C&P*, Part IV, ch. 1). Smerdyakov, at the third interview, hands
Ivan the money and the sentence: you wanted it, I was only the instrument (*BK*, Book XI, ch. 8).
The device: **you meet someone doing what you secretly wish or fear you would do.** Condemning
them is condemning yourself; helping them is confessing kinship. René Girard reads the whole
corpus through this mimetic mirror
([Girard, *Resurrection from the Underground: Feodor Dostoevsky*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Girard), 1996).
It is the most surgical self-knowledge probe available to a quiz.

### 1.2 The engine underneath (five mechanics to steal whole)

1. **Polyphony — no thumb on the scale.** Ideas exist only as *persons*; every position gets a
   fully voiced advocate; the author refuses to adjudicate
   ([Bakhtin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problems_of_Dostoevsky%27s_Poetics)). Quiz translation:
   every option is written *from inside* by someone who believes it.
2. **The threshold.** Bakhtin observes that Dostoevsky's decisive scenes happen in doorways,
   stairways, entrances, public squares — places of crisis and turning, never in the settled
   biographical time of drawing rooms ([Bakhtin, ch. 4 on the threshold chronotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problems_of_Dostoevsky%27s_Poetics)).
   Quiz translation: catch the reader *mid-crossing* — at a door, a deathbed, a fire, a court
   morning — never "in general."
3. **The scandal scene.** The carnivalised gathering where decorum detonates — Nastasya's
   birthday party ending with a hundred thousand roubles in the fire and Ganya fainting rather
   than snatching them out (*The Idiot*, Part I, ch. 16); Katerina Ivanovna's funeral dinner
   collapsing into accusation (*C&P*, Part V). All masks slip at once; where you *stand* in the
   room is who you are.
4. **Money as moral instrument.** Dostoevsky prices everything exactly — the onion, the hundred
   roubles, the hundred thousand, the trampled notes. A sum is a scalpel: it makes a value
   conflict *measurable* without making it abstract. The quiz should use concrete stakes (siller,
   keys, papers, a name on a bond) the same way.
5. **Unfinalizability.** No person is ever finished, summed, or fully explained — Bakhtin's
   deepest claim about Dostoevsky's people, and Morson's about his openness to genuine
   alternatives in time ([Morson, *Narrative and Freedom: The Shadows of Time*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Saul_Morson), 1994).
   Quiz translation: **the quiz never tells the reader what their answer meant.** No mid-quiz
   verdicts, no "you chose compassion!" toasts. The reader must leave feeling *seen, not
   sentenced*.

One biographical fact worth keeping in the authors' room: Dostoevsky stood before a firing squad
on Semyonovsky Square in December 1849, was reprieved with minutes to spare, and wrote about
choice and irreversibility for the rest of his life as a man who had once counted his last
minutes ([Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: A Writer in His Time*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691155999/dostoevsky), Princeton UP, 2010).
That is the register: choices in this quiz are small, but they must *feel* countable.

## 2. What makes a great D&D prompt work

1. **Second person, present tense, sensory-concrete.** The DM's half of the play loop is a
   describable place with things in it, addressed to *you, now*
   ([D&D Basic Rules, "How to Play"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/basic-rules-2014)). No
   great prompt begins "Imagine that hypothetically…".
2. **Situations, not plots.** Prep the pressure and the actors' wants; never prep the outcome.
   The moment a scenario has an intended answer, the player's choice is fake
   ([The Alexandrian, "Don't Prep Plots"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots);
   [—, "Tools, Not Contingencies"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/37422/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots-tools-not-contingencies)).
3. **Meaningful choice = informed + consequential + irreversible.** The player must see the
   stakes *inside the prompt* (no gotchas), the options must lead somewhere genuinely different,
   and there is no reload. A choice with a take-back is a preference survey.
4. **No obviously-correct option.** The GM-craft consensus, evangelised across Matt Colville's
   *Running the Game* series: a dilemma only functions when reasonable players genuinely split,
   and each horn has a real constituency at the table
   ([Colville, *Running the Game*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Colville)). If the table
   would vote 9–1, it is not a dilemma; it is a quiz about whether you were paying attention.
5. **The cost lives in the option text.** "Save the villagers (but the bridge falls)" — the price
   is stated, not sprung. In our quiz every option carries its own visible bill.
6. **NPCs want things, and the tempter gets the best lines.** The offer that is easy to refuse
   reveals nothing (see the Grand Inquisitor, §1.1).
7. **Player skill is not character virtue.** D&D prompts fail when they can be *solved*; the
   moment system mastery beats disposition, you are sorting for cleverness. Our questions must
   have no optimal play — only self-portraits.

## 3. Laws of the question (the fusion)

Every question in the quiz obeys all ten. The acceptance gate (§8) operationalises them.

- **L1 — One scene, one threshold.** A single concrete place and moment of crossing; a face, an
  object, a clock.
- **L2 — Second person, present tense,** in the narrator's voice throughout.
- **L3 — The choice is irreversible** inside the fiction, and the prompt makes that felt.
- **L4 — Four options, four kinds.** Options differ in *kind of good pursued*, never in degree of
  goodness or competence.
- **L5 — Matched desirability.** Each option is an answer a serious person could be proud of;
  each carries a visible cost ([forced-choice design](https://neurolaunch.com/forced-choice-questions-psychology/)).
- **L6 — Give the devil the best lines.** Any tempter, briber, or accuser inside the scene is
  intelligent, kind where possible, and at least half right.
- **L7 — The narrator hauds his wheesht.** No adverb, epithet, or cadence may tip the scale
  toward an option; no post-choice moralising, ever.
- **L8 — No craft nouns, no hobby tells.** Anvils, looms, ovens, razors and their kin are banned
  from prompts and options alike; the mapping from answer to craft is invisible scoring, done
  later, one-to-many.
- **L9 — Suffering must argue.** Hard things may happen in scenes only when the choice requires
  them (Ivan's dossier of cruelties exists to force a question — *BK*, Book V, ch. 4 "Rebellion";
  it is never décor).
- **L10 — No verdicts.** The quiz never explains an answer back to the reader (unfinalizability,
  §1.2.5).

## 4. The question forms

Eleven named patterns. Each entry: root, shape (three beats), what the options must span, a
skeleton in narrator voice, and what it sorts. The authoring session should spread the quiz
across forms — **no more than two questions per form**, and at least seven forms represented.

### F1. The Confession
- **Root:** Marmeladov (*C&P* I.2); Stavrogin at Tikhon's (*Demons*).
- **Shape:** (1) someone of no advantage to ye chooses *ye* and lays down a weight; (2) the
  confession quietly implicates or tempts ye — knowing it changes yer standing to someone; (3)
  the confessor asks for *nothing*, which is the trap.
- **Options must span:** compel truth-telling · quiet practical repair · watchful custody of the
  secret · silent carrying of it.
- **Skeleton:** *"A man ye scarcely ken speaks low by the fire, and tells ye a thing that canna
  be untellt. He asks ye for naething. The morn he will walk the toun as ever, and only ye will
  ken. What do ye do wi' what he has gien ye?"*
- **It sorts:** what a person believes *hearing* is for — repair, justice, guardianship, or
  witness.

### F2. The Unearned Gift
- **Root:** the onion (*BK* VII.3); the exchange of crosses (*The Idiot* II.4).
- **Shape:** (1) ye receive something disproportionate, unasked, from someone who gains nothing;
  (2) it cannot be repaid in kind; (3) the giver is gone or refuses thanks.
- **Options must span:** plain acceptance · immediate repayment-in-another-coin (refusing
  indebtedness) · passing it onward · wary distrust of gifts and their hooks.
- **Skeleton:** *"It is mair than ye need and far mair than ye earned, and the giver will no' be
  found to be thankit. What do ye do wi' a thing ye canna pay for?"*
- **It sorts:** a person's relationship to debt, grace, and pride.

### F3. The Temptation at the Threshold
- **Root:** the tavern utilitarians (*C&P* I.6); Bakhtin's threshold chronotope.
- **Shape:** (1) an open door — an easy, deniable, unwitnessed wrong (or shortcut); (2) a
  reasonable voice, possibly yer ain, making the strong case for it; (3) a clock: the door closes
  the nicht.
- **Options must span:** refuse and pay the visible price · refuse and *close the door for
  others too* · take a lawful half-measure that keeps the advantage · walk away without deciding
  (which is itself a decision the prompt must price).
- **Skeleton:** *"Naebody watches. Naebody wad ever ken. And the argument for it, mind, is no' a
  bad argument. Ye have till the bell."*
- **It sorts:** what restraint is made of — fear, principle, solidarity, or habit.

### F4. The Trial
- **Root:** "A Judicial Error" (*BK* XII); Luzhin framing Sonya (*C&P* V.3).
- **Shape:** (1) ye must judge — formally or informally — with evidence that is strong but not
  whole; (2) the crowd has already decided; (3) delay, mercy, and rigour each have a stated
  price.
- **Options must span:** verdict by the letter · mercy against the evidence · costly delay for
  fuller truth · refusal to judge at all (with its abandonment named).
- **Skeleton:** *"The proofs sit before ye, and they are guid proofs — only no' the whole. The
  room kens its answer already. Yours is the mouth it waits on."*
- **It sorts:** the ranking of law, mercy, truth, and self-protection when they split.

### F5. The Inquisitor's Bargain
- **Root:** *BK* V.5 — and the discipline that the answer to the Inquisitor is a kiss, not a
  rebuttal.
- **Shape:** (1) a kind, intelligent, *genuinely benevolent* figure offers comfort, order, or
  certainty; (2) the price is freedom, truth, or conscience — stated plainly, not hidden; (3) his
  case is strong enough that the prompt concedes ye canna call him wrang.
- **Options must span:** take the bargain sincerely (order as mercy) · refuse publicly (witness)
  · refuse privately (integrity without crusade) · take it intending to turn it (power to redeem
  power).
- **Skeleton:** *"He is no' a wicked man; that is the deil o' it. He offers bread, quiet, and a
  smooth road, and asks but the ae thing for it. And lookin' at the toun, ye canna say he's
  wrang."*
- **It sorts:** what a person will pay for certainty — and whether they fight systems from
  inside, outside, or not at all.

### F6. The Double
- **Root:** *The Double*; Svidrigailov's "same field" needling (*C&P* IV.1); Smerdyakov's third
  interview (*BK* XI.8).
- **Shape:** (1) ye meet someone whose act is the thing ye have only *wanted*, or feared ye
  would do; (2) they claim kinship with ye — plausibly; (3) their fate now passes through yer
  hands.
- **Options must span:** condemn (and accept what that says of ye) · help (and accept the
  kinship) · hold them at arm's length while studying yersel · name the kinship aloud and act
  *with* them toward repair.
- **Skeleton:** *"He says: you and me, we are berries aff the ae field. And the warst o' it is
  no' that he's lying."*
- **It sorts:** self-knowledge against self-protection.

### F7. The Public Shaming
- **Root:** the scandal scenes — Nastasya's party (*The Idiot* I.16), the funeral dinner (*C&P*
  V); shame-as-exposure per [Martinsen](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv17vf5vw).
- **Shape:** (1) a crowded room; (2) a private thing made suddenly public — yours or another's;
  (3) the room turns, and there are perhaps three seconds in which where ye *stand* will be
  remembered forever.
- **Options must span:** stand beside the shamed (share the exposure) · restore the order of the
  room · say the exact truth aloud whatever it shames · draw the fire onto yersel.
- **Skeleton:** *"The room gaes quiet the way a room does when it has been gien a body to eat.
  Ye have the space o' a breath."*
- **It sorts:** what a person protects when dignity collapses in public — the person, the truth,
  the peace, or the honour of the house.

### F8. The Wager on a Soul
- **Root:** Myshkin's whole method (*The Idiot*); Alyosha sent into the world by Zosima (*BK*
  VI); Dmitri trusted against all evidence.
- **Shape:** (1) someone with a bad record asks ye to stake something real on their word —
  name, siller, caution before the court; (2) the record says no; something unarguable in ye
  says maybe; (3) nobody else will do it, and refusing is safe and blameless.
- **Options must span:** full stake as an act of grace · partial stake with the truth attached ·
  presence without underwriting (walk beside them, sign nothing) · principled refusal as respect
  ("carry yer ain weight the whole way once").
- **Skeleton:** *"He has broken his word twice, ance to ye. He says he is changed. Men say that.
  But he said it lookin' ye in the ee, and something in ye was persuadit. Yer wife was not."*
- **It sorts:** whether trust is an act, a contract, a companionship, or a discipline.

### F9. The Fire
- **Root:** a hundred thousand roubles in the flames while Ganya faints (*The Idiot* I.16).
- **Shape:** (1) irreversible destruction already in progress — fire, flood, a sinking boat, a
  closing gate; (2) ye can save exactly one armful; (3) the candidates are priced in different
  currencies: survival, rights, memory, or a gamble at the whole.
- **Options must span:** the practical (siller, bread) · the rightful (papers, proofs) · the
  irreplaceable (the picture, the letters) · the gamble that risks all to save all — or the
  person over every thing.
- **Skeleton:** *"The stair will stand for ae minute mair — time for ae armfu', nae mair. Choose,
  and be choosin' the rest o' yer life what ye left."*
- **It sorts:** the private exchange-rate between provision, justice, memory, and hope.

### F10. The Onion
- **Root:** *BK* VII.3, entire.
- **Shape:** (1) a very small good deed — yours or another's — is placed on the scale against a
  large account; (2) the question is what the small deed is *worth* and what claiming credit does
  to it; (3) someone is watching who will learn from what ye do.
- **Options must span:** quiet repeated smallness · the systematic scheme that helps more and
  warms less · the grand public gesture · refusal of the whole ledger ("guid deeds are no' for
  counting").
- **Skeleton:** *"It was only the ae onion, mind. But it was hers, and she gied it. Now: whit's
  it worth, and wha's to say?"*
- **It sorts:** scale-of-goodness intuitions — the wee and warm against the wide and cold, and
  whether credit itself corrupts.

### F11. The Deathbed Charge
- **Root:** Markel's deathbed and Zosima's charge to Alyosha (*BK* VI); the promise at Ilyusha's
  stone (*BK*, Epilogue, ch. 3).
- **Shape:** (1) a dying or departing person binds ye with an instruction; (2) keeping it will
  cost ye and benefits them nothing — they will never know; (3) the instruction is *slightly
  wrong* — outdated, too hard, or unfair to ye — so that keeping it literally, keeping its
  spirit, and setting it down are all live options.
- **Options must span:** literal fidelity · faithful reinterpretation · honest renegotiation
  with the living who remain · release ("the dead ask what the dead needed").
- **Skeleton:** *"She had yer hand and she had yer word before ye weel kent ye'd gien it. She is
  by-hand now, and only ye ken what was promised, and only ye wad ken gin it were broken."*
- **It sorts:** fidelity when enforcement is gone — the deepest single probe of character the
  corpus offers.

## 5. The narrator: a 17th-century Scots voice

### 5.1 Who speaks

An auld clerk of the burgh — the voice of a man who has kept minute-books and poor-rolls since
before yer faither was breeked, who has watched three generations of souls come before the
gathered crafts to be weighed. Historically anchored: Glasgow's fourteen incorporated trades and
the Trades House were constituted by the **Letter of Guildry, 1605** (see the sibling dossier
[01-hammermen.md](./01-hammermen.md) and [Lumsden & Aitken, *History of the Hammermen of
Glasgow*, 1912](https://archive.org/stream/historyofhammerm00lums/historyofhammerm00lums_djvu.txt)),
so his furniture is the burgh of the 1600s: kirk and tolbooth, causey and close, common box and
poor-box, candle, ledger, bell, and plague-talk kept to a murmur. He is grave, wry, watchful, and
entirely without contempt. He speirs at yer soul, no' yer CV.

### 5.2 Register and cadence

- **Cadence over costume.** The music is kirk-session minute-book crossed with the Authorised
  Version: long clause, short verdict. *"He asks ye for naething. That is the trap."*
- **The narrator never judges.** He reports what he has seen folk do, sets the scene, and hauds
  his wheesht. Wryness is permitted; winking is not. He is never ironic *about the reader*.
- **End on the choice.** Every prompt's last line is the question or the closing door — never
  commentary.
- **Gravity without gloom.** He has buried friends and still likes his dinner. At least a third
  of the scenes should contain warmth, humour, or plain human daftness; Dostoevsky's rooms are
  full of laughter half a page before the knife.

### 5.3 The Scots dial — seasoning, not submersion

Genuine 17th-century Scots orthography (*quha*, *quhilk*, *scho*, *ane* for "one") is documented
in the Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue, which covers Scots to 1700
([Dictionaries of the Scots Language / DOST](https://dsl.ac.uk/); e.g. the entry for
[*quha*](https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/quha); [Older Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Older_Scots)).
**We deliberately do not reconstruct it.** Full period spelling blocks comprehension and reads as
a philology exam. The rule is: **authentic world and cadence, lightly modernised spelling** —
Scots as seasoning a modern reader can taste at reading speed. Target roughly one Scots lexical
item per sentence, rarely three; every sentence must parse first time when read aloud.

**Tier 1 — free use (guessable from context):** ye, yer, yersel, ken/kent, nae, aye, no' , wee,
auld, guid, gang/gaed, awa, sic, mair, maun, gie/gien, tak, mak, hame, ain, folk, the morn, the
nicht, richt, wrang, sair, canny, douce, thrawn, greet (weep), fash, mind (remember), speir
(ask), haud, wheesht, bairn, ee/een, ance, aff, o', wi'.

**Tier 2 — sparing use, context must carry it (at most one per question):** siller (money), kist
(chest), howff (tavern), causey (street), close (alley), tolbooth, burgh, kirk-session, gin (if),
syne (since/ago), by-hand (over, done), breeked (put into trousers), stoup, mortcloth, caution
(surety, legal), deacon, poor-box, weedow.

**Grammar kit:** *maun* for must; *dinna/canna/wadna/didna*; *-it* past tense sparingly
(*persuadit*, *grantit*); *yon/thae* demonstratives; *ae* for a single one of.

### 5.4 Banned from the narrator's mouth

- **Anachronisms:** okay, fine, career, job, boss, percent, seconds/minutes as precise units,
  psychology-speak (values, empathy, boundaries, process), and any metaphor from machinery later
  than a mill.
- **Tartanry:** och aye, bonnie wee lassie, braw bricht moonlicht pastiche — the Brigadoon
  register. Also Burns pastiche; he is a century later and a different music.
- **Stage-English "olde" filler:** prithee, forsooth, mayhap, 'tis (use "it is" or restructure).
- **Sectarian bait:** the kirk is period furniture, not a scoring axis; piety is never rewarded
  or mocked by prompt or option (see X2).

## 6. Worked examples — form-pure, craft-free

Calibration standards. Note in each: matched option desirability, costs stated inside the option,
four *kinds*, zero narrator judgment, zero craft nouns. Disposition tags (in brackets) are for
authors' eyes; they never ship.

### W1 — The Confession (F1): *The man at the howff fire*

> Ye are late at the howff, and the fire is low. A man ye ken but little — a douce enough body,
> weel thocht of in the toun — sits doon aside ye and speaks low. Years syne, he says, he let his
> ain brither bear the blame for siller that he himsel took. The brither paid it, and never kent.
> The man is no' greetin, and he asks ye for naething. The morn he will gang aboot the toun as
> ever, and only ye will ken.
>
> What do ye do wi' what he has gien ye?

- **A.** Tell him plain: he maun tell his brither himsel — and when he does, ye will stand at his
  shoulder. *[repair through truth, shared cost]*
- **B.** Keep the secret and keep an ee on him. A man that confesses is halfway mended; yer
  watching will tell ye which half. *[custody, patience]*
- **C.** Say naething to him — but gang quiet-like and see what the brither lacks. A debt can be
  paid without the tale ever being telt. *[restitution over revelation]*
- **D.** Carry it, then and ever. It wasna gien ye to use; it was gien ye to hold. *[witness,
  silence as service]*

### W2 — The Fire (F9): *The fire in the close*

> A hoose in yer close is burning. The weedow that bides there stands safe on the causey, but a'
> she has is inside, and the stair will hold for ae minute mair — time for ae armfu', nae mair.
> In the front room: the kist wi' her siller and the papers that prove her richt to the hoose;
> on the wa', the ae picture of her man that was. And doon the close, neebors that might yet mak
> a line of buckets — gin somebody rallies them, and gin the stair waits, whilk it may not.
>
> The heat is on yer face. Whit's in yer arms when ye come oot?

- **A.** The kist — siller and papers baith. She maun eat, and she maun keep her hoose; grief
  can be grieved under a roof. *[provision and rights]*
- **B.** The picture. Siller can be gotten again, ae way or anither. Her man cannot. *[the
  irreplaceable]*
- **C.** Naething — ye are awa doon the close for the buckets, hazarding the whole to save the
  whole. *[the gamble, the rallying of others]*
- **D.** Naething — ye stand on the causey wi' her and watch it burn. A hoose is no' a life, and
  she shouldna stand alane while hers goes up. *[presence over property]*

### W3 — The Inquisitor's Bargain (F5): *The provost's offer*

> A great man of the burgh taks ye aside — kindly, weel-likit, and nae fool. He can mak yer road
> smooth, he says: wark, bread, a seat among decent men, and yer bairns set fair after ye. He
> asks but the ae thing: when he speaks in the council, ye haud yer tongue and vote as he votes.
> "Folk dinna want their freedom," he says, and no' unkindly. "They want their dinner, and quiet
> streets, and somebody else to carry the blame. I gie them a' three. Help me gie it them." And
> the deil of it is — looking at the toun, at the fed weans and the quiet streets — ye canna say
> he's wrang.
>
> Whit's yer answer?

- **A.** Tak his hand, and mean it. Bread and quiet *are* mercy, and pride never fed onybody.
  *[order as care, working within]*
- **B.** Refuse him to his face, in the open, whatever it costs ye. A smooth road ill-bought is
  dear at ony price. *[public witness]*
- **C.** Refuse him quiet-like, and keep yer ain sma' freedom — judging nae man that taks the
  bargain, for their bairns are hungrier than yours. *[integrity without crusade]*
- **D.** Tak his hand — and mean, some far day, to be the one that says whit the bargain buys.
  *[power kept to redeem power]*

### W4 — The Wager on a Soul (F8): *Caution for a broken man*

> A man that has twice broken his word — ance to his maister, ance to ye — stands before the
> burgh court the morn, and he wants ye to stand caution for him: yer name and yer siller set
> against his guid behaviour. Naebody else will do it, and naebody wad blame ye for refusing. He
> says he is changed. Men say that. But he said it looking ye in the ee, and something in ye —
> ye canna richt say whit — was persuadit. Yer wife was not.
>
> The morn comes. Whit do ye do?

- **A.** Stand caution for the whole, and tell him why: because somebody maun gang first, and it
  may as weel be ye. *[grace as act]*
- **B.** Stand for the half, and tell him plain whit the half withheld means, and whit wad earn
  the rest. *[trust with truth attached]*
- **C.** Sign naething — but walk wi' him to the court and stand at his side while he answers
  alane. *[presence without underwriting]*
- **D.** Refuse, and tell him to stop asking ithers: a man is only mended by carrying his ain
  weight the whole road once. *[severity as respect]*

## 7. Anti-patterns — banned

Each entry: the tell (how a reviewer detects it) and the fix. A question exhibiting any X-item is
returned to its author, not patched in review.

**X1 — The Hobby Question.** *"Whit wark pleases yer hands — the wood or the wool?"* Sorts by
taste, not soul; collapses the fiction into a careers quiz. **Tell:** the question could appear
on a job-matching site with the Scots removed. **Fix:** sort by disposition under pressure; if a
material must appear, it appears as stakes, never as preference.

**X2 — The Halo Option.** One option is The Good Person and everyone knows it. Social
desirability eats the signal — the precise failure forced-choice design exists to prevent
([matched-desirability pairing](https://neurolaunch.com/forced-choice-questions-psychology/);
[TIRT forced-choice development](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs14121118)). **Tell:** read the four
options to five people; if four pick the same one, it glows. **Fix:** price the halo (its cost
stated in-text) or split its virtue across two options.

**X3 — Visibly Unequal Attractiveness.** A dominant option, or a dud that exists to be scenery.
Kin of X2 but includes competence gaps: one option is simply *smarter*. **Tell:** the authors
cannot argue for an option with a straight face for sixty seconds. **Fix:** the Sixty-Second
Rule — every option gets a spoken defence in the writers' room before the question ships (this
is L5 enforced socially).

**X4 — The Costless Choice.** Nothing is irreversible; the choice is a vibe. Violates the core
of meaningful choice ([Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/4147/roleplaying-games/dont-prep-plots);
§2.3). **Tell:** you can append "…and later ye changed yer mind" to the scene without absurdity.
**Fix:** add the clock, the closing door, the witness who will remember.

**X5 — The Trolley Abstraction.** Disembodied hypotheticals — five strangers, a lever, no smell
of tallow. Dostoevsky never argues in the abstract; even Ivan's rebellion is built from *named,
documented* cruelties (*BK* V.4). **Tell:** no proper place, no object, no face. **Fix:** one
close, one kist, one weedow; concrete sums, concrete papers.

**X6 — The Moralising Narrator.** The voice tips the scale: "ye *bravely* refuse," "the *easy*
road." **Tell:** the adverb audit — strip every adverb and evaluative adjective from options and
prompt; if the meaning shifts, it was leaning. **Fix:** the narrator reports, prices, and speirs;
he never grades (L7).

**X7 — Sorting-Hat Telegraphy.** Options march in a fixed craft order, or wear craft costumes,
so readers metagame their way to the badge they want. **Tell:** a reader who has seen three
questions can predict the mapping of position → outcome. **Fix:** one-to-many scoring (every
option feeds several crafts at different weights), rotated option orders, and L8's ban on craft
nouns.

**X8 — The Explained Moral.** Mid-quiz feedback: "You chose loyalty!" Kills unfinalizability —
the reader is sentenced, and every later answer becomes a performance
([Bakhtin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problems_of_Dostoevsky%27s_Poetics); L10). **Tell:** any
copy between questions that characterises the reader. **Fix:** delete it; the only verdict is the
final sorting, and even that describes the *craft*, not the reader's virtue.

**X9 — Purple Scots.** *Quhilk*-and-*scho* orthography, or dialect density that forces re-reading.
Authenticity theatre at comprehension's expense (§5.3;
[DOST](https://dsl.ac.uk/) is the pantry, not the costume). **Tell:** a modern reader stumbles
reading the prompt aloud first-pass. **Fix:** the read-aloud test; demote vocabulary to Tier 1;
keep the cadence, modernise the spelling.

**X10 — Tartanry and Anachronism.** Och-aye pastiche at one pole; "percent," "career," or a
psychology word at the other. Both puncture the world. **Tell:** the banned-word list (§5.4)
run as a lint pass over every draft. **Fix:** period furniture only; if a concept needs a modern
word, the scene is wrongly chosen.

**X11 — Misery Porn.** Cruelty as atmosphere — dead bairns for shock, four options that are four
flavours of gloom. In Dostoevsky suffering always *argues*; it exists to force the question, and
laughter lives on the same page. **Tell:** the hard detail could be removed and the choice would
survive intact. **Fix:** L9 — suffering only where the choice requires it; and the warmth quota
(§5.2) across the whole quiz.

**X12 — The False Binary (and the Secret Third Thing).** Two options where the reader's true
answer is a third; or four options that are really two positions wearing coats. **Tell:** pilot
readers say "none of these" or "A and C are the same." **Fix:** the four-kinds discipline (L4) —
map each option to a distinct verb (repair / witness / provide / gamble…) before writing prose;
pilot every question on at least five readers before ship.

## 8. The acceptance gate

A question ships only when all twelve boxes tick. (This is the review checklist; L-numbers refer
to §3, X-numbers to §7.)

1. ☐ One scene, one threshold, one clock; a place, an object, a face (L1, X5).
2. ☐ Second person, present tense, narrator's voice end to end (L2).
3. ☐ The irreversibility is *felt* inside the prompt (L3, X4).
4. ☐ Exactly four options; each maps to a distinct disposition-verb agreed before prose was
   written (L4, X12).
5. ☐ Sixty-Second Rule passed: every option defended aloud in the writers' room (L5, X3).
6. ☐ Every option's cost is stated inside the option (L5).
7. ☐ Any tempter or accuser in-scene is intelligent and at least half right (L6).
8. ☐ Adverb audit passed; no narrator lean, no post-choice commentary (L7, X6, X8).
9. ☐ No craft nouns, no hobby framing; scoring is one-to-many and invisible (L8, X1, X7).
10. ☐ Read-aloud test passed by a modern reader at first pass; Tier-2 Scots ≤ 1; banned-word
    lint clean (§5.3–5.4, X9, X10).
11. ☐ Any suffering in the scene is load-bearing for the choice (L9, X11).
12. ☐ Piloted on ≥ 5 readers: no option drew > 45% or < 10%, and nobody said "none of these"
    (X2, X12) — thresholds provisional, to be tuned in the authoring session.

Quiz-level checks: ≥ 7 of the 11 forms represented; ≤ 2 questions per form; ≥ ⅓ of scenes
containing warmth or humour; option-position rotation verified.
