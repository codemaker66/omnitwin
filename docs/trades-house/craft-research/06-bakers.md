# Incorporation of Bakers

> Craft #6 of the fourteen Incorporations of the Trades House of Glasgow (Letter of Guildry, 1605).
> Research dossier for the sorting-quiz identity programme. Primary spine: the Incorporation's own
> history, *The Incorporation of Bakers of Glasgow* (Ness, 1891; rev. ed. 1931), digitized by the
> Trades House Digital Library, cross-checked against burgh records and Trades House sources.
> Anything not traceable to a source is tagged **[extrapolated]**.

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Bakers are the craft of the daily covenant: the town must be fed, every day, at an honest
weight, and heaven help the man who breaks that trust — or the magistrate who presumes to define
it for them. Alone among Glasgow's fourteen crafts they did not merely regulate a trade, they
*ran a business as a body* — grinding the city's wheat at their own mills on the Kelvin for over
three centuries, a privilege won in a single audacious act of catering: baking bread for the Regent
Moray's army in the tense days before Langside, 1568. Their soul lives in the balance held between
three wheat sheaves on their arms, under an irradiated eye of Justice and a hand descending from
clouds: provision is sacred, weight is truth, and the miller's wheel — like the oven — must turn
in fair rotation for every brother. They fed the poor with confiscated loaves, jailed rather than
signed bonds dictating their craft, fought forty-year lawsuits over a ladleful of grain, and kept
a regiment of cats on the payroll. Their motto is the tradesman's grace said over every batch:
*Praise God for all*.

## History

- **Origins before record.** The exact founding date is unknown; the Incorporation "existed long
  prior to its first official mention in 1556" ([Trades House — Bakers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).
  The original Letter of Deaconry (Seal of Cause) is lost — tradition says it burned in the Great
  Fire of Glasgow that began 17 June 1652 and destroyed about a third of the city
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
  The burgh records prove the letter existed: on 8 May 1641 the council ordered Deacon Thomas Scot
  "to produce the lettere of deaconheid of the baxters" ([1931 history, burgh extracts](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **6 October 1556 — the Act of Council.** The earliest surviving title: the Provost, Bailies and
  Council granted "the baxteris of glasgw" three market days a week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday)
  for bringing their bread to the Cross, barred out-of-town bread except on those days, and made
  the Deacon of the Baxters the enforcement officer "under the bailie"
  ([British History Online, Glasgow charters](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27);
  full text with modern rendering in the [1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
  The 1931 clerks thought this Act "very probably" a clause of the lost original Letter of
  Deaconry, extracted under the burgh seal. The same year the craft was assessed for Queen Mary's
  taxation, with David Landles, Thomas Scott and William Heriot appointed "Stentaris" (assessors).
- **1568 — the Wheat Mill of Partick.** After the Bakers provisioned the Regent Moray's troops
  camped at Langside before the battle of 13 May 1568, the grateful Regent granted them the
  "Ancient Quheite Mill of Partick" (the Archbishop's Mill, later the Bunhouse Mill) — probably
  as twenty-six shares called "mill-days," each holder entitled in turn to a day's grinding
  ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/); [1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
  Title was regularized by charters of 1587/1588/1591 (Walter Stewart, Commendator of Blantyre;
  feu-duty £23 Scots) and again on 26 May 1665 by Archbishop Alexander Burnet after the fire of
  1652 "burned and destroyed" the old writs.
- **1605 — Letter of Guildry.** The Bakers became one of the fourteen incorporated crafts under
  the Trades House; section 28 regulated unfreemen selling whitebread ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
  The roll of Deacons runs unbroken from George Young, 1604 ([1931 history, deacons list](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **The milling centuries (1556–1884).** The craft bought and built more mills — a second wheat
  mill in 1653 (over Blythswood's objection, seen off by interdict), the Clayslap Mills in 1771
  (making the Deacon "Head Miller on the Kelvin"), plus stores, barns, a change-house, and the
  celebrated Bun and Yill House tavern that named the Bunhouse Mill
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf);
  [TheGlasgowStory — Bunhouse Mill](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/image/?inum=TGSA01176)).
  This made them "the only Incorporation which actually carried on business institutionally in
  the city" ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).
- **Privilege defended, privilege lost.** With the seal of cause gone, the craft enforced its
  monopoly on baking and selling bread within the city by prescriptive right — winning interdict
  in 1803–06 against John Wotherspoon and the Grahamston Baking Company, who sold outside-baked
  bread within the Royalty ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
  The Act 9 Vict. c.17 (14 May 1846) abolished all exclusive trading privileges in Scottish burghs.
- **1884–1886 — the end of the mills.** Roller milling made the craft's millstones obsolete
  (profit of £2,046 in 1862–63 collapsed to a loss by 1883–84); the mills were let in August 1884,
  then burned on Saturday 6 February 1886 (£20,000 of damage). The site was feued to ex-Lord
  Provost John Ure — five generations of his family had been members — whose "Regent Mills"
  passed to the Scottish Co-operative Wholesale Society in 1903
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf);
  [Mills Archive — Bunhouse Flour Mills](https://new.millsarchive.org/mills/index/?which=13338)).
  The Kelvingrove lands were sold piecemeal: 7¾ acres to the University of Glasgow in 1865
  (£17,389 7s.) and the recreation-ground block to the City in 1895 (£30,000) — the Kelvin Hall
  stands on it. The Incorporation remains feudal superior of the Regent Moray Street feus
  ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).
- **1923 — arms matriculated.** After questions about using an unregistered coat (in use since at
  least 1719), the arms were registered unaltered with the Lord Lyon on 20 December 1923
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf);
  [Trades House Digital Library — arms table](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
- **Today.** The craft elects four members to the Trades House, gives Christmas gifts to
  pensioners and prizes to baking students at City of Glasgow College, and keeps the Moray link
  alive by electing successive Earls of Moray honorary members
  ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).

## Legends & Episodes

**The bread before the battle (1568).** The founding legend. As Moray's army camped at Langside,
the Bakers "from motives no doubt weighty, and as events proved, judicious, made special exertions
to supply his troops with bread." After the victory that sent Queen Mary into exile, the Good
Regent gave them the Wheat Mill of Partick. The tradition was "carefully handed down" in a Scots
phrase the craft repeated for centuries: **"the Regent, he gi'ed us the mill."** The inside lid of
the Deacon's Box (1719) tells the tale in full — their "distinguishing valure in conjunction with
the citizans of Glasgow... in the battel fought and victory obtained at Langsyd... upon the 13th
day of May, 1568." The 1828 foundation plate of the rebuilt mill credits the grant to "their zeal
in the cause of the Protestant Reformation, and for their spirited and well-timed assistance."
In 1885 the craft subscribed £25 to the Langside Battlefield Memorial; in 1915 the 17th Earl of
Moray swore the member's oath *de fideli* and signed the roll
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The Great Fire (1652).** Beginning 17 June 1652, the fire consumed the Saltmarket, Trongate,
Gallowgate and Bridgegate — and, tradition says, the Bakers' original charter and most of their
records. The craft spent the next 150 years litigating with one hand tied behind its back,
proving by "use and wont" what parchment could no longer prove
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The Dam Wars (1569–1762).** Water was power, and the Bakers fought for every inch of it. 1569:
found "in the wrong" for damming water from Lyon's mill. 21 June 1656: the deacon and everyone
with him fined £50 each for heightening their dam "in contempt of the toune," the council sending
men that same afternoon to "*ding doune againe so muche of the said dame as is newlie highted*."
1697: the height fixed forever at twenty-four English inches, cut into a brass standard sealed by
the Dean of Gild — a copper double of which still lies in the Deacon's Box, inscribed "*Gadge of
the Height of the Baxters Milne-dam. Settled by Act of Councill, 7 Auguft, 1697*." And in 1762,
unbowed, the craft flatly minuted an "Act refusing to lower mill dam as required by Magistrates"
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The twelve baxters in the Tolbooth (1696).** The provost imprisoned twelve named bakers "upon
the accompt of makeing insufficient bread and for not furnishing the mercat," holding them until
they signed bonds to bake bread "*such as the bakers of Edinburgh doe*." They refused the bond.
The council cut the penalty from £100 to £50 and ordered them back to prison until they signed.
Forty years earlier the council had actually imported two Edinburgh baxters, publicly declaring
the Glasgow men "*wald never amend, to the great discredit of this burghe*" — and paid the
incomers' bakehouse rent. No humiliation in the craft's memory cut deeper than being told
Edinburgh baked better ([1931 history, burgh extracts](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The Ladle Plea (1574–1834).** The ladle was the burgh's tax on grain — literally a ladleful
per boll. In 1575 William Glen was convicted of "defrauding of the ladle" and, refusing to swear
how many pennies he'd taken, was fined nine ladlefuls of wheat. In 1694 Deacon James Muirhead
"in ane haughty maner refused" to pay and was jailed; he and six brethren then sued the
magistrates before the Privy Council, and the Principal of Glasgow College had to mediate the
peace. In 1783 the craft opened "the famous Ladle Plea" against the magistrates, funding it by a
farthing on every boll ground at their mills for thirty years; they lost in 1790, kept paying,
kept protesting — and outlived the tax, which the Council suspended in 1834
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The deacon at the booth door (1573–74).** Baxter Alexander Scott was convicted of physically
barring Deacon George Young from entering his booth "to exerce the statute anentis the weying of
his breid"; William Neilsoun likewise abused the deacon — and the deacon was himself fined "for
stryking of the said Williame Neilsoun." Days later Scott was fined again for setting the wheat
mill three times running and refusing to let the deacon grind. Bread inspection was a contact
sport ([1931 history, burgh extracts](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**Confiscated loaves on the council table (1656).** Bread "altogether wnsufficient" was laid
before the magistrates; the whole batch was ordered "*to be given to the poore*" and each baker
fined £5 — future offenders £10, split between the merchants' and crafts' hospitals, "by and
besyd the confiscatioune of the breid." Bad bread literally became charity
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The ban on buns (1641).** The council statute: "*the baxters baik no bunes to be sold, bot
onlie sufficient loaves to serve the toun and countrey, under the paine of fyve pund toties
quoties*." In a hungry century, fancy-work was outlawed; the oven owed the town necessities
first. (The craft got its revenge in place-names: its most famous property was the Bun and Yill
House tavern.) In 1730 the craft passed its own "Act against baking of ginshbread by strangers" —
gingerbread was worth defending by then
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**The regiment of cats.** The accounts record regular income from "Sundry Rat Tails" — a bounty
scheme at the mills — until a better solution was found: "*introducing a regiment of cats, and
the maintenance of these was provided for by an annual payment to the Head miller*." The same
accounts pay for pipes and tobacco for the men who hauled a new millstone, "Buns and ale" for the
mason lads building the storehouse "from first to last," an annual shilling for the officer "to
drink the King's health," and repeated expenses guarding, shaking, and prosecuting the breakers of
the storehouse pear tree ([1931 history, Collector's accounts](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**Feeding the city in dearth (1800–01).** During the famine years the Incorporation subscribed
£600 as a body — its individual members another £720 — "for purchasing supplies of Corn for the
City of Glasgow," and paid a 15% cess on it. In 1766 it had bound itself to bear a share of loss
on the Trades House scheme for "supplying the City of Glasgow with meal and grain"
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

**Dynasties of the oven.** John Ure — Deacon 1854 and 1857, Lord Provost — bought the burned
mill site in 1887, fifth generation of his family in the craft; the 1886 foundation-stone bottles
were re-deposited in his new Regent Mills. Sir William Bilsland (Hydepark Bakery, 230,000 loaves
a week by 1900) became Lord Provost in 1905; as Deacon in 1898 he paid to renew the Deacon's chain
and medal ([Wikipedia — William Bilsland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bilsland);
[1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
Alexander Ure, Deacon 1915, was by then **Lord Strathclyde**, Lord President of the Court of
Session. The craft gave the Trades House nine Deacon-Conveners between 1647 and 1917. Famous
baking families: Beattie, Stevenson, Montgomerie, Bilsland, McFarlane Lang, Peacock, Currie
([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).

**Politics of the loaf.** 1778: £200 voted "towards raising a battalion to assist in suppressing
the present unnatural rebellion in America," and the deacon authorized to petition against repeal
of the anti-Popery laws; 1825: paid to advertise a petition against Catholic Emancipation; 1784:
delegates sent to Edinburgh for burgh reform "on the ground that the same is unfavourable to the
political importance and liberty of the citizens at large." Establishment loyalists in war and
religion; radicals about their own representation
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Arms (Lyon Register, matriculated 20 December 1923, in use since at least 1719):**
  "*Gules, a hand paleways proper holding a balance between three garbs Or, on a chief barry of
  six Azure and Argent between two foul anchors Sable, the eye of Justice irradiated shining on
  clouds proper from which the hand descends; and in an Escrol above the shield this Motto —
  Praise God for all*" ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Motto:** **"Praise God for all"** — grace before meat, turned into a corporate identity;
  shared with the Worshipful Company of Bakers of London ("Praise God For All"), whose fellowship
  the Glasgow craft acknowledged by making the Lord Mayor of London an honorary freeman in 1901
  ([Worshipful Company of Bakers](https://bakers.co.uk/about-us/history/); [1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Symbolism.** The three garbs (wheat sheaves) are harvest and the honest reward of labour; the
  balance held by a hand descending from clouds under the irradiated eye of Justice makes the
  weighing of bread a divine audit — the assize of bread rendered in heraldry. The wavy blue-and-
  silver chief and the two fouled anchors recall the water that drove their mills and the
  waterborne grain trade on which the ladle dispute turned. **[interpretation — symbolism is not
  glossed in the patent]** In 1807, for the Barony Church galleries, the craft borrowed the
  Edinburgh Baxters' near-identical blazon (hand from a cloud, balance, three garbs) after
  comparing it with the arms painted on their 1719 box ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **The classical patroness.** The Deacon's Box of 1719 is painted with the motto *"Alma Ceres
  terrae cunctis alimenta ministrat"* — "kindly Ceres supplies nourishment to all the earth" —
  the goddess of grain presiding over a Presbyterian craft box
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Patron saint.** No Glasgow-specific patronal dedication survives in the sources consulted;
  the craft's own records begin only in 1556/1700. Elsewhere in pre-Reformation Scotland the
  baxters' patron was **St Obert** (Aubert of Cambrai), with altars in Edinburgh, St Andrews and
  Dundee, and a riotous torch-lit St Obert's Eve play kept by the Perth baxters each 10 December
  ([Folklore Society](https://folklore-society.com/blog-post/the-folklore-societys-advent-calendar-2024-part-2/)).
  It is reasonable to present St Obert as the ancestral patron of the Scottish baking craft
  **[extrapolated — no direct Glasgow evidence found]**. The craft's one documented sacral relic
  points instead to Glasgow's own saint: the Deacon's Chair (1905) is made of oak from the Abbey
  Church of Culross, "the birthplace of Saint Mungo, the patron saint of Glasgow"
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Rituals & Customs

- **The Essay.** Every entrant owed the Master Court a proving-piece, judged by appointed **Essay
  Masters**. In 1783, "to prevent partiality in fixing Essays," it was standardized as **a batch
  of loaves** — but the range had been the whole craft: one intake was examined on "beef-steak
  pies, veal pies, pigeon pies, pork pies, mutton pies, rabbit pies, apple tarts, plum tarts,
  pear tarts, frouches, custards, and souffles," submitted at a special meeting in the Trades
  Hall, where "the same were approved of." A man admitted without his essay could neither trade
  as a master, grind at the mills, vote, nor hold office "till he shall have made such Essay."
  Essays were finally dispensed with on 16 August 1872
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **The oath *de fideli*.** Sworn by every freeman, even honorary ones (Lord Provost Smith's 1824
  admission preserves it): to be faithful to the Incorporation, obey its lawful acts, and "*not
  pack nor peel with unfreeman nor cover unfreeman's goods with his own*" under £20 sterling
  penalty — no fronting for outsiders, ever. Members were bound to grind at the trade's own mills
  in rotation, on pain of double payment and a £5 fine — drought excepted, "by the Deacon's
  permission" ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Fines and the box.** Freedom fines on entry (£6 sterling in 1741; freemen's sons £3,
  sons-in-law £20 by 1786–88); yearly **Quarter Accounts** of one shilling at the Lammas Court for
  the poor of the trade; a "new upset" (£20 Scots) for insulting a brother at meetings; £63 Scots
  once exacted for "defaming the Deacon." Debtors to "the box or poor of the Trade" could neither
  vote nor hold office (rule of 1722)
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Discipline by the wheel.** The deacon could stop a member's grain at the mills until fines
  were paid — and was sued for it in 1737, the trade defending him. Even rank gave no exemption:
  in 1790 Deacon Galloway *and* the miller were fined 25s. each for grinding 100 bolls out of
  turn ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Apprentice rites, pruned.** Apprentices' entry treats were restricted in 1724 (fine £5 Scots
  on the prentice, £10 on the master) after "frequent abuses and extravagances"; the mysterious
  custom of **"apprentice headwashing"** — an initiation washing/dousing rite — was formally
  abolished in 1769; "No treats or drinking at the entry of freemen" (1741); even dinners at
  Partick on deacon's-election day were struck off the trade's expense in 1770
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Insignia.** The Deacon's gold chain and medal (1829/1834; Bakers' Arms on the obverse, City
  Arms on the reverse), renewed 1898 and formally re-invested on the Deacon by the oldest living
  ex-Deacon; the Collector's silver replica (1923); the Deacon's Box (1719, oak, painted arms and
  the Ceres motto, once guarded by rival "Boxmasters of the New Box" and "of the Old Box"); the
  Bell inscribed "Incorporation of Bakers, Glasgow, 1786," rung by the Deacon at meetings and
  dinners; two ram's-horn snuff mulls with hammer, piker, spoon, claut and hare-foot, their silver
  ribbons engraved with every Deacon's name since 1776 — when the Auld Horn filled up in 1869 a
  new one was procured, with the verse: "*The Auld Horn's cled wi' honour'd names, / Till
  Aughteen saxty-nine; / And I ha'e ta'en the record up / O' Deacons' names sin' syne*"
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **The Old Deacons' Club (founded February 1815).** "The House of Lords of the Incorporation,"
  confined to those who have "passed the Deacon's Chair." Its own historian writes: "As to its
  'mysteries,' it would be unwise to speak, still less to write." Its gavel is Glasgow Cathedral
  roof-oak ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Feasts and observances.** Lammas Court for dues; election of the Deacon each autumn before a
  bailie, with drummers and the town officers paid in drink-money, and expenses "Spent in Widow
  Finlayes at the Electione"; the officer paid yearly "as usual to drink the King's health" on the
  King's birthday; the craft's two seats in the Barony Kirk (Acts of 1692/1696 — permitted a new
  seat "providing always that they raise it no higher than the seat before it, to the prejudice
  of the hearing of them that sit behind"); a paid dinner at the Barony Sacrament; the annual
  **loch-visiting**, when the Master Court inspected the Kilmannan reservoir that watered their
  mills; and the **tail-shaking**, a distribution to "Sundry Poor" connected with the tails or
  sweepings of the flour ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Precedence.** Sixth of the fourteen crafts in the fixed order (Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners,
  Maltmen, Weavers, **Bakers**, Skinners, Wrights, Coopers, Fleshers, Masons, Gardeners, Barbers,
  Bonnetmakers & Dyers), the order settled after the "Grand Decerniture" of 28 February 1777
  ([Glasgow Trades Hall — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow_Trades_Hall);
  [Hammermen of Glasgow](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/our-history/); craft order as listed in
  the 1791 Trades Hall title, [1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Modern entry.** Near Hand (kin of members) £95; Far Hand £97; four representatives to the
  Trades House ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).

## Sensory World

Flour dust hanging in the light of a Kelvin-side mill; the grind and thunder of water-driven
stones, and the terror of friction — in 1754 miller William Watson had his wage docked from 1d to
¾d a load for letting the gudgeon of the nether mill run dry of "*creish and oyle* whereby the
axletree took fire." The cool dark of the **moulter chist**, its key kept by the Collector alone
(statute of 1703, £20 penalty). Grain measured in firlots and bolls against brass standards
sealed by the Dean of Gild; the copper dam-gauge; the iron gauge bolted mid-dam. The heat-wall of
the bakehouse — the craft met in the Deacon's own bakehouse in 1680 — the slap of dough, the
long-handled **peel** drawing loaves (the Journeymen's box of 1765 shows a baker with his peel and
another with a threshing flail, flanking three wheat sheaves and a set of scales —
[TheGlasgowStory](https://theglasgowstory.com/image/?inum=TGSE01127&t=2)). Penny loaves weighed to
the ounce and drop against the printed assize table — sixpenny, fourpenny, threepenny, twopenny,
"Seven Rolls for 3d." — and the "custed loavs... a litle spoiled in baking" passed cheap to
country carriers. The yeasty smell of **barm**, scarce enough in 1766 that the Deacon petitioned
the Justices to stop whisky distilling to save it for bread. Gingerbread "lettered bread" cakes
of seven ounces. Snuff passed at Master Court from ram's horns with silver appendages; pipes and
tobacco for millstone-haulers; ale in the Bun and Yill House where half the trade's business was
"considered and settled," booked as "Spent with the Deacon and Masters at a Meeting." Mill cats
underfoot. The bell of 1786. Rat tails counted out for the bounty
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Values

1. **The town must be fed — provision as covenant.** The 1556 Act ordered townsfolk "first
   servit" before any bread left for strangers; in 1696 twelve baxters were jailed not only for
   bad bread but "for not furnishing the mercat"; in 1800–01 the craft and its members put up
   £1,320 to buy corn for a starving city. Feeding Glasgow was a legal duty before it was a
   business ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
2. **Honest weight — truth you can put on a scale.** The deacon personally weighed bread at booth
   doors (1573–74); insufficient bread was confiscated for the poor (1656); the arms place a
   balance under the irradiated eye of Justice. The craft's whole moral vocabulary is gravimetric
   ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
3. **The well-timed act — courage read as catering.** Langside, 1568: bread delivered to an army
   before its victory, "from motives no doubt weighty, and as events proved, judicious." The craft
   prized decisiveness at the moment of maximum uncertainty, and dined out on it for 350 years
   ([Trades House](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-bakers/)).
4. **Fair rotation — nobody jumps the queue at the wheel.** The mill-day system gave each holder
   his day's grinding in turn; even a sitting Deacon was fined for grinding out of turn (1790).
   Order at the shared resource was sacred ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
5. **Stubborn self-rule — jail before a dictated bond.** The dam heightened in contempt of the
   town (1656); the ladle refused "in ane haughty maner" (1694); prison chosen over signing bonds
   on the magistrates' terms (1696); an "Act refusing to lower mill dam as required by
   Magistrates" (1762). The Bakers obeyed the assize of bread and almost nothing else
   ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
6. **The box and the brother — mutual aid without sentimentality.** A "destrest brother" given a
   load of multure wheat (1681) — against his signed promise "never to trobell the baxteres of
   glesgow any further"; mortifications for "three poor men above the age of forty"; pensions,
   prentice fees, widows' and daughters' rolls; £2,504 of £3,329 total expenditure in 1930 went
   straight to the pension roll ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
7. **Stewardship across generations.** Twenty-six mill-days in 1568 became mills, lands, feus, and
   a £108,533 stock by 1930; deacons' names engraved on silver ribbons "in all time coming"; the
   1828 foundation bottles re-buried in the 1887 mill. The craft thought in centuries
   ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Shadow

Every Baker virtue curdles into control of other people's bread. **Provision becomes monopoly:**
the same craft that fed the town prosecuted the Grahamston Baking Company for daring to sell
cheaper bread inside the Royalty (1803–06), taxed English flour £3 a hundredweight (1741), taxed
any member who ground elsewhere (1742), banned discounts (1771), banned even New Year's gifts to
customers — advertised in both newspapers (1782). **Honest weight becomes a price floor:** the Act
of 1731 punished not the man who baked light, but the man who sold full-weight penny loaves *too
cheap*, fining him for "the great prejudice of these members" — consumer protection inverted into
cartel discipline. **Stubbornness becomes discredit:** the council found their bread so bad, so
long, that it shipped in Edinburgh bakers and told the town the Glasgow men "wald never amend" —
the guild's pride in self-rule had rotted into complacency about the product itself. **Loyalty
becomes reaction:** money for suppressing the American rebellion (1778), petitions against
Catholic relief (1778, 1825). **Stewardship becomes rent:** by 1931 the craft of the oven owned no
oven — it had become a landlord and pension fund, feudal superior of streets named after its own
legend, its "trade" a dinner club whose mysteries "it would be unwise to speak" of. The staff of
life, gripped long enough, becomes a cudgel — and then a walking stick
([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Rivals & Kin

- **The Magistrates and Town Council — the eternal sparring partner.** The assize of bread, the
  ladle, the dam height, imprisonments, the "Ladle Plea": more of the craft's recorded history is
  litigation with the town than with anyone else. Yet the same council granted their market days
  and defended their market from strangers ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **The Edinburgh Baxters — the hated mirror.** Imported in 1656 to shame Glasgow's bread;
  invoked in the 1696 prison bonds ("such as the bakers of Edinburgh doe"); yet in 1807 Glasgow
  quietly borrowed Edinburgh's coat of arms for the Barony Church panel
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Rival millers on the Kelvin.** Archibald Lyon's mill upstream (decreet of 1569), the Bishop's
  Mill downstream (forced to lower its dam), Campbell of Blythswood (tried to stop the second
  mill, 1653; boundary "submission" 1758); Baillie Lyon's Plea over the widened lade (1748)
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Outentowners, suburban bakers and pretenders.** The Gorbals master whose apprentice was
  refused booking (1739); Gorbals freemen charged half again in multures (1773); processes against
  men "that had pretended to carry on the Trade within the Town" (1775); Wotherspoon and the
  Grahamston Baking Company (1803). The ladle fell only on town bakers — suburban rivals baked
  tax-free, which kept the wound open ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Their own journeymen.** 1787: a "combination" of journeymen refusing to engage for more than
  a week at a time — the accounts show the trade paying town officers "for apprehending
  Journeymen Bakers." The Society of Journeymen Bakers (1765) and the Friendly Society of Sons of
  Freemen Bakers (1793) organized in the craft's shadow
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf);
  [TheGlasgowStory](https://theglasgowstory.com/image/?inum=TGSE01127&t=2)).
- **Allies by errand.** The **Maltmen** — fellow grain-men — joined them against the ladle (1693)
  and split the Partick turnpike subscription (1755); the **Fleshers and Gardeners** stood with
  them on billeting soldiers (1774, 1782); the ten lesser-represented crafts (Bakers included)
  fought the four great ones for equal Trades House representation (1771–77) and lost; the
  **Tailors** were on the other side of an 1887 action over Corporation Duty
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).
- **Kin by marriage and blood.** Entry classes priced the family tree: freemen's sons £3,
  sons-in-law £20 (1786); marrying a baker's widow could admit an outsider — Robert Dunlop, 1774,
  on essay plus £100. Dynasties (Ure, Bilsland, Beattie, Scott, Muirhead) ran the craft for
  generations; the Earls of Moray are hereditary honorary kin
  ([1931 history](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/bakers_book_1931.pdf)).

## Lexicon

Scots/period terms a narrator can use, all attested in the sources above:

1. **baxter** — baker (the craft's own old name: "the Baxteris of Glasgow").
2. **batch** — one oven-round of loaves; after 1783, the standard entry essay.
3. **the Essay** — the proving-piece demanded of every entrant, judged by Essay Masters.
4. **mill-day** — a 1/26 share in the Wheat Mill, carrying the right to a day's grinding in turn.
5. **multure / moulter** — the toll of grain paid for grinding; kept in the **moulter chist**.
6. **firlot** and **boll** — dry measures of grain (a load of wheat = six firlots, 1723).
7. **the ladle** — the burgh tax of a ladleful of grain per boll; hence the "Ladle Plea."
8. **outentowner** — an out-of-town trader; the enemy at the Cross.
9. **unfreeman** — one who trades without freedom of the craft; to "pack nor peel" with him was
   oath-breaking.
10. **upset / new upset** — the entry-fee; also the standard fine (£20 Scots) for miscalling a
    brother, as if you had to buy your way in again.
11. **quarter accounts** — the yearly shilling owed to the poor's box at the Lammas Court.
12. **letter of deaconry / seal of cause** — the craft's founding charter, burned 1652.
13. **ding doune** — to knock down (what the council did to the heightened dam, 1656).
14. **toties quoties** — "as often as it happens": every offence, every fine, no limit.
15. **barm** — brewer's yeast; scarce when the distillers bought the grain (1766).
16. **custed loaves** — crust-damaged loaves sold cheap to carriers and tapsters.
17. **tapster / to tap** — to sell by retail; huxters were forbidden to "tap" strangers' bread.
18. **gingebread / lettered bread** — moulded gingerbread cakes with lettering, seven ounces each.
19. **peel** — the baker's long wooden paddle for drawing loaves from the oven.
20. **the tail-shaking** — the distribution connected with the tails (sweepings) of the flour,
    with money for "Sundry Poor" at it.

## Question Seeds

Dilemmas rooted in documented episodes; each names the value (or its shadow) a choice reveals.

1. **The Regent's order (1568).** An army camps at Langside tonight; the battle is tomorrow, and
   nobody knows who wins. Baking for the Regent feeds soldiers — and marks you forever as his
   man if the Queen prevails. The craft's own historian admits the motives were "no doubt weighty,
   and as events proved, judicious." *Do you fire the ovens?* — Choosing to bake reveals **the
   well-timed act** (decisive commitment under uncertainty); hesitating reveals prudence that
   history will never reward with a mill.
2. **The bond in the Tolbooth (1696).** You are one of the twelve in prison. Sign a bond to bake
   bread "such as the bakers of Edinburgh doe" — a true standard, and your bread *has* been poor —
   and you walk free tonight. Refuse, and you rot on principle: no magistrate dictates the craft.
   *Sign or rot?* — Signing reveals **honest weight** (the standard matters more than who sets
   it); refusing reveals **stubborn self-rule** (and its shadow: pride defending bad bread).
3. **The underseller (1731).** Your neighbour bakes full-weight, honest penny loaves — and sells
   a dozen under the fixed twelve shillings Scots. The trade's act fines him three shillings "for
   the use of the poor of the Trade." Reporting him protects your margin and funds the box;
   staying silent lets the poor buy cheaper bread. *Do you inform the Deacon?* — Informing
   reveals **fair rotation / solidarity** (the compact holds or nobody eats); silence reveals that
   you weigh the town's hunger above the craft's peace — the provision covenant against the
   cartel's shadow.
4. **The deacon at your booth door (1573).** The Deacon comes to weigh your bread. The batch is
   light — the wheat was dear, your children are hungrier than the statute. Alexander Scott barred
   the door and was fined; the loaves of others were seized and given to the poor. *Do you open
   the door?* — Opening reveals **honest weight** as submission to the scale even when it ruins
   you; barring it is the confession that your hunger outweighed your oath.
5. **Drought at the wheel (1746).** The Kelvin runs low; your mill-day is a week away and your
   ovens are cold. Stephen Rowan ground at a stranger's mill without the Deacon's leave and was
   fined half-a-crown; Deacon Galloway himself was fined for taking 100 bolls out of turn. *Do
   you wait your turn while your customers walk to the Gorbals?* — Waiting reveals **fair
   rotation** (order at the shared resource above personal ruin); grinding elsewhere reveals
   provision-at-any-price, the value that built the craft and breaks its rules.
6. **The purchased mercy (1711).** James Gray cannot pass the Essay — he is too short-sighted to
   judge his own crumb. The court admits him anyway for £60 Scots to the poor's box. You are an
   Essay Master: the fee feeds widows all winter, but every purchased freedom cheapens the batch
   you once sweated to prove. *Do you vote to admit?* — Admitting reveals **the box and the
   brother** (mercy and mutual aid above gatekeeping); refusing reveals the Essay as sacred
   standard — craft identity that cannot be bought, even for the poor.
