# Incorporation of Barbers

> Thirteenth in precedence of the fourteen Incorporated Crafts of the Trades House of Glasgow, ranked "between the Gardeners and Bonnetmakers" by Act of the Trades House, 1724. Formerly the Incorporation of Chirurgeons and Barbers — the only one of the fourteen founded by Royal Charter.

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Barber is the craft of trust at the throat. Every morning, half of Glasgow tilted its head back and bared its jugular to a man with a razor — and talked. The barber bled you, pulled your teeth, dressed your wig, and carried the town's news from chin to chin; he was the smallest and least lordly of the crafts, yoked for 120 years to surgeons who held him in contempt, and he beat them — not with the lancet but with the law, appeal after patient appeal, until the learned men "threw up the sponge" and cut themselves off, leaving the Barbers to inherit the charter, the box, and the name. It is the craft of the underdog who wins by showing up, of intimacy made into a livelihood, of gossip held in one hand and discretion in the other, practised — as its arms insist — *In the Presence of God*.

## History

**Before the charter.** Glasgow's barbers almost certainly met and elected a deacon informally long before they had any legal standing — the 1899 *Records* argue this by analogy with the Bonnetmakers, who were being summoned before the magistrates as an organised craft two years before their own Seal of Cause ([Records of the Incorporation of Barbers, Glasgow, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). In April 1599 the Town Council, prodded by the Kirk-Session, moved against "medicineris and chyrurgianes quha dayele resortis and remainis within this towne, and is not able to discharge thair dewtey" — quacks were killing people, and someone had to examine them ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).

**The Royal Charter, 29 November 1599.** "Maister" Peter Lowe — army surgeon, author, self-promoter — went over the Council's head straight to King James VI, who at Holyrood granted a Charter under the Privy Seal to Lowe, the physician Mr Robert Hamilton, and (for drug inspection) the apothecary William Spang: power to examine and license everyone practising "the said airt of Chirurgie" across Glasgow, Renfrew, Dumbarton, Lanarkshire, Kyle, Carrick and Cunninghame, to fine the unqualified forty pounds, to inspect every person "hurt, murtherit, poisonit, or onie other persoun tane awa extraordinary," and the duty to meet "every first Mononday of ilk moneth… to visite and give counsell to pure disaisit folkis gratis" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [RCPSG Heritage, The Charter of 1599](https://heritage.rcpsg.ac.uk/exhibits/show/maister-peter-lowe/the-charter-of-1599)). This same charter is the founding document of what is now the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Glasgow — the Barbers and the Royal College share a birth certificate. The Town Council endorsed it on 9 February 1600; the society was actually constituted at a meeting in Blackfriars Kirk on 3 June 1602, where Thomas Hamilton was chosen first Deacon and it was "statute and ordained, that Barbers shall pay at their admission Fortie pund Scots, and ilk year twentie shilling to the puir" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). It is the only one of Glasgow's fourteen crafts created by Royal Charter rather than by the town ([Trades House of Glasgow — Barbers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-barbers/)).

**The Letter of Guildry, 1605.** In the great Merchants-versus-Crafts arbitration that created the Trades House itself, the Chirurgeons punched far above their weight: two of the twelve Craft commissioners were theirs (Peter Lowe and Robert Hamilton), and Lowe's father-in-law, the Rev. David Weems, Dean of Glasgow, was one of the four oversmen who drew the Decree Arbitral. Lowe was then made first Master of the Trades Hospital — trusted with the money at a time when, as Lumsden notes, some deacons "could not even sign their own names" ([Lumsden, Traditions of the Trades House of Glasgow](https://electricscotland.com/history/medical/14Article10TraditionsOfTheTradeHousesOfGlasgow.pdf)).

**Seal of Cause, 16 August 1656.** Wanting the same local standing as the other thirteen trades, the "Chirurgianis and Barbouris" (Deacon John Hall) petitioned the Council and received a Letter of Deaconry/Seal of Cause — a document the Incorporation still preserved, "legible as when it left the hands of the Town Clerk," in 1899. It famously contains a clerical error dating the royal patent to "ane thousand five hundred ffyftie-nyne" (1559) — a year when James VI had not yet been born ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). Parliament ratified the royal gift on 11 September 1672 (Chap. 127 of Charles II's parliament, passed alongside the ratification of the Letter of Guildry) ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House of Glasgow — Barbers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-barbers/)).

**The long war with the Surgeons, 1657–1722.** The union "was not a peaceful one." Milestones: 1657, an apothecary elected Deacon; the Barbers appealed and had the election annulled. 1704, the Council ruled the Letter of Deaconry "the Standart and rule" against surgeon encroachments. 1707, a full arbitration settled offices — Deacon always a surgeon, Collector alternating "year about ane Chyrurgeon and Barber per vices," a barber and a surgeon box-master each, and, crucially, "the haill Barbers that are now unentered and Qualified to Enter shall have free access" — the clause that let barber numbers swell past the surgeons. 1708, a formal division of the joint stock: three-fifths to the surgeons' Faculty, two-fifths to the joint trade, the Trongate hall shared — and "the Bookes, Skeletone and all rarities now in the hall" reserved to the surgeons alone. 1719, deadlock: the dispute over freemen's sons' rights went against the surgeons at every level, Deacon John Gordon simply refused to convene the Incorporation and "keeped up" the key of the hall, and the Barbers met instead in the Tron Church under the Trades' Baillie. On 19 December 1719 the surgeons renounced the Letter of Deaconry entirely; on 22 September 1722 the Council accepted the renunciation and issued a new Letter of Deaconry erecting the 28 freemen Barbers as an independent Incorporation ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); the same sequence — 1719 renunciation, 1722 final split — is given by the [Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Glasgow's history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons_of_Glasgow)).

**Independence, 1722 onward.** First meeting as a separate body in the Trades' Hospital, 28 September 1722; Alexander Milne elected Deacon. Readmitted to the Trades House in October 1722 with two representatives, raised to three on petition a month later for £20 Scots yearly to the House's poor. In 1724 they fought the Masons over precedence and lost: ranked "immediatly before the Bonnetmakers To witt between the Gardeners and Bonnetmakers" — thirteenth of fourteen — "in all time comeing," a ruling confirmed by the Court of Session in 1777 ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). The 1726 Acts of the Trade fixed their working constitution (essays, Sabbath rules, wig standards — see Rituals). In 1732 the barbers and wig-makers of the village of Gorbals petitioned to be taken on as a "pendicle" (subordinate) incorporation, and were. The Act of Parliament of 1846 abolished the exclusive trading privileges of all Scottish incorporations; the Barbers survived it as a charitable and civic body, equalised pendicle and operative members in 1865, sold the Barbers' Close in the Saltmarket to the City Improvement Trust for £8,000 in 1867, and prospered — 138 members on the roll by 1899 ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). Today the Incorporation continues within the Trades House — supporting pensioners, awarding prizes to hairdressing students, and gifting books to the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons whose charter it shares ([Trades House Digital Library — Barber](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html); [Trades House of Glasgow — Barbers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-barbers/)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **Maister Peter Lowe, "a strange compound of mystery, audacity, and quackery."** The founder concealed his own birthplace, soldiered as an army chirurgeon abroad, styled himself surgeon to the King and "chief chirurgiane to oure dearest son the Prince," had already fallen foul of Kirk-Session discipline "for some offence not recorded," and published *An easie, certaine, and perfect method to cure and prevent the Spanish sickness* (London, 1596) — a cure for one disease "whereby the learned and skilful chirurgian may heale a great many other diseases." He petitioned the King because, as he wrote, "It pleased the king to hear my complaints about certain abuses of our art" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [RCPSG Heritage](https://heritage.rcpsg.ac.uk/exhibits/show/maister-peter-lowe/the-charter-of-1599)).
- **"Sendit nain" — the weeding of Dumbuk Ford, 1612.** The Chirurgeons were fined £6 Scots by the Deacon Convener for failing to send two men to help the other crafts weed the ford: they had "sendit nain." The grandest craft in Glasgow, too proud for mud ([Lumsden](https://electricscotland.com/history/medical/14Article10TraditionsOfTheTradeHousesOfGlasgow.pdf)).
- **The locked hall, December 1719.** The Deacon (a surgeon) refused to convene the trade, and when the Barbers arrived at the Chirurgeons' and Barbers' Hall with the Trades' Baillie and Deacon Convener at the appointed hour, the officer announced "that the Deacon had the key and keeped up the same." The Barbers turned on their heel and held the meeting in the Tron Church, minuted every grievance — the poor "altogether neglected," entrants turned away, unfreemen with "open shops" and "publick signes" — and won everything on paper within three years ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The surgeons' self-amputation.** Tennent's own scalpel-edged verdict on 1719–22: the Surgeons, "deprived of the power which as a clique they had so long asserted, could find no other remedy than the amputating knife, and in their haste they decided upon self-excision" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The skeleton clause, 1708.** When the joint stock was divided, the surgeons kept "the Bookes, Skeletone and all rarities now in the hall" — the Barbers formally renounced all claim to the skeleton, forever ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Deacon Duncan Niven at the Battle of Falkirk, 1746.** Barber of Bell's Wynd, whose shop was a famous meeting-place: he marched with the Trades' Battalion against the Jacobites at Falkirk, and "for his gallant and heroic behaviour" was chosen Deacon of the Barbers the next year. He rose to Deacon Convener of all the Trades (1761–62, 1777–78), and in 1778 went to London with the Lord Provost to offer the King a Glasgow regiment — the 83rd — for the American War ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **A hundred and fifty periwigs for the poor, 1723.** Alexander Fullerton, merchant and periwig-maker, gifted the newly independent trade 150 new periwigs (26 of them campaign wigs), a parcel of hair, and his whole work-looms and tools, all rouped the next day for a thousand merks for the poor's box ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The Sabbath wars.** In 1734 the trade's own officer testified on oath that he had seen a young man leave John Carse's shop "upon a Sabbath day betwixt sermons, carrying in his hand a pirriewig rolled up in the net with the tail of it hanging out… and saw it was powdered." The apprentice George Lang then confessed he had "shaved and dressed two gentlemen in the house of Collector Wood, and likewise dressed Principal Campbell's son's hair… all upon the Sabbath day," and was fined ten pounds Scots for the poor. The campaign never ended: overtures against the "Sinful and wicked practice of shaving and dressing on the Lord's day" (1778), a petition to the Presbytery of Glasgow (granted, 1795), memorials to the Lord Provost for police action in 1821, 1827 and 1834 — and the customers kept demanding Sunday chins all the same ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The Sabbath post office protest, 1760.** The trade formally declared its "Detestation and abhorrence" that the mail packet arrived on Sunday morning and that citizens "Dare and presume to call at the Post office for Letters and Newspapers, read and consider the same, and make answers to their Letters" — fearing God would "pour out His Righteous Judgement upon the City & its Trade and Business" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Sandy Barber and Glasgow, 1741.** The minute book records: "Sandy Barber, a negro, is booked journeyman with Thomas Glendinning, and Glasgow, a negro, is booked journeyman with John Carse" — two Black men formally enrolled in a Glasgow craft in 1741; in 1769 "a negro boy belonging to Matthew Orr of Stobross" was booked "as learning to shave and dress" — the ledger of an incorporation recording, without comment, both membership and enslavement. And in 1791–92 the trade booked women: "Miss Rankine, Miss Jamieson, Miss M'Kay, Miss Weir, Miss M'Kenzie, and two Miss Erskines… learning to dress," and "Jean Aird learning to shave and dress" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The slave-trade petition, 1792.** "Which day the Incorporation unanimously agree to petition Parliament for the abolition of the African Slave trade, and to advertise the same in the 'Advertiser' and 'Mercury' newspapers" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The tied election, 1727.** Deacon's Choosing: 22 votes for Alexander Leggat, 22 for Charles Anderson, decided by the sitting Deacon's casting vote — protested (he had already voted once on the roll-call) and appealed all the way up before Leggat was seated the following May ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The lost Charter, 1835.** The Faculty of Physicians and Surgeons asked whether the original Royal Charter of 1599 lay in the Barbers' charter chest. The chest was solemnly searched: "they did not find there the Charter wanted." The original was never recovered — the College holds only a notarised 1804 copy and a transcript in its first minute book ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [RCPSG Heritage](https://heritage.rcpsg.ac.uk/exhibits/show/maister-peter-lowe/the-charter-of-1599)).
- **Reform barbers, 1831.** The Incorporation addressed King William IV three times in support of the Reform Bill — "restoring to the people their just rights" — and received a gracious reply over the signature of Lord Melbourne himself ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Ritchie Falconer's razor and Latin.** Strang's sketch of the eighteenth-century Glasgow barber (reprinted as the Records' closing flourish): a friseur with "a grammar-school education" who peppered his shaves with *Gaudeo te valere* and *O tempora! O mores!*, knew "everything transacted in the City," and — when the Council threatened to ban Sunday shaving — revealed the trade's workaround: apprentices would "parade the streets on that morning in white hose, and you have only to raise the window, haud up your wee finger, and my sang! your chin will sune be as smooth as it is noo, Sunday tho' it be" ([Records, 1899, quoting Strang's *Glasgow and its Clubs*](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The Basle brotherhood, 1999.** The Incorporation of Barbers of Basle sent a delegation to Glasgow in 1999 as the Glasgow craft marked the 400th anniversary of its charter ([Trades House Digital Library — Barber](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Arms.** "Vert, on a chevron argent, between two razors endorsed, open, blades downwards, in chief, and a peruke in base, three lancets expanded, all proper." The three instruments of the whole calling on one shield: razors for barbery, lancets for blood-letting, a peruke (periwig) for the wig trade ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Motto on the arms:** *In the Presence of God* — fitting for a craft whose daily work was a blade at a bared throat ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The painted motto.** The Incorporation's old oil painting in the Trades' Hall bears *MUNDA HAEC ARS VIGEBAT DIEBUS JOSEPHI, Jacobi patriarchae filii, Aegypti praefecti* — "This elegant art flourished in the days of Joseph, son of Jacob the patriarch, governor of Egypt." The craft claims descent not from a saint but from Scripture: Joseph, who had to be shaved before he could stand before Pharaoh ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House of Glasgow — Barbers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-barbers/)).
- **Patron saint: none recorded.** Unlike Glasgow's medieval crafts, the Chirurgeons and Barbers were founded in 1599 — after the Reformation — so there is no altar, saint's light, or patronal feast in their record. (Elsewhere in Britain barber-surgeons looked to saints such as Cosmas and Damian, but no source consulted attaches any saint to the Glasgow craft; the biblical Joseph functions as its ancestral figure instead.) [extrapolated: the general barber-surgeon patronage; the absence in Glasgow is directly evidenced by the founding date and the silence of the Records.]
- **The barber's pole.** The craft's street-heraldry: "a white painted pole entwined with a red fillet or ribbon," with a brass basin hung beneath — the ribbon for tying the arm, the pole gripped by the patient during bleeding, the basin to catch the blood. A former president of the Edinburgh Faculty of Surgeons is quoted calling it testimony to "the universality of the ancient connection of the Barbers with the Surgeons" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House of Glasgow — Barbers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-barbers/)).

## Rituals & Customs

- **The oath.** Every entrant "gave his oath *de fideli* as use is" — of faithful administration — a usage kept until 1871. Behind it stood the Burgess Oath (required of every freeman until the early 1800s): "Here I protest, before God, that I confess and allow with my heart the true Religion… renouncing the Roman Religion called Papistry… I shall not colour unfreemen's goods under colour of my own… I shall make concord, where discord is, to the utmost of my power" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The essay (masterpiece).** No admission without "a sufficient essay… appointed him by the Deacon & masters." For barbers this meant making two wigs, chosen from pairings such as "A Grizel cut Bob wig and a Queue wig… A Bag wig and a Scratch wig… A Brigadier wig and a Natural wig… A Campaign wig and a Spencer wig… A Foxtail wig and a Drop lock wig, or A Long Bob Wig with a feathered fore top." A man intending only a small trade was tried on shaving and dressing a head; a hair merchant on an essay of curled hair. Essay masters inspected the work. The last essay was made in 1864 ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). In the joint era the surgeons' essays ran to dissecting "the eye of ane animal" and discoursing "on the brain."
- **Apprenticeship.** "Fyve yeirs as prenteis and twa yeirs for meit and fee" — five years bound, two more for meat and wages — indentures written only by the Clerk, booked within three months or double dues. One apprentice per master per five years (relaxed 1771). Charity apprentices (Kirk Session, Highland Society, Mortifications) booked free ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Lumsden](https://electricscotland.com/history/medical/14Article10TraditionsOfTheTradeHousesOfGlasgow.pdf)).
- **Courts and elections.** Four head courts a year "and oftner pro re nata," absentees poinded half a merk "for the use of the poore"; poinding also for absence "from courtis and buriallis" — attendance at a brother's funeral was enforceable. Lammas Court in August settled all dues before anyone could vote; Deacon's Choosing before Michaelmas. From 1780 the incoming Deacon paid a guinea and a half and the Collector half a guinea, "in place of being put to the expense of a breakfast," to fund a dinner for the trade "ready at three o'clock afternoon" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The Deacon's box.** The trade's charter chest: in 1773 a new box was made and the old titles, bonds and books solemnly transferred; the Deacon kept the key of the upper part, the Collector the key of the inner box, and the two box-masters a key each — four keys, four officers, no single hand on the treasure ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Insignia and relics.** The Bell inscribed "Unity and Order," presented by Collector James Barton in 1798 "to be used at their convivial meetings, for the preservation of good order" — still rung at Incorporation dinners in 1899. The Deacon's gold medal and chain, procured 1861, celebrated with a supper in the Waverley Hotel. A goat's-head snuff-box with silver mountings, "the lid being a magnificent Cairngorm set in silver," gifted by Deacon William Kyle in 1864. Albums for the portraits of Deacons (1873, 1897). The 1656 Seal of Cause itself, preserved and legible after two and a half centuries ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Wig quality law.** Under the 1726 Acts: no freeman may "work up old hair in a Pirrie-wigg & sell it for new"; no thread nets, thread bordering, or woollen crowning in a wig; from 1743, a wig **stamp-master** examined every wig made for export and struck it with the trade's stamp — unstamped export wigs cost the maker his vote and any office. Two members searched the shops quarterly for "insufficient work" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Decorum.** "That no freeman give undecent names to another freeman in any court or meeting of the Trade" (fine: three pounds Scots to the poor); no interrupting a brother "when speaking to the Deacon" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Pendicles.** Freemen's sons and sons-in-law who had not learned the trade entered cheaply as "pendicles" — members barred from office and from the tools — until 1865, when "all the members, whether operative or pendicle, shall be considered on an equality" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Charity as constitution.** Nearly every fine in every act is destined "to the poor of the Trade." The independent trade paid £20 Scots yearly to the House's poor as the price of its three seats; joined bulk purchases of thousands of bolls of meal in famine years "to be sold to the poor of the City at cost price"; subscribed £60 to found the Royal Infirmary in 1787, buying the right to recommend two patients a year; and by the twentieth century kept the gentler custom of shortbread gifts to its pensioners ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House Digital Library — Barber](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html)).

## Sensory World

Hot water and suds; the rasp of a strop and the small cold kiss of steel under the jaw. The shop opens at six in the morning and the candles burn till nine at night — eleven on Saturdays, when the act of 1726 finally orders the door shut. Blood in a brass basin, the patient gripping the painted pole, a red fillet knotted round the arm. Teeth pulled in the same chair where chins are lathered. Then the eighteenth century turns and the shop fills with hair: parcels of it bought and sold, work-looms and wig-blocks, campaign wigs and bag wigs and scratch wigs on stands, curling tongs heating at the fire, clouds of "moost" — powder — thrown into a gentleman's hair, bear's grease and honey-water, the stamp-master's punch on an export wig. Apprentices in white hose slip through the Sunday streets between sermons, a powdered periwig rolled in its net "with the tail of it hanging out." And always the talk: the barber as the town's living newspaper, trading the Provost's dinner-menu and the Council's plots with every scrape of the razor, in a close off the Saltmarket where the Molendinar Burn floods the laigh houses ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).

## Values

1. **Trust — the bared throat.** The whole trade rested on being safe to touch people: razor at the neck, lancet at the vein, hands in the hair of everyone from the Lord Provost down. The arms' motto *In the Presence of God* sanctifies exactly this; the charter's duties (report every violent death honestly, treat the poor gratis) institutionalise it ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
2. **Tenacity within the law.** Eighteen years of appeals (1704, 1707, 1712, 1719) — never a riot, never a brawl, always the petition, the minute, the magistrate — until the surgeons destroyed themselves. Even locked out of their own hall they went to church and took minutes ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
3. **Equality among brethren.** The causes they fought for were levelling ones: free access for every qualified barber (1707), equal rights for freemen's sons regardless of branch (1719), office rights for members outside the Royalty (1796, carried on James Barton's own motion), pendicle equality (1865). Their minute books enrolled Black journeymen in 1741 and women learners in 1791 without ceremony, and in 1792 they unanimously petitioned to abolish the slave trade ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
4. **Workmanship warranted.** No entry without an essay; shops searched quarterly; no old hair sold as new; export wigs stamped or confiscated for the poor. The craft's honour was enforceable, in pounds Scots ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
5. **The poor of the trade first.** Every fine, every booking fee, every forfeited wig flows to "the use of the poore" — from the 1602 rule of "twentie shilling to the puir" yearly, through famine-year meal schemes at cost price, to Infirmary beds and pensioners' shortbread ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html)).
6. **Piety and order.** Sabbath enforcement pursued for a century (their own apprentices fined, the Presbytery petitioned, the police enlisted); a bell called "Unity and Order" to govern even their drinking; fines for "undecent names" in court ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
7. **A citizen's voice.** The smallest craft repeatedly took public positions: burgh reform (1783, 1819), opposing "tyrannical and oppressive" police bills, opposing Corn Laws that would starve "the labouring classes," addressing kings on Reform. Their 1783 paper demanded "freedom and liberty to be the word" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).

## Shadow

Every virtue of the chair has its curdled twin. **Trust** curdles into the gossip's treason — the barber was "the principal newsmonger," and the same tongue that soothed a customer could sell his secrets round the town by noon ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). **Tenacity** curdles into litigiousness: 120 years of grudge, appeal, and counter-appeal, a corporate life defined by who it was suing. **Equality among brethren** curdles into exclusion of everyone else: the Burgess Oath that barred Catholics from the trade entirely, the petitions *against* Catholic emancipation in 1778, 1825 and 1829, the seizure of an outsider's ten periwigs, the prosecution of "usurpers" and King's freemen, five guineas subscribed to crush a militiaman's right to work ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). **Workmanship warranted** curdles into protectionism — the stamp and the search as tollgate rather than guarantee. **Charity** curdles into control: the 1754 act that cut off charity forever from any transgressor's "descendants or relations." **Piety** curdles into sanctimony — masters who fined their apprentices ten pounds for Sunday shaving while quietly pocketing the fees of gentlemen who demanded it; Strang's barber saw it plainly: "when the unco guid get into power, they're aye scadding their tongues in ither folks kale." And **order** curdles into the masters' boot: in 1750, when the journeymen combined to work "only" fourteen hours a day instead of fifteen, the trade crushed the combination and piously feared the men would "squander [their evenings] in vagueing on the streets, or in tippling and drinking" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).

## Rivals & Kin

- **The Surgeons — the defining rivalry.** One incorporation, two castes: surgeons claimed "priority in all things," barbers claimed the letter of the law, and "Surgeons often arraigned Barbers before [the Deacon Convener] in judgment, and vice versa" ([Lumsden](https://electricscotland.com/history/medical/14Article10TraditionsOfTheTradeHousesOfGlasgow.pdf)). The split of 1719–22 was Scotland's pattern: Edinburgh's surgeons and barbers had divorced in 1719, and Tennent jokes the Glasgow surgeons "caught the infection from their brethren in the East" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)). Yet the kinship never died — the modern craft gifts books to the Royal College born of their shared charter ([Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html)).
- **The Masons — the precedence fight, 1724.** The Barbers claimed their old seat above the Masons and lost before the whole House: thirteenth, "between the Gardeners and Bonnetmakers," forever ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **The Baxters (bakers).** In the dearth of 1741 the trade complained its members were "greatly imposed upon by the Baxters in buying flour" — and simply bought its own grain in bulk ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Kin by marriage — the Weavers.** The pendicle rolls show weaver after weaver entering "as married to" a barber's daughter (Robert Muir, Thomas Dobbie, John Russell, 1748–49): the wig-maker's family tree ran on the loom side ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Subordinate kin — the Gorbals barbers.** Taken on as a "pendicle" incorporation in 1732, with the delightful reciprocal clause that a Gorbals barber selling a wig in Glasgow "shall have liberty to Shave the Buyers head and face if required" ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf)).
- **Allies at need.** The Edinburgh barbers (joint Sabbath campaign, 1794); the Cordiners and the Hammermen of Leith (subscriptions to fight militiamen's trading claims, 1819–21); the Incorporation of Barbers of Basle (fraternal visit, 1999) ([Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf); [Trades House Digital Library](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/barber.html)).

## Lexicon

Period and craft words a Scots narrator could put in a barber's mouth (all from the [Records, 1899](http://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/records_of_the_barbers_1899_second_copy.pdf) unless noted):

1. **barborie** — the art of the barber ("any poynt of the airt of Chirurgianrie or Barbourie").
2. **pirrie-wig / peruke** — a periwig; the craft's second livelihood.
3. **the essay / sey** — the trial-piece required for freedom; judged by **essay masters**.
4. **upsett** — the entry payment of a new master; "pay ane new upsett" was the standard fine.
5. **freedom fine** — the entry money paid at admission as freeman.
6. **booked** — formally enrolled (apprentice, journeyman, or learner) in the Clerk's register.
7. **meit and fie** — "five years as prentice and two years for meat and fee": the apprentice's seven-year road.
8. **pendicle** — a member (son or son-in-law) not qualified to work the trade or hold office; also a subordinate incorporation (the Gorbals barbers).
9. **quarter accounts** — the freeman's quarterly dues to the poor's box.
10. **poind** — to distrain, fine by seizure; absents from "courts and buriallis" were poinded.
11. **packing and peeling** — trading in league with unfreemen; the classic craft crime.
12. **cardowing** — moonlighting; a journeyman making wigs privately "under the way of cardowing as it is commonly called."
13. **unfreeman** — one practising without freedom of the trade; his signs pulled down, his wigs seized.
14. **de fideli** — the oath of faithful administration sworn by every entrant and officer.
15. **toties quoties** — "as often as it happens": how fines recur.
16. **letter of horning** — royal letters compelling payment on pain of outlawry (in the 1599 charter).
17. **Deacon's Choosing / Lammas Court** — the electoral and dues-settling head courts of the year.
18. **moost** — hair-powder ("throwing a little moost into your hair").
19. **the far hand** — entrants with no family or apprenticeship claim on the trade ("admitting members at the far hand").
20. **galravages** — riotous merrymaking ("the Corporation galravages tell on a man's chin and mak it tender").

## Question Seeds

Dilemmas built from the Barbers' actual record — each reveals the value (or its shadow) it tests.

1. **The Locked Hall.** Your deacon — a man of higher station who despises your rank — has locked the hall of your own incorporation and "keeped up the key," and the poor's box is inside going empty. Your brethren are at the door with crowbars in their eyes; the Trades' Baillie stands ready to walk with you instead to the Tron Church and put every wrong down in a minute-book that may take three years to bite. Break the door, or write the minute? — *Choosing the minute reveals **tenacity within the law**; choosing the crowbar reveals its absence (and the appetite for force the Barbers historically refused).*
2. **The Powdered Wig Between Sermons.** You are officer of the trade, sworn *de fideli*. On the Sabbath, between sermons, you see an apprentice — a boy you like — slipping out of a shop with a powdered periwig rolled in its net, the tail hanging out. Report what you saw on oath and the boy is fined ten pounds he does not have, "for the use of the poor"; hold your tongue and your own oath rots. — *Testifying reveals **piety and order** (and its shadow, sanctimony); silence reveals mercy set above the sworn word.*
3. **The Chair Hears Everything.** The Provost's man talks under your razor — carelessly, because you are only a barber — and what he lets slip would win your trade its lawsuit against the surgeons tomorrow. It was said to the chair, not to the court. Use it, and no one will ever know how you knew. — *Refusing reveals **trust — the bared throat**; using it reveals the newsmonger's treason, the craft's oldest shadow.*
4. **Old Hair, New Wig.** A ruinous winter. You have a parcel of old hair that, worked up and powdered, would pass any customer's eye as new — but not the stamp-master's, and the act says such a wig is confiscated for the poor and your name read out at the quarter court. Your children are hungry now; the trade's honour is an abstraction. — *Refusing reveals **workmanship warranted**; the rationalisation ("everyone does it," "the buyer will never know") reveals protectionism's hollow core — a stamp defended only when convenient.*
5. **The Journeymen's Hour, 1750.** You sat in the meeting that enacted six-in-the-morning-till-nine-at-night, and you voted with the masters. Now your own journeymen have combined for one hour less, and the act you helped pass says a master who yields loses office and vote for seven years. You were a journeyman once; you remember the ninth hour. Enforce the act, or stand with the men and be struck from the roll? — *Standing with the men reveals **equality among brethren** carried past its comfortable border; enforcing reveals **order** — and its shadow, the masters' boot.*
6. **The Stranger's Thousand Merks.** A rich stranger — no apprenticeship, no freeman's daughter, no burgess line — lays a bond of a thousand merks on the table "for the use of the poor" and asks to be made free of your trade. The poor's box is thin; the rules are the rules; the surgeons want the money for themselves either way. Your vote decides. — *Voting no reveals **equality among brethren** (the rule that binds rich and poor alike); voting yes reveals **the poor of the trade first** — and asks which loyalty is really the deeper one.*

### Honesty notes

- **No patron saint** is recorded for this craft anywhere in the sources consulted — the 1599 post-Reformation founding explains the absence, and the dossier says so rather than inventing one; the biblical Joseph motto is the craft's genuine self-chosen "ancestor."
- The **"In the Presence of God"** motto is taken from the 1899 Records' description of the Incorporation's coat of arms; the Latin Joseph inscription is on the old Trades' Hall painting and is the motto quoted by the modern Trades House. Both are given, distinguished.
- The 1899 Records could not be OCR-checked against the printed page for every character; obvious scan artifacts in quoted passages were conservatively corrected (e.g. "ars" in the painting motto per the Records' own text) and quotations kept to passages where the extraction was clean.
- Tennent (1899) is openly **partisan for the Barbers**; his characterisations of the Surgeons ("threw up the sponge," "self-excision") are quoted as his voice, not as neutral fact.
