# Incorporation of Maltmen

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Maltmen are the craft of patient transformation and stubborn survival. They took the hardest grain — bear-barley from cold Scottish fields — steeped it in Molendinar water, coaxed it awake on the barn floor, and arrested it in kiln-smoke, turning dormancy into sweetness the way they turned every institutional catastrophe into continuity. Twice Parliament declared them "na craft"; twice they simply renamed their chief, kept the Box locked, and bought their liberty back with patient silver. They never restored the title "Deacon" that was taken from them in 1555 — they made the imposed word "Visitor" their own and have carried it for four and a half centuries, the only craft in Glasgow to do so. Theirs is a soul of quality-guarding, quiet loyalty, ferocious mutual care bounded strictly by the burgh, and the conviviality of the malt itself: the craft that fed Glasgow's ale-vats now watches over its whisky bars. Slow to change, impossible to kill.

## History

- **Remote antiquity, by their own charter.** The precise origin is unrecoverable: tradition holds the Incorporation's records "were destroyed in a great fire" in Glasgow in 1601, and the [Letter of Guildry of 1605 said only that the Incorporation was "established in remote antiquity"](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/). Early Glasgow title-deeds show the trade was everywhere: "there is no description of property so frequently recurring as that of a 'malt-kiln and pertinents'" ([Chronicles of the Maltmen Craft in Glasgow, 1605–1879](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1503** — first statute touching the trade: maltmen must "present their malt to the mercat, and sell none till nine hours, under the pain of escheat of the malt," and take no more than "ane boll of the bear for making the chalder of malt, under the pain of oppression" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Before 1556** — already organized: when Queen Mary's tax was stented on Glasgow in 1556, the Burgh Records name "Stentaris for the Incorporation of Maltmen" — James Mudy, William Wilsoune, Andro and Alexander Pettigrew ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1555** — under the Regency of Marie de Guise the elected Deaconships were suppressed as dangerously democratic and replaced by appointed inspectors called **Visitors**. Other crafts later took the Deacon title back; [the Maltmen never did, and their chairman "enjoys the unique title of Visitor" to this day](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/).
- **1567** — Act of James VI, c. 29: there shall be "na Deakon of Craft of Maltmen … to be repute na craft." The first attempted extinction ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1574** — the Magistrates appoint twelve maltmen, in six pairs, to walk their districts weekly and "taist ye ail browin … and quha brewis yat are unfre" — official ale-tasters and detectors of unfree brewers from "ye Rattonraw and Drygate" to "ye Brig and Stockwell" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **10 November 1604** — Deed of Submission between the Merchant and Craft ranks; James Fisher, Maltman and Clerk of the Incorporation, signs for the craft, "probably … on account of others not being able to write" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **6 February 1605** — the **Letter of Guildry**, Glasgow's great constitutional settlement, establishes "ane Dean of Gild, ane Deacon Conveener, and ane VISITOUR of Maltmen" — the craft's formal charter, ratified by the Town Council on 9 February 1605. The correct designation was "Maltmen and Mealmen," though the craft "kept by the 'Malt' and threw aside the 'Meal'" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). The first Deacon Convener of the Trades House was [Duncan Semphill, a Skipper, "whose craft along with the Mealmen was merged with that of the Maltmen"](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/) — "Duncan Semple" appears among the craft commissioners in the Letter of Guildry itself.
- **8 October 1616** — first recorded election of a Visitor: a leet of four names plus the old Visitor presented to the Provost, Bailies and Council, "who shall make choice of one of them to be VISITOR for that year … and shall be sworne" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1649** — office of **Collector** (treasurer) created; quarter accounts collected at Candlemas, Beltane, Lammas and Hallowmas.
- **1669–1672** — the second extinction and the great resurrection (see Legends below). Parliament's ratification of the Guildry followed on **8 October 1672** (Statute c. 129), and the Archbishop of Glasgow, Arthur Ross, granted a **Charter of Confirmation on 3 January 1684** — the Collector's accounts note £373 11s Scots spent "passing ye Confirmationne … getting ye seall yrto … sundry rides to Edinburgh" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1677** — Town Council act empowering the craft to exact "ane hundredth pounds Scots" from every far-hand stranger entrant, aimed at "straingers incomers" who "steal Malt out of ye town mills excyse frie" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **17 February 1775** — **The Grand Decerniture**: the Court of Session fixes forever the precedence of the fourteen trades, the Maltmen **fourth** — behind only Hammermen, Tailors and Cordiners ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **10 October 1815** — the last official "visiting of the kilns": Visitor Robert Tennent, Junior reports he has "tried the Meal and Bear in the Kilns, Houses, and Shops" of the burgh — the ancient power quietly going into desuetude ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1833** — Burgh Reform: the Visitor is thereafter chosen "by the direct vote" of the assembled freemen, not by the magistrates.
- **1846** — exclusive trading privileges abolished by statute (9 & 10 Vict. c. 17); the Maltmen, on the motion of Dr. Strang, "unanimously declined to interfere with the subject" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). New Laws sanctioned by the Court of Session, 1852.
- **21 July 1924** — arms matriculated in the Lyon Register (vol. 26, fol. 38) ([Court of the Lord Lyon blog](https://officeofthelordlyon.blogspot.com/2024/07/)).
- **Today** — a registered charity representing "people that work in the Scottish Licensed Trade," [funding a brewing student at Heriot-Watt University annually](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/), supporting pensioners, presenting an annual [Best Bar Award](https://sltn.co.uk/2026/03/31/incorporation-of-maltmens-best-bar-award-goes-to-the-ben-nevis/) at the Trades Hall dinner, and admitting members at "Far Hand" and "Near Hand" exactly as in 1621 ([maltmenofglasgow.org](https://www.maltmenofglasgow.org/maltmen-glasgow-membership/)). The Trades House museum notes they are ["the only Incorporation who do not admit female members"](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/maltmen.html).

## Legends & Episodes

- **The craft that Parliament killed twice — and couldn't.** In 1669 Parliament revived the dormant 1567 statute: the Maltmen were "to be repute na craft," their Visitor abolished. The craft's answer was neither riot nor surrender. For 1670–71 there is "no election of 'Visitor'" — instead their chief is quietly styled **"Oversman of their calling"** (John Findlay), assisted by "four or fyve of the most wyse and qualified of the brethrein." They voted to advance "one hundredth merks of every thousand merks they have of frie money for defence of ther and the rest of the Treads' liberties," and **Patrick Bryce was "appoyntit to ryd eist to Edinburgh to attend the Parliament, to see what mitigatioun he can gett."** The Town Council colluded by appointing the man who *would have been* Visitor as burgh inspector of the bear and meal markets. In 1672 Parliament ratified the Guildry; Bryce delivered the unspent war-chest back into the Box and was made **Deacon-Convener of all the Trades for 1672–74**, succeeded by another Maltman, William Wallace: "The Maltmen are in high favour" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The flag of Bothwell Brig.** Preserved inside the Incorporation's carved walnut Box in 1879: "a portion of a silk flag that was carried at Bothwell Brig" — the Covenanters' doomed field of 1679 — "and five or six bodies of old Scotch money," minute fourth-parts of a farthing handed out over the years "as relics to the curious" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). A craft of ale and grain, carrying a battle-flag of the persecuted Kirk in its money-chest.
- **Shoes for the rebels, December 1745.** Prince Charles's Highland army occupied hostile, Whiggish Glasgow and levied 6,000 pairs of shoes, 12,000 shirts and cloth coats from the city. The Maltmen's minute is laconic and unforgettable: *"Spent with the Visitor and some of the Masters at ordering some of the Masters to gather shoes for the Rebels, and given to Porters for carrying them … 18s."* — and, that September, money "spent … ordering the Officer to warn the Members to keep their Servants off the streets in time of disorder" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Highland Host and the stolen kye.** In 1678 Lauderdale's "Highland Host" was let loose on the Covenanting west; the craft's tenant-farmers at Balgray, "staunch adherents" of a conventicle at Provanside, were plundered, and the Master Court compensated them: "twentie pund Scots, for the expense and loss he got by the Hielanders." A decade later, under James VII, the accounts record 36s "spent by ye Visitor and Collector in getting back the Balgray Kyne taken by ye Sodgers" — and, in the same breath, hire of horses for fourteen men to ride out and greet the new Archbishop, the Visitor claiming £2 "in convoying of his Ladie" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). Loyal to the Crown's face; paying the Covenanters' losses out of the Box.
- **The scandal in Francis Hall's house, May 1620.** Jon Merschell, maltman, accused of "abuising of the Visitour … with divers skandelous and opprobrious speiches," confessed before the Quarter Masters in the Craftis Hospitall and was fined "ane new upsett of Twentie merks." By 1637 a standing law: whoever "railes or miscalles ye Visitour … either be word or deid, privatelie or publiklie" pays ten pounds ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **"Not ane honest Maltman in all Glasgow."** In 1665 Robert Corss swore "he vould to God, or be God, yt there was not ane honest Maltman in all Glasgow, but a whein of false villane knaves." Witnesses proved it against him. His sentence was exquisite: the confession was "set doune in ye book and he to subscryve the samyne" — *(Signed) "Robert Corss"* — publishing his shame in the minute-book for all time; the fine itself went unrecorded ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Test on their knees, 1682.** After a disputed election the Town Council annulled James Muir's Visitorship as "inorderlie"; Muir's minority party "produced a protest … and yrafter went out ye kotis" (out of doors). The remaining electors, "all in ane voyce, havin all takin and sworn the Test, on their knees, bareheaded," elected James Walker — who then, shrewdly, named the defeated Muir one of his own Masters, and Muir "sensibly gave his oath with the others" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Gorbals bargain, 1650.** The craft took a quarter-share (with the city and Hutchesons' Hospital) in the 347-acre lands of Gorbals and Brigend — "the bargane of the Gorballs" — an investment still paying the Incorporation its share of the "Cast" of the Gorbal Lands in 1879. Balgray, bought 1666 for 9,800 merks and sold 1730 for 18,600 to freeman John Orr of Barrowfield, was the one that got away; Orr promptly "mortified" 2,000 merks to the craft's poor — the **Barrowfield Mortification**, its only endowed fund ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Bengal refusal, 1874.** Asked by the Lord Provost for a donation to the Bengal Famine Fund, the craft took its Clerk's opinion: the funds' charitable power "must be within the community of the City of Glasgow," and any dissenting member "would succeed in having it found that the Incorporation had done what it had no right to do." The opinion was "unanimously agreed to" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)) — charity with a burgh wall around it, from a body that had given £500 to Crimean widows and built infirmaries at home.
- **The historian in the roll.** John M'Ure, first historian of Glasgow, was admitted freeman in 1682 "quat of ye ordinary dewes … for services done and to be done" — the craft collected lawyers and chroniclers the way it collected bonds ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). And the **Tennent brewing dynasty** runs through the book: Robert Tennent, Junior, Visitor, made the last kiln inspection in 1815; Gilbert Rainy Tennent, Clerk 1845–52, left to run "the Brewery at Wellpark … that world-famed firm" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **1792, against the Jacobins.** The craft publicly declared against "wild, new-fangled, and extravagant writings and opinions, subversive of our present happy constitution," pledging "their lives and fortunes" to King and Constitution, published in the Glasgow and Edinburgh newspapers ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)) — the book's own verdict on the craft's temperament: "either discreetly silent or safely loyal."

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Arms** (matriculated 21 July 1924, Lyon Register vol. 26, fol. 38): *"Gules on a chevron between two garbs in chief and in base a malt measure full of malt and across the mouth of the same a rolling-pin in bend all Or, two malt shovels of the field"* ([Court of the Lord Lyon blog](https://officeofthelordlyon.blogspot.com/2024/07/)). Red for the kiln-fire; gold for the grain: **garbs** (sheaves of bear-barley), the **malt measure** heaped full, and the crossed **malt shovels** of the barn floor. The craft's symbol — "malt shovels and sheaves of corn" — [can still be found on gravestones all over the country](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/).
- **The medal inscription that serves as a creed.** The Visitor's gold medal and chain (first worn 22 December 1851 — the Incorporation "almost the last of the Trades' House" to adopt one) bears the arms and the words: *"Established in remote antiquity. Sanctioned by the Letter of Guildry, 6th February, 1605. Ratified by Parliament, 1672"* ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Motto:** none is recorded in any source consulted — the Lyon blazon, the Chronicles, and the Trades House pages are all silent. Treat the 1851 medal inscription as the craft's functional motto.
- **Patron saint:** none recorded. The craft's pre-1601 records burned, and its surviving constitution is post-Reformation, when Glasgow's craft altars were already dissolved. [extrapolated: pre-Reformation Scottish crafts typically maintained an altar and patron in the burgh kirk, and the Maltmen's pre-1560 devotion, if any, is simply lost — any quiz use of a patron for this craft should lean on the *absence*: a craft whose sanctities are the Box, the book, and the oath.] The Letter of Guildry's own stated end was "the advancement of God's glory … as faithful Christians and loving citizens" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).

## Rituals & Customs

- **The oath.** Every entrant "gaif his aith of fidelitie for obedience to the pnt Visitor and his successors in office, **and to mak Malt weill and skilfullie**, under the pane of twentie merks" — with a named **cautioner** (guarantor) standing bound for him ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). No masterpiece or essay-piece is recorded for entry (unlike the Hammermen or Wrights): the maltman's trial was perpetual — every making of malt was inspectable, forever.
- **Entry at near-hand and far-hand.** Sons and sons-in-law of freemen entered for 20s; burgesses for 20 merks; and by the Act of 1621, "all strangeris … not marieand ane Burges manis dochter, being ane maidin undeflorit, sail pay for admissionne Fourtie merks" — marriage into the craft literally halved the price. By 1677, £100 Scots for far-hand strangers; in 1852, £100 sterling far-hand against £5 5s near-hand. The two doors survive today as the **Far Hand** and **Near Hand** application forms ([maltmenofglasgow.org](https://www.maltmenofglasgow.org/maltmen-glasgow-membership/)).
- **The dinner.** Every new freeman owed "for the denner" £6 13s 4d besides his entry silver — "Ressaivit for his denner and mortclaith" sit incongruously together in the accounts — until 1642, when the dinner was commuted to ten merks paid into the Box "for the common good" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)). The annual dinner survives at the Trades Hall, where the [Best Bar Award plaque is presented](https://sltn.co.uk/2026/03/31/incorporation-of-maltmens-best-bar-award-goes-to-the-ben-nevis/).
- **Election and the leet.** Annually in October: four names plus the old Visitor presented to the magistrates until 1833, thereafter by direct vote of the freemen. Quarter accounts fell due at **Candlemas, Beltane, Lammas and Hallowmas**. The career ladder was fixed by custom: Collector, then "Last Collector" *ex officio* in the Master Court, then Visitor ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Box.** The craft's ark: carved walnut front showing "a Master Maltman overseeing an apprentice and servant — one shovelling the malt from the barn floor into a caup, the other meting the grain with a meting stick." Two or three **Key-keepers** ("Box-maisters") each held a key; at each new Visitor's installation the Box moved to his house and was inventoried. It held the bonds, the free money, the Bothwell Brig flag, and the Grand Decerniture's notarial copy ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Mortcloths and the duty of presence.** From 1622 the craft kept velvet mortcloths — "ane meikill claithe of velvet … for aigit peopill, and ane less claithe of velvet for young childring" — hired at 16s and 8s "for supplie of ye puir decayit Bretherin." Attendance at the burial of any brother, his wife or bairn was compulsory, "from the Buriall House to the Wyndheid, under the pain of four shillings Scots, to be paid without forgiveness" (1694). Mortcloth hire became a major revenue: £218 16s Scots in the mortality year 1675 ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Visiting.** The Visitor's defining rite, from which his very title flows: "the monthly visitin and sichtin the haill kills in toun" and weekly inspection of the Bear and Meal Market, with power to "try all Meal and Bear either in kills, houses, or booths" and to condemn "hot, rotten, or frost-slain stuff." Freemen warned to accompany him and refusing paid "twentie shillings toties quoties" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Sabbath discipline.** The Visitor "shall take tryall of them that prophane the Sabbath day in their calling, in dighting, receiving and delivering of Meall, Bear, Corn, and Malt, carrying of steep water, ingleing of kills" — ten shillings fine, "by the unlaw of the Session of the Kirk" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **The Collector's levee.** Pensioners held parchment tickets marked with five years of pay-dates and attended in person quarterly at the Trades' House — "a cheerful meeting, where … kindly greetings are exchanged, inquiries are made for the absent aged and infirm" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).

## Sensory World

Bear-barley in sacks at the Cross in Trongate; the plunge and reek of the **steep**, servants carrying steep water through the closes at dawn; grain **couched** and spread on the barn floor, turned with wooden **malt shovels** until it chits; the sweet, warm, bread-and-earth smell of green malt; then the **kiln** — "ingleing of kills," peat and coal smoke drifting over the Rottenrow, the crackle and the dry-biscuit scent of the cured malt. The knock of the **meting stick** levelling grain in a **caup**; the dusty business of **dighting** (winnowing); the Molendinar burn running so clean "that people in the Bridgegate took the water thereof for the brewing of their ale." Market mornings: the ladle-men taking their due, the Visitor and his assisters plunging hands into sacks hunting "hot, rotten, or frost-slain stuff." Ale-tasters walking their paired beats weekly from the Wyndheid to the Barrasyett. The cold iron of three keys turning in the walnut Box; velvet mortcloths carried in wallets to a door where the whole craft stands bareheaded; and at the year's end, candle-lit accounts — "compts" — read aloud in the Craftis Hospitall ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).

## Values

1. **Guardianship of quality.** The craft's founding power was to *try* — to condemn "hot, rotten, or frost-slain stuff" wherever found and fix its price. The oath itself binds every member "to mak Malt weill and skilfullie." Quality was not a preference; it was sworn.
2. **Mutual burden-bearing.** Entry silver, quarter accounts, mortcloth hires and fines all flowed to "the weill of the tred" — pensions for "decayed brethren," widows, children and unmarried daughters; a kist and winding sheet bought for a dead brother's widow (1662). By 1879 the craft judged itself "one of the richest Incorporations in Scotland" measured by care given.
3. **Tenacity of institution.** Declared no craft in 1567 and 1669; records burned in 1601; privileges abolished in 1846 — and continuously operating throughout, down to the "Visitor" title kept since 1555. Survival by adaptation, never by surrender.
4. **Ordered loyalty.** "Either discreetly silent or safely loyal": fines for miscalling the Visitor, the Test sworn kneeling in 1682, shoes gathered for an occupying army rather than let servants riot, the 1792 declaration. Peace in the burgh outranked private conviction.
5. **Prudent stewardship.** Money in bonds with cautioners, the Gorbals quarter-share paying for centuries, no loans "on personal security alone," nothing lent to a Master Court member "in any shape or form whatever" — and the Bengal refusal, because the funds belonged to Glasgow's own.
6. **Fellowship of the table.** The exacted entry "denner," the Collector's "cheerful" levee, and the living link to the licensed trade — the craft that made the drink still honours those who serve it, [presenting its Best Bar plaque with 300 whiskies behind the gantry](https://sltn.co.uk/2026/03/31/incorporation-of-maltmens-best-bar-award-goes-to-the-ben-nevis/).
7. **Presence at the graveside.** Four shillings, "without forgiveness," for missing a brother's burial: the conviction that showing up — for the dead, the widow, the bairn — is the irreducible duty of belonging.

## Shadow

Every Maltmen virtue has its curdled twin. Guardianship of quality becomes **protectionist monopoly**: the same Visitor who condemned rotten malt hounded Malcolme Bankier for the crime of making malt while unfree, and priced strangers out at £100 Scots. Mutual care becomes **parochialism** — the Box's mouth opens for Glasgow widows and snaps shut at Bengal's starving, on legal advice, unanimously. Tenacity becomes **obstinacy**: kiln inspections performed as empty theatre by 1815, the last craft to take a medal, and still in the 21st century [the only Incorporation admitting no women](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/maltmen.html). Ordered loyalty becomes **servility** — the Test sworn on the knees, bareheaded, to keep an election; sentiments trimmed to whichever Archbishop or Prince rides in, convoying his Ladie for £2 while quietly compensating the plundered conventiclers. Prudence becomes **miserliness and self-dealing risk** — a craft so acquainted with temptation it had to legislate that its own Masters could never touch the funds. And the fellowship of malt carries its oldest shadow: the drink itself, conviviality sliding toward Robert Corss roaring in his cups that there is "not ane honest Maltman in all Glasgow" — the craft's fear that the thing it makes might unmake it.

## Rivals & Kin

- **The Merchants' rank** — the founding adversary. The 1604–05 contest between Merchant and Craft ranks produced the Letter of Guildry itself; James Fisher signed for the maltmen at that table.
- **The first Four against the last Ten.** In 1771 the ten lesser trades demanded equal representation in the Trades House; the Maltmen stood with the **Hammermen, Tailors and Cordiners** through four years of litigation to the **Grand Decerniture** (1775), which froze precedence forever — Maltmen fourth. The four victors formed a standing compact, lodging the 742-folio decree in the Hammermen's box as common custodiers ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Bakers and Brewers** — commercial cousins and irritants: they bought victual in the country and slipped it past the market, "disappointing the Burgh of the Custom or Ladles"; in 1786 the Maltmen funded "a process against the Bakers, Brewers, Fleshers, and Gardeners, relative to the billetting of soldiers" ([Chronicles](https://archive.org/details/chroniclesofmalt00incoiala)).
- **Unfreemen, strangers and excise-dodgers** — the perennial enemy: "straingers incomers … taks many sinistrous ways and courses to steal Malt out of ye town mills excyse frie," underselling "honest men" (1677 petition). The craft's remedy was always the same: force them inside the fold or price them out of the burgh.
- **Kin absorbed:** the **Mealmen** and the **Skippers** were merged into the calling at the Guildry — a craft of grain that quietly swallowed the craft of the river; the very first Deacon Convener, Duncan Semphill, was [a Skipper of that merged line](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-maltmen/).
- **Allies of convenience:** the **Kirk Session** (joint enforcement of Sabbath fines), the **Town Council** (which chose the Visitor from the leet until 1833 and rescued the craft in 1671 by appointing its man as burgh inspector), the **Gorbals feuars** (fellow-privileged in malt, joint victims of strangers), and the **Writers** — a dozen lawyers admitted free "for services done to the Calling," including Glasgow's first historian John M'Ure.

## Lexicon

1. **Visitor** — the craft's unique chief; he who *visits* the kilns. Never "Deacon."
2. **bear** (bere) — the hardy four-row barley of old Scotland, the maltman's raw stuff.
3. **boll / chalder** — grain measures; one boll of bear per chalder of malt, by law of 1503.
4. **upset / upsett** — entry fee; "ane new upset" — a fine equal to re-entering the craft.
5. **unlaw** — a fine; "unlawit in ane new upsett of Twentie merks."
6. **unfreeman** — one practising the craft without freedom of the Incorporation; the enemy.
7. **far-hand / near-hand** — entering as a stranger vs. by blood or marriage; still on the application forms.
8. **steep water** — water carried for steeping the barley; carrying it on the Sabbath was an offence.
9. **ingleing of kills** — kindling the kiln fires.
10. **dighting** — cleaning/winnowing grain.
11. **leet / lyte** — the shortlist of names for election.
12. **mortclaith silver** — the entrant's payment toward the velvet funeral pall.
13. **caution / caur** — a guarantor bound for another's obligations.
14. **the Box / Box-maisters** — the craft's chest and its key-keepers.
15. **Quarter Maisters** — the Visitor's assistants, one per quarter of the year's collections.
16. **toties quoties** — "as often as it happens": fines charged per offence, every time.
17. **compt / compts** — the accounts, "maid compt" aloud at each quarter.
18. **"hot, rotten, or frost-slain stuff"** — the Letter of Guildry's litany of condemned grain.
19. **ladles** — the burgh's toll scooped from every sack sold at market.
20. **Oversman** — the emergency title assumed 1670–71 when "Visitor" was outlawed.
21. **stentars** — assessors who apportioned a tax; the craft's earliest documented officers (1556).
22. **"mak Malt weill and skilfullie"** — the working clause of the entrant's oath.

## Question Seeds

1. **The Oversman's silence (1669–72).** Parliament has declared your calling extinct — your title illegal, your meetings void. One brother wants open defiance and a riot at the Cross; the craft instead renames its chief "Oversman," keeps meeting quietly, and sends Patrick Bryce east with a purse to buy the liberty back. *You are asked to fund the purse and hold your tongue for three years.* Do you? — Choosing the long patient purse reveals **Tenacity of institution** (and Prudent stewardship); choosing the riot reveals its absence: pride mistaken for principle.
2. **Shoes for the Rebels (December 1745).** The Pretender's army holds your city; you despise everything it stands for. The magistrates order your craft to gather shoes from your neighbours for the shirtless Highlanders — feed the enemy, or invite the sack of Glasgow. You are the Master sent door to door with the porters. Do you gather the shoes diligently, gather them badly, or refuse? — Diligence reveals **Ordered loyalty** (the burgh's peace above your politics); refusal reveals conviction valued above community survival.
3. **The Bengal letter (1874).** The Lord Provost himself asks your rich craft for famine relief half a world away. The Clerk warns: the Box exists for *your own* — Glasgow's widows, your decayed brethren — and one dissenting member could have any gift struck down in court. The starving are real either way. Do you vote the donation and dare the dissenter, or agree unanimously to refuse? — Refusing reveals **Prudent stewardship / Mutual burden-bearing** in its bounded, covenantal form; voting the gift reveals compassion that breaks the covenant to obey the conscience.
4. **Robert Corss's toast (1665).** Deep in drink, you believe what Corss believed — that some brothers mix frost-slain bear among the good and the Visitor winks at his friends. Swear it aloud and you will be tried by the very men you accuse, and made to sign your shame in the book forever. Stay silent and the rot continues under the velvet. Which do you choose? — Speaking reveals **Guardianship of quality** raised above fellowship; silence reveals **Fellowship of the table** — and its shadow, complicity.
5. **The frost-slain winter.** The frost has slain half your own crop of bear. Mixed cunningly among the good, it would pass — the Visitor's circuit is monthly, your kiln was sighted last week, and your bairns are hungry. Declared honestly, the Visitor and two assisters will price your whole making at a loss. There is no chance of discovery. What is the malt worth when no one tries it? — Declaring reveals **Guardianship of quality** as private discipline (the sworn oath kept in the dark); mixing reveals the craft's oldest sin, the one its whole constitution was built to prevent.
6. **The unfreeman at your kiln (1615).** Malcolme Bankier is skilled, poor, and unfree; he asks to make malt secretly in your kiln, in partnership. The law says report him. Richard Donaldson's actual answer was a third way: he stood **cautioner** — bound his own purse — to force Malcolme *into* the freedom within a fortnight. Do you shelter him lawlessly, report him, or bind yourself for him? — Standing cautioner reveals **Mutual burden-bearing** fused with **Ordered loyalty** (mercy that repairs the fold rather than breaching it); sheltering reveals kindness that corrodes the covenant; reporting reveals law without cost to yourself.
