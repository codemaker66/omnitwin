# Incorporation of Coopers

> Research dossier for the Trades House "sorting hat" quiz. Ninth of the fourteen
> Incorporations of the Trades House of Glasgow. Compiled 2026-08-05 from the web
> sources listed at the end; enriched the same day in a second pass that
> extracted the full text of the primary printed sources (Lumsden's *Records of
> the Trades House 1605–1678*, Crawfurd's 1858 *Sketch*, Cleland's *Annals*,
> Muir's 1939 Hammermen relics volume, the Coopers' own past-deacons roll, and
> the comparative Aberdeen/Edinburgh guild histories). Early-Scots quotations
> are given as printed in the scholarly editions, with scribal contractions
> lightly expanded. Anything not directly evidenced is tagged [extrapolated];
> customs documented only for OTHER cooper guilds are tagged [comparative] —
> neither is ever presented as Glasgow-Coopers fact.

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The cooper's whole art is bending straight things into a circle that holds. Oak
staves that want to spring apart are coaxed by fire, steam and iron hoops into a
vessel that must be *tight* — proof against water, brine, ale, whisky, and the
roll of an Atlantic crossing. It is a craft with no façade: no cathedral bears a
cooper's name, no garment shows his stitch. His masterpiece is a container for
other people's wealth, judged by the one merciless test of whether it leaks.
From their first charter the Glasgow Coopers institutionalised this ethic —
weekly searching of every man's work "to see if it be sufficient and good and
able for serving our sovereign lord's lieges" — and paired it with the quiet
arithmetic of mutual care: a penny a week from every master for the craft's
"decayed brethren", and, when the barrel-wood came up the Clyde, a division of
the timber "to the poor of the said calling als weill as the rich alyk without
respect of persones." Tightness outside, tightness inside. The barrel and the
brotherhood are the same object: separate staves, bound by a hoop, useless
alone, watertight together.

## History (founding, seal of cause date, key episodes)

- **Before 1551 — inside the masons' house.** Coopering existed in Glasgow long
  before incorporation; the craft "goes back to ancient times and was an
  essential part of the life of every old burgh in Scotland"
  ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).
- **14 October 1551 — the composite seal of cause.** The Provost and bailies
  granted a Seal of Cause to the "Masons, Coopers, Slaters, Sawyers, and
  Quarriers dwelling within the burgh" — one umbrella craft for stone and wood.
  Its articles supported the **altar of St Thomas**, fixed seven-year
  apprenticeships, Saturday-afternoon inspection of work, upset (entry) fees of
  20s with 10s per apprentice to the altar, and penalties paid in **a pound of
  wax to the altar** ([British History Online, Abstracts of Glasgow charters,
  entry 323](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).
- **27 April 1569 — the Coopers' own charter.** Nine years after the Scottish
  Reformation, the bailies and council ratified separate articles for the
  coopers — the Coopers left the composite craft thirty-one years before the
  Wrights did (1600). The articles mirror the Hammermen's (1536) and Masons'
  (1551) seals, with one epochal change: payments now go "to the common charges
  of the craft and the relief of its decayed brethren, and not to the upkeep of
  the altar as is the case in the earlier documents." An increased upset of £6
  for an unfreeman's son and £4 for a freeman's son "had to come in place of
  the dinner and banquet which was formerly in use to be made" ([BHO, entry
  345](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).
  (Crawfurd's 1858 *Sketch* twice dates the disjunction "1567" in passing but
  gives the seal of cause itself as 27 April 1569 — the charter date stands.)
- **The 1569 working rules.** "Upon each Saturday at evening, three or four of
  the worthiest and most perfect craftsmen … shall pass and search and visit
  all men's work" to see it be "sufficient and good and able for serving our
  sovereign lord's lieges or not"; apprenticeships no less than seven years; no
  craftsman to keep more than one apprentice at a time; a weekly levy of 1d per
  craftsman for the poor; entrants to be of good moral character, "in a visible
  way of supporting himself", and a burgess of Glasgow
  ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).
- **1573 — the national gauge.** Parliament's Measures Act fixed the vessels the
  craft lived by: "euerie salmond barrel … sall contene twelf gallounis of the
  Striuiling pynt and … euerie barrel of hering & quhite fische contene nyne
  gallounis" (Acts of the Parliaments of Scotland III 82/2, quoted in
  [DOST, *salmond-barrell*](https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/salmond_barrell)).
  The salmon barrel had been 14 gallons c.1500 — "the barell bind of salmond
  suld kepe & contene the assise & mesour of xiiij gallonis"
  ([DOST, *barrell*](https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/barrell_n)) — and would
  shrink again to ten by 1685; a Scots cooper's working life was framed by
  statute gallons.
- **1587 — the deacon roll opens.** The Incorporation's own list of past
  deacons begins with **Gillane Snype, elected 1587** — the first of a Snype
  dynasty (Gillane, Robert, Andrew, William, Phillane) that recurs in office
  into the 1650s ([Coopers past-deacons roll, Trades House Digital Library
  PDF](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_coopers_~_deacons.pdf)).
- **February 1605 — twenty-three coopers.** The burgess roll drawn up after the
  decreet arbitral divided Glasgow into 213 merchant-rank and 363 trades-rank
  burgesses; the Coopers counted 23 — a mid-sized craft beside the Tailors' 65
  and above the Masons' 11 ([Crawfurd, *Sketch of the Rise and Progress of the
  Trades' House* (1858), archive.org](https://archive.org/details/asketchriseandp00crawgoog)).
- **1605 — the Letter of Guildry.** The Coopers became one of the fourteen
  incorporated crafts under the new Trades House; "David Scheirar for Johnne
  Hall dekin of ye cowperis" appears at the Deacon Convener's council on 4
  October 1605 ([Lumsden, *Records of the Trades House of Glasgow 1605–1678*,
  archive.org](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **1607 — buying into brotherhood.** On 23 April 1607 "Jo. Richie for himself
  and in ye nae of ye cowperis his brether of craft promeisis to ye buylding
  fordwart of ye craftis hospitall the sowme of twentie pundis money" — the
  Coopers' subscription to the crafts' almshouse. In the hospital's first
  contribution roll ("The hospitallis new Rentell callit ye craftis
  cotributioun") the Coopers stand at £8 — level with the Wrights, half the
  Hammermen's £20 (same source; the roll is undated in the extract).
- **1609 — a cooper leads all the trades.** Thomas Morrison ("Thomas Morsoun
  dekin of ye cowperis" in the 1607 records) became Deacon Convener of the
  Trades House in 1609 — within four years of the House's creation — and held
  the office again in 1610, 1612 and 1620; David Shearer, Cooper, followed in
  1624 ([Trades House Digital Library, Deacon Convenors list
  PDF](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/deacon-convenors.html)).
- **29 May 1613 — the drum proclamation.** The burgh ordained "ane publicatioun
  to be maid be sound of drum throw this burcht commanding the couparis within
  the samyn to mak thair barrellis of the jadge and mesour of Leyth" — Glasgow's
  coopers ordered, by drumbeat through the streets, onto the east-coast
  standard, on pain of confiscation ([BHO, Glasgow burgh records vol. 1,
  1613](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol1/pp335-338)).
- **11 February 1630 — the search turns violent.** Deacon Robert Snype,
  searching "be vertew of ane chartour grantit be the towne to the haill
  cowperis … to schewe all and quhatsumever wnsufficient work", confiscated "ane
  wnsufficient kirne" (churn) from Gabriel Allasoun — who "vterit manie
  injurious speitchis", then "pullit wp his hand and hatt the said deikin on ye
  halfett ctrar his aithe of obedience." Gabriel confessed he did it "out of
  his druckin humor"; the House sentenced him to make public "homadge to ye
  deikin at ye mercat croce", confessing his fault and "crawing God and his
  deikin pardoun", and to pay "to ye puir of ye decayit breyr of ye cowper
  craft ane new upsett" ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **1635 — the craft under arms.** "James Pollok deacone of cowperis producit
  and gave in … ane muscatt with staf and bandeleiris conforme to ye ordo'" —
  each craft's deacon owed a musket to the hospital armoury; a 1638 survey
  counted "threttie fyve musquattis w' stafis and bandelleirs" there. In 1646
  "Cuthbert Grege lait dekine of cowperis gave in y muskats onlie bot not y
  bandaliers" (same source).
- **1638 — the decreet of the venture goods.** Eight named cooper burgesses
  complained against their own deacon, James Pollok, that the old custom — all
  barrel-timber "imbrought … be watter" being "equallie delt to the poor of the
  said vocatioun als weill as to the richt … without respect of persone" — had
  been corrupted by "the specialls and od men of the sd traide combynyng
  togidder", who took "sumtymes thrie tymes and vther tymes four tymes als
  mutch as they give to ony ane of the poor britherine", so that the poor "ar
  forcit to sit ydle" and buy timber back "att ane verie deir rait." The House's
  decreet restored the old equality: the deacon with "twa or thrie of the honest
  men" must buy all venture goods for the whole trade, every brother warned by
  the craft's officer to the division, each to his "lott or kavill", and a poor
  brother without ready money free to sell his share (same source).
- **1639 — the last pauper.** With John Wyllie placed in the hospital, "ye
  cowperis hes nae mae poor to sustein bot ye sd Dauid Wilsoun"; the House
  reassigned the freed pensions and "ordanit the deacon of couperis to sie ye
  said Dauid Wilsoun honestlie sustenit qlk he faithfully promeist to doe"
  (same source).
- **1643 — the drinking rebuke.** It was "maid manifest and known … that the
  cowpers spent thair wnlawis and wther goodis of ye calling ydllie in drinking
  quhilkis sould be bestowed on the poor and wther pius usuis to the great
  offence of God hurt and prejudice of ye poor"; the House ordered the coopers'
  deacon, present and future, to account yearly for every fine before the
  Convener (same source).
- **1645–46 — the plague blank.** The Coopers' deacon roll records no election
  for two successive years, annotated "Not given (plague?)" — Glasgow's plague
  years leave a hole in the craft's own memory
  ([past-deacons roll](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_coopers_~_deacons.pdf)).
- **1649 — the deacon who kept the box.** "Becaus Cuthbert Greg late deikin of
  ye cowperis hes refuisit to rander ye box being requyrit severall tymes be
  the officer … and refuiset to come heir this day being warnit", the House
  asked the magistrates "to put him in waird q[uhill] he be sensible of his
  fault", fined him ten pounds to the poor, and barred him from office until
  satisfaction ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
  The deacon roll nevertheless shows Greig back in office in later years.
- **1653 — the gauge scandal.** The burgh records: "It being regraited and
  compleanit vpon be sundrie that thair wer many herring barrellis brocht to
  the towne that wer not the full gedge, contrair to the proclamatioune givin
  out thairanent, to the great hurt and prejudice of all persones buyers
  thairof, the saidis magistratis and counsell thairfor hes ordanit the water
  bailyie wha hes now chairge of the gedge to confiscat all barrellis who ar
  not the full gedge…" (29 January 1653, [BHO, Glasgow burgh records vol.
  2](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol2/pp253-282)).
  Short measure in a barrel was a civic offence, policed at the waterside.
- **1665 — the year of the common bargain.** Three linked acts: (1) on a
  complaint that at entry "ther was too much exacted aff them ther being no
  constant pryce sett downe", the House statuted "saxteine pund sail be all
  that sail be exacted heirafter" from burgess and freemen's sons; (2) John
  Gemmill, caught "buying of runges in Dumbartone" privately, submitted to a
  fine and to the craft "wnder the paine of fourtie pundes to be applyet for
  the vse of ther poor", and *every cooper entered and to be entered* had to
  subscribe the act "that non of them sail buy runges abrod aither in burghe or
  landwart but what sail be a commoune bargane to the haill tred"; (3) on 16
  May, "all staves girthes stepes and wthers of that kind imported at the
  Broomelaw be made ane commoune bargane amongest the haill calling and each
  brother to have his cavell thairof" — he who owed for former lots "shall have
  non at all" ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **1687–1695 — Parliament against the town.** The craft secured an act of the
  Lords of Treasury and Exchequer (15 July 1687), further town-council
  regulations (15 May 1691), then a full parliamentary ratification (William
  II, 1695, c. 138) which "ratified and confirmed the hail ancient rights,
  liberties, and privileges of the incorporation and trade of the coupars of
  Glasgow" and — in Cleland's summary — "discharg[ed] all country coopers to
  interfere with the trade. The Town of Glasgow protest against it"
  ([Crawfurd](https://archive.org/details/asketchriseandp00crawgoog);
  [Cleland, *Annals of Glasgow* vol. 2](https://archive.org/details/b29329450_0002)).
  The coopers went over their own magistrates' heads to Parliament, and won.
- **1776–1778 — the precedence lawsuit.** The four "premier" crafts
  (Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners, Maltmen) sued "the last ten trades" —
  Coopers included — before the Court of Session over the order of precedence.
  The Decreet of Declarator (1776, notarial copy 1777) fixed the order:
  "Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners, Maltmen (the four premier Crafts), Weavers,
  Baxters, Skinners, Wrights, Coopers, Fleshers, Masons, Gardeners, Barbers and
  Bonnetmakers" — Coopers ninth ([Muir, *The Hammermen of Glasgow* insignia
  volume (1939), PDF](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/3.-Traditions-and-Customs-of-the-Hammermen-of-Glasgow-and-Insignia-and-Relics-%E2%80%93-of-the-The-Incorporation-of-Hammermen-1939-.pdf)).
  Note the sting: ninth in precedence despite holding the eighth-oldest charter
  (1569 beats the Wrights' 1600 and the Maltmen's 1605)
  ([incorporation dates table](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
- **1781–1796 — coopers at the top of the boom.** Three cooper Deacon
  Conveners in fifteen years (R. Auchincloss 1781–82, W. Auchincloss 1793–94,
  Robert Robertson 1795–96) — exactly the decades when Glasgow's Atlantic
  trade ran on coopered containers: tobacco hogsheads, sugar and rum casks,
  herring barrels (Deacon Convenors list; Crawfurd's convener roll agrees, and
  adds Robert Hood 1819–20 and John Stewart 1847–48).
- **1791–1794 — the small stave in the great Hall.** When the Adam-designed
  Trades Hall was built, the Coopers subscribed £71 16s 2d — beside the
  Maltmen's £611 and the Tailors' £509, and above only the Masons, Gardeners
  and Dyers; their cumulative Hall contribution to 1840 was £87 7s 5d. Yet the
  House ledger of 1857 shows £600 standing in the Coopers' name — the craft was
  never rich, and never absent
  ([Crawfurd](https://archive.org/details/asketchriseandp00crawgoog)).
- **1846 — the monopoly ends.** Like every Scottish craft, the Coopers lost
  their exclusive rights in the mid-nineteenth century; anyone could now make a
  barrel in Glasgow without joining ([Trades House —
  Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/); the Wrights' page
  names the 1846 Act,
  [Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/)).
  A cooper, John Stewart, sat as Deacon Convener of all the trades in 1847–48,
  the first years after abolition (Crawfurd). The Incorporation pivoted to
  charity and education — it funded scholars in the Trades Free School
  (1873–76) and kept pension rolls into the twentieth century
  ([Trades House Digital Library — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/coopers.html)).
  The Victorian deacon roll reads like a map of the working river — Dunlop
  Street, Jamaica Street, Gallowgate, Oswald Street, Kelvinhaugh — and one
  deacon of 1856, John King Jnr, is marked simply "America. Died: 23 February
  1899" ([past-deacons roll](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_coopers_~_deacons.pdf)).
- **26 June 1924 — arms matriculated** with the Lord Lyon (incorporation dates
  table, above). The Hammermen had led the way in 1918; Muir records that
  before then "none of the Glasgow Incorporations have registered their Arms"
  ([Muir 1939](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/3.-Traditions-and-Customs-of-the-Hammermen-of-Glasgow-and-Insignia-and-Relics-%E2%80%93-of-the-The-Incorporation-of-Hammermen-1939-.pdf)).
- **2008 — royal cooper.** HRH Prince Edward (Earl of Wessex; in Scotland,
  Earl of Forfar) accepted Honorary Membership and lends his name to the Earl
  of Forfar Awards for apprentice coopers
  ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).
- **2014 — the roll changes voice.** Margaret McCafferty's election as Deacon
  puts the first woman's name on a roll that opens in 1587; Primrose Stark
  (2016) and Sheonagh Beaton (2019) follow. The roll's last annotations echo
  its oldest: John F. MacLeod's entries for 2020 and 2021 read "No image due to
  COVID" — a pandemic blank to answer the plague blank of 1645
  ([past-deacons roll](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_coopers_~_deacons.pdf)).
- **Today.** Deacon Gavin Queen, Collector Jim Mearns, Clerk Thomas W.
  Monteith; charity SC004620 at 9 George Square. The Incorporation still elects
  four members to the Trades House, keeps ties with the Worshipful Company of
  Coopers of London and other Scottish cooper bodies, sponsors a Wine and
  Spirit Education Trust scholarship whose winner tours Scotland's distilleries
  and cooperages, and supports the living trade "through links with established
  cooperages" ([Incorporation of Coopers of Glasgow](https://www.incorporationofcoopersofglasgow.org.uk/);
  [About](https://www.incorporationofcoopersofglasgow.org.uk/about.php)).

## Legends & Episodes (the memorable stuff a storyteller would steal)

- **The proxy who became the chief.** In October 1605, at the very first
  meetings of the new Trades House, the coopers' deacon John Hall did not
  appear in person — "David Scheirar" stood in for him. Nineteen years later a
  David Shearer, Cooper, sat in the Convener's chair ruling all fourteen crafts
  (1624). The errand-man outlived and outrose the master he answered for
  ([Lumsden records](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas);
  [Deacon Convenors list](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/deacon-convenors.html)).
  The Coopers' own deacon roll shows a David Shearer as craft deacon from 1603
  — so the proxy of 1605 was already a past deacon standing in for the current
  one. [extrapolated: that the 1605 proxy and the 1624 convener are the same
  man is an inference from the name and dates, not proven.]
- **Thomas Morrison, the four-time Convener.** A cooper held the third-highest
  civic office in Glasgow four times between 1609 and 1620, when the House was
  young and the merchant Dean of Guild still looked down on craftsmen. For a
  craft ranked ninth, this is the founding "we were there first" boast.
- **The blow at the halfett (1630).** Deacon Robert Snype seized an
  insufficient churn from Gabriel Allasoun's shed under the charter's search
  right; Gabriel, "out of his druckin humor", cursed him and struck him "on ye
  halfett" — the side of the head — "ctrar his aithe of obedience." The
  sentence was theatre: public homage at the mercat croce, pardon craved of God
  and deacon, and a whole new upset fee paid to the craft's decayed brethren.
  The search was sacred enough that striking the searcher meant re-buying your
  place in the brotherhood
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **The rich men and the river timber (1638).** Eight coopers put their names
  to a bill against their own deacon: the "specialls and od men" had cornered
  the venture timber coming up the Clyde, taking three and four shares to a
  poor man's one, till the poor sat idle and bought their own trade's wood back
  "att ane verie deir rait." The House sided with the eight — every brother,
  "the poor … als weill as the rich alyk without respect of persones", got his
  cavell of every cargo. A guild-sized rehearsal of every argument about
  capital and commons since (same source).
- **The barrels that bought Parliament.** Clyde herring "were larger, firmer,
  and of a better taste" than others, and Glasgow's magistrates "often sent
  presents of barrels of herring to persons in London and Edinburgh whom they
  wished to influence" to keep the fishing free of monopoly — the cooper's
  tight barrel as an instrument of civic lobbying ([Smith, *Greenock and Port
  Glasgow: Two Ports in Self-Created Storms*,
  PDF](https://s3-eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3.spanglefish.com/s/38288/documents/john-s/two-ports-in-self-created-storms-greenock-and-port-glasgow-finished-1.pdf)).
- **The water bailie and the short barrels (1653).** After public outcry over
  herring barrels "not the full gedge", the magistrates set the water bailie to
  confiscate every undersized barrel at the quay. Somewhere in that story is a
  cooper who shaved an inch — and every honest cooper whose brand was cheapened
  by him ([BHO burgh records](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol2/pp253-282)).
- **The craft that drank its fines (1643).** The House put it on record that
  the coopers "spent thair wnlawis … ydllie in drinking quhilkis sould be
  bestowed on the poor … to the great offence of God", and condemned the
  deacon to annual audit forever after. The brotherhood's own court, writing
  the brotherhood's shame into the minute-book
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **The deacon who would not give up the Box (1649).** Cuthbert Greig, late
  deacon, refused the officer three times, refused the summons, and kept the
  craft's box — charters, bonds, money, memory — until the House asked the
  magistrates to ward him "quhill he be sensible of his fault." No reason for
  his refusal survives. The roll shows the craft elected him deacon again
  afterwards anyway (same source; past-deacons roll).
- **The Gemmill bond (1665).** John Gemmill bought barrel-rungs privately at
  Dumbarton; the craft fined him, bound him under £40 penalty, and then made
  *every cooper present and future* subscribe the act: no man buys wood "aither
  in burghe or landwart but what sail be a commoune bargane to the haill tred."
  One man's sharp dealing became a signature every apprentice would set his
  name under at entry (same source).
- **The dinner that became a poor-fund.** The 1569 charter abolished the new
  member's obligatory "dinner and banquet" and converted it into entry money
  for the craft's common purse and its "decayed brethren" — the Reformation in
  miniature: feast into fund, altar-wax into widow's coal
  ([BHO charters abstracts](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).
- **Parliament against the Provost (1695).** The Coopers took their privileges
  to the Parliament of Scotland and won a ratification "discharging all country
  coopers to interfere with the trade" — and Glasgow's own town council
  formally protested against its own craft's triumph. The hoop drawn so tight
  even the burgh outside it objected
  ([Crawfurd](https://archive.org/details/asketchriseandp00crawgoog);
  [Cleland](https://archive.org/details/b29329450_0002)).
- **The lawsuit over ninth place (1776).** The Coopers spent money and years as
  defenders in a Court of Session action about *walking order* — and the
  decree that fixed them ninth was so treasured by the winners that the
  Hammermen kept a 742-page notarial copy "lodged in the box" and later in a
  bank vault ([Muir 1939](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/3.-Traditions-and-Customs-of-the-Hammermen-of-Glasgow-and-Insignia-and-Relics-%E2%80%93-of-the-The-Incorporation-of-Hammermen-1939-.pdf)).
- **Another craft guards the Coopers' patent.** Muir's 1939 inventory of the
  Hammermen's relic box lists, as Appendix II item 14: "Facsimile of Patent of
  Arms of Incorporation of Coopers" — filed between the Hammermen's superseded
  seal and a musket-prize medal of 1804. The ancient rival-kin crafts literally
  keep copies of each other's honours (same source, verbatim).
- **84,782 barrels (1798).** A Parliamentary Committee found herring exports
  to the West Indies had risen to 84,782 barrels a year, 61% of them Scottish,
  shipped mostly from the Clyde at Greenock — salted herring to feed enslaved
  people on the plantations. Every one of those barrels was a cooper's day's
  work; the craft's prosperity was hooped to the darkest trade of its age
  ([The Scotsman](https://www.scotsman.com/heritage-and-retro/heritage/how-scots-fishing-towns-boomed-from-sale-of-salted-herring-to-slave-plantations-2936978)).
- **The stockbroker cooper who ran Govan.** James Kirkwood, admitted "Cooper
  Burgess and Guild Brother … at Far Hand" in 1890, Deacon of the Coopers 1899
  (the roll places him at Carling Lodge, Ibrox), Provost of Govan for ten years
  (police station, town hall and public baths built in his tenure), Deacon
  Convener 1905–06, presenter of addresses to King Edward VII in 1903
  ([Bryce, *The Story of Deacon Convener Provost James Kirkwood*, Trades House
  Digital Library PDF](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/deacon-convenors.html)).
- **A cooper led the trades into the Second World War.** James Leggat, Cooper,
  was Deacon Convener 1939–40; another cooper, Robert Davidson, had held the
  chair through the aftermath of the First (1919–20) (Deacon Convenors list;
  both appear as craft deacons on the past-deacons roll, Leggat in 1933–34,
  Davidson in 1910).
- **The two blank pages.** The deacon roll's only silences are eloquent:
  1645–46, "Not given (plague?)"; 2020–21, "No image due to COVID." Three
  hundred and seventy-five years apart, the craft's memory keeps the same kind
  of wound ([past-deacons roll](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/incorporation_of_coopers_~_deacons.pdf)).
- **The apprentice in the barrel.** The trade's initiation — "trussing-in",
  now usually "the blackening" — survives in Scottish cooperages: at the end of
  his time the apprentice is stripped, doused in a "muck" of flour, treacle,
  soot, feathers, shavings and beer, sealed into the cask he has just built,
  and rolled across the yard to the ringing of hammers on quarter hoops; he
  climbs out a journeyman and is stood a dram. The custom is traced to the
  fourteenth century and still performed at Speyside Cooperage
  ([Cooper King Distillery](https://www.cooperkingdistillery.co.uk/blog/cooper-trussing-in-ceremony);
  [Malt Whisky Trail — A Cooper's Blackening](https://maltwhiskytrail.com/a-coopers-blackening/);
  [The Woodworker on Theakston's ceremony](https://www.thewoodworkermag.com/theakston-brewery-hold-once-generation-trussing-ceremony)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto (heraldry, patron saint, motto with translation, symbolism)

- **Patron (pre-Reformation): St Thomas.** The 1551 composite seal of cause
  that covered the coopers maintained the altar of St Thomas, funded by entry
  fees and fines paid in wax ([BHO charters
  abstracts](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).
  The Coopers' own 1569 charter, arriving after the Reformation, pointedly
  redirects that money to the poor — the Glasgow Coopers are, in charter terms,
  a *post-saint* craft: their devotion was transferred from altar to poor-box
  at birth. (Cooper patronage varied by burgh: Edinburgh's cooper craft, joined
  to Mary's Chapel in 1489, served "Sanct Jhonis altar situat in our College
  Kirk of Sanct Gele" and owed "the oukly penny to God and to Sanct Jhone"
  [comparative — Edinburgh;
  [Colston](https://archive.org/details/incorporatedtrad00cols)].)
- **Arms.** Matriculated with the Lord Lyon on **26 June 1924**
  ([incorporation dates table](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)) —
  six years after the Hammermen became the first Glasgow incorporation to
  register (1918), at a time when, per Muir, "none of the Glasgow
  Incorporations have registered their Arms." The arms appear around the dome
  of the Grand Hall of the Trades Hall, on the fourteen silver shields of the
  Deacon Convener's Chair of State, on the carved oak benches of the entrance
  hall, and (in older, pre-Lyon form) in the 1888 stained-glass window on the
  Trades Hall stair
  ([Trades House Virtual Museum](https://www.tradeshousemuseum.org/coopers.html);
  [Visitors' Guide to Scotland — Trades Hall](https://www.visitorsguidetoscotland.com/2024/12/glasgows-trades-hall/)).
  **The precise blazon and motto of the Glasgow Coopers could not be verified
  from any online source consulted** — the museum pages show the arms only as
  images, and heraldry-wiki was unreachable (expired certificate). The Public
  Register of All Arms and Bearings is imaged online only to 1924 via
  ScotlandsPeople — the 26 June 1924 matriculation sits at the very edge of
  that coverage and is the natural next research step
  ([ScotlandsPeople guide](https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/guides/coats-arms)).
  A facsimile of the Coopers' Patent of Arms is preserved in the Hammermen's
  relic box (Muir 1939, Appendix II item 14).
- **Kin heraldry (for flavour, clearly labelled as London's, not Glasgow's).**
  The Worshipful Company of Coopers of London (grant of 1509) bears gyronny
  gules and sable, a chevron with **annulets** — rings, for the hoops that bind
  the cask — between **adzes** (the cooper's axe) and a chief of **lilies** for
  the Virgin; its pre-Reformation motto *Gaude Maria Virgo* ("Rejoice, Virgin
  Mary") was replaced after the Reformation with **"Love as Brethren"**
  ([Worshipful Company of Coopers](https://coopers-hall.co.uk/history/the-companys-history);
  heraldic details via [Heraldry of the World](https://www.heraldry-wiki.com/heraldrywiki/index.php?title=Worshipful_Company_of_Coopers)).
  The symbolism is the craft's own catechism: the ring that holds, the blade
  that shapes, brotherhood as the motto. [extrapolated: Glasgow's emblem, as
  reproduced in Trades Hall imagery, follows the same visual vocabulary of
  barrel and cooper's tools, but no verified blazon is claimed here.]

## Rituals & Customs (oaths, essays, feast days, precedence, relics, insignia)

- **The Saturday search.** "Three or four of the worthiest and most perfect
  craftsmen" inspected all members' work every Saturday evening — peer
  judgment, weekly, forever, written into the founding charter
  ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).
  The 1630 Snype case shows the search in action: deacon and masters entering a
  brother's premises, seizing an insufficient churn, and the House enforcing
  the searcher's authority with public penance
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **The oath of obedience.** The 1630 sentence turns on Gabriel Allasoun
  striking his deacon "ctrar his aithe of obedience" — entry to the craft bound
  a man by oath to his deacon, and breach of it was the charge, more than the
  blow (same source).
- **The penny to the poor.** A weekly levy of 1d from every craftsman for the
  support of the craft's poor (Trades House); the 1569 charter's phrase is
  relief of "decayed brethren"
  ([BHO](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).
- **The cavell — timber divided by lot.** From 1638 (re-enacted 1665) every
  cargo of stave-wood landed at the Broomielaw was bought as "ane commoune
  bargane amongest the haill calling", the officer warned every brother to the
  division, and "each brother [had] his cavell thairof" — his lot, poor and
  rich alike. A brother who couldn't pay could sell his lot at a profit; a
  brother who owed for old lots got none
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **Entry.** Seven years' apprenticeship minimum; one apprentice per master at
  a time; entrant must be a Glasgow burgess, of good moral character, "in a
  visible way of supporting himself"; upset fees graded by blood — £4 for a
  freeman's son, £6 for an unfreeman's son — the differential that replaced the
  old entry banquet (Trades House; BHO). Indentures could run longer than the
  minimum: in 1630 John Paterson was booked to Daniel Anderson, cooper, "for ye
  space of aucht zeiris as prenteis and ae zeir for meit and fie" — eight years
  bound, plus a ninth worked for his keep (Lumsden). By 1665 entry-money
  gouging forced a cap: "saxteine pund sail be all that sail be exacted
  heirafter" from freemen's sons (Lumsden). Entry categories by blood, marriage
  or purchase persisted: James Kirkwood entered "at Far Hand" (no family claim)
  in 1890 ([Kirkwood biography PDF](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/deacon-convenors.html)).
- **The essay.** [extrapolated for Glasgow] Like other Scottish incorporations,
  entrants by craft were expected to prove themselves by an essay (trial
  masterpiece); no Coopers-of-Glasgow essay text was found online. The Aberdeen
  Wrights and Coopers' essay is documented and gives the likely shape
  [comparative]: "ane salmond salt for holding four barrels of salmond, or ane
  three boll salt for brewing; ane firlot and peck, ane salmond barrell conform
  to bind and measure of Aberdeen, the goadges made for that effect to be given
  to him be the deacon and masters" — note that the lawful *measures
  themselves* (firlot and peck) were cooper's work, made against gauges issued
  by the deacon. Aberdeen's essay masters were "strictly enjoined to examine
  the work without speaking to each other, and declare their voices to the
  clerk and deacon, so that the one could not know what the verdict of the
  other was", could lock the candidate "into a stranger's workshop while he is
  at work", and were themselves fined four pounds Scots for failure of duty
  ([Bain, *Merchant and Craft Guilds* (1887), p. 250](https://archive.org/details/merchantcraftgui00bainuoft)).
- **The Box.** The craft's chest of money, papers and relics — the Digital
  Library preserves the Coopers' "box opening minutes 1904–1964", and the
  ceremonial opening of the box was a working ritual into living memory
  ([Trades House Digital Library — incorporations page](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/incorporations.html)).
  What the box meant is measured by 1649: when a late deacon refused to hand it
  back, the House had him warded by the magistrates and barred from office
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)).
- **The musket in the hospital.** Each deacon owed the crafts' armoury a musket
  "with staf and bandeleiris conforme to ye ordo'"; surveys counted them (35 in
  1638), and outgoing deacons were chased for arrears — Cuthbert Greig returned
  "y muskats onlie bot not y bandaliers" in 1646. The craft was a militia unit
  as well as a trade (same source).
- **Trussing-in / the blackening.** The apprentice's barrel-rolling initiation
  at the close of his time (see Legends) — a trade custom documented in
  Scottish and English cooperages, traced to the fourteenth century, and still
  performed at Speyside Cooperage: buckets of flour, treacle and molasses,
  feathers, the last cask the apprentice made, and a dram at the end
  ([Cooper King Distillery](https://www.cooperkingdistillery.co.uk/blog/cooper-trussing-in-ceremony);
  [Malt Whisky Trail](https://maltwhiskytrail.com/a-coopers-blackening/)).
- **The croze at completion.** Today's Earl of Forfar Awards ritualise the
  toolchest: presentation of cooper's tools to newly accepted apprentices; *The
  Cooper and his Trade* to second-years; the Incorporation's own history to
  third-years; and **a croze — the tool that cuts the groove holding the cask
  head — to those who complete their apprenticeship**
  ([Incorporation of Coopers — About](https://www.incorporationofcoopersofglasgow.org.uk/about.php)).
- **Precedence.** Ninth of the fourteen crafts, fixed by the 1776 Decreet of
  Declarator; four members elected to the Trades House
  ([Muir 1939](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/3.-Traditions-and-Customs-of-the-Hammermen-of-Glasgow-and-Insignia-and-Relics-%E2%80%93-of-the-The-Incorporation-of-Hammermen-1939-.pdf);
  [Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).
- **Wax fines and the altar.** Before 1560, infractions in the composite craft
  cost a pound of wax to St Thomas's altar — sin literally burned off in
  candlelight ([BHO](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-charters/1175-1649/no1/pp3-27)).

## Sensory World (materials, tools, sounds, smells, the physical texture)

- **Materials.** Oak staves split and shaved; iron and wooden hoops; the
  imported "ventour guidis" of the trade — "stappis rungis stingis and vtheris"
  landed by water at the Broomielaw and divided by lot
  ([Lumsden](https://archive.org/details/recordsoftradesh00glas)); reeds/flag
  for caulking seams [extrapolated: standard cooperage practice]; brine, salt
  and fish-oil in the herring yards; later, sherry- and bourbon-soaked oak in
  the whisky cooperages.
- **Tools with Scots names.** "The croze board and the adze; borer, bick iron
  and belly knife; scullup, shiv and swarth" — a working cooper uses more than
  twenty tools, and "coopers on the east and west coasts of Scotland have
  different names for their tools"
  ([Inside the Cask — The Auld Art of Coopering](https://insidethecask.com/2019/01/08/the-auld-art-of-coopering/)).
- **The gauge.** The measuring life of the craft: statute gallons of the
  Stirling pint (twelve to a salmon barrel, nine to a herring barrel, by the
  Act of 1573), the burgh's "gedge" enforced at the quay, the drum of 1613
  ordering Glasgow barrels onto "the jadge and mesour of Leyth", and — in
  Aberdeen — "goadges" issued by the deacon for the essay itself
  ([DOST](https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/salmond_barrell);
  [BHO 1613](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/glasgow-burgh-records/vol1/pp335-338);
  [Bain](https://archive.org/details/merchantcraftgui00bainuoft)).
- **Sounds.** The "ringing of axes" that opens a trussing-in ceremony
  ([Cooper King Distillery](https://www.cooperkingdistillery.co.uk/blog/cooper-trussing-in-ceremony));
  the town drum crying the coopers' orders through the streets (1613); the
  drumbeat of driving hoops, the hollow boom of an empty cask rolled on
  cobbles [extrapolated]; the silence a cooper listens for — a tight cask
  doesn't drip.
- **Smells and heat.** Steam and scorched oak when a cask is fired into its
  bend; "Pompey juice", the reeking goo steam-cleaned out of old beer barrels
  ([Cooper King Distillery](https://www.cooperkingdistillery.co.uk/blog/cooper-trussing-in-ceremony));
  flour, treacle and molasses in the blackening buckets
  ([Malt Whisky Trail](https://maltwhiskytrail.com/a-coopers-blackening/));
  fresh shavings ankle-deep; the salt-and-gut stench of a curing yard where
  around 2,000 coopers served the fishing industry in the 1860s
  ([Artisans in Scotland](https://artisansinscotland.wordpress.com/2016/04/15/april-coopers-ca-1889/)).
- **The body.** Work is done bent over the cask, the wood held between knee
  and chest; the trade is learned young and kept for life, "skills that are
  passed down through generations"
  ([Inside the Cask](https://insidethecask.com/2019/01/08/the-auld-art-of-coopering/)).
  Scotland still had 184 coopers and 37 apprentices in 2018, nearly all in
  whisky (same source); modern apprenticeships run four years, typically from
  sixteen ([Malt Whisky Trail](https://maltwhiskytrail.com/a-coopers-blackening/)).

## Values (5-7 core values, each with WHY rooted in evidence)

1. **Tightness — work that holds.** The founding charter's only aesthetic is
   sufficiency: work "sufficient and good and able for serving our sovereign
   lord's lieges." A barrel is pass/fail; there is no decorative cooperage. The
   1630 case shows the standard enforced to the point of violence and penance.
2. **Honest measure.** The 1573 statute gallons, the 1613 drum ordering the
   Leith gauge, the 1653 confiscations: a Glasgow barrel *is* the quantity it
   claims. Short measure was not sharp practice but civic betrayal.
3. **Peer accountability.** The Saturday-evening search — the worthiest
   craftsmen inspecting their brothers' work weekly — made judgment by equals a
   sacrament of the craft from 1551 onward; and when the coopers themselves
   failed (drinking their fines, 1643), the same machinery of peer audit was
   turned on the deacon.
4. **Brotherhood as infrastructure.** The penny-a-week poor fund, the £20
   pledge to the crafts' hospital (1607), the "decayed brethren" clause, the
   cavell dividing every timber cargo "to the poor … als weill as the rich alyk
   without respect of persones" (1638), the deacon's personal promise to keep
   David Wilson "honestlie sustenit" (1639), the pension visitation records
   into the 1940s: mutual relief is the craft's longest-running product,
   outlasting the barrels themselves.
5. **Time-served patience.** Seven years minimum (eight-plus-one in the 1630
   Paterson indenture), one apprentice per master, the croze handed over only
   at completion — mastery is not talent but endured time, and the trussing
   ritual marks the crossing like a birth.
6. **Usefulness without glory.** The cooper's work is a container for other
   trades' goods — herring, tobacco, ale, whisky. The craft's self-worth is
   service that disappears into everyone else's success; its Trades Hall
   subscription was among the smallest, and its men sat in the Convener's chair
   anyway.
7. **Adaptive survival.** Left the masons' umbrella early (1569), rode
   herring, then Atlantic trade, then whisky; took its privileges to Parliament
   when the town wavered (1695); and — when the monopoly died in 1846 — remade
   itself as a charity whose "membership is bigger than ever"
   ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/)).

## Shadow (the craft's virtue curdled into failure)

The hoop that binds is also the hoop that constricts, and the Coopers' shadow
is unusually well documented — by their own court. **Tightness curdles into
the closed shop**: the same charter that guaranteed quality also priced
outsiders' sons at half again the entry fee of insiders' and made burgess-ship
a wall; by 1695 the craft had Parliament itself "discharging all country
coopers to interfere with the trade" — quality control perfected into a
monopoly so hard the town protested. **Honest measure curdles into the
informer's eye**: a craft built on weekly inspection of brothers breeds men who
watch each other for the joy of the fault found — and sometimes the watched man
swings first, as Gabriel Allasoun did at his deacon's head in 1630.
**Brotherhood curdles into the syndicate**: the 1638 bill shows the "specialls
and od men" cornering the river timber against their own poor brethren until
the House forced the cavell back on them; and in 1643 the craft stood accused
of drinking the very fines owed to its poor, "to the great offence of God."
**Brotherhood curdles into moral blindness** on the grand scale too: the
poor-box filled no matter what the barrels carried, and by 1798 tens of
thousands of Clyde barrels a year carried salt herring to feed enslaved people
on West Indian plantations — the craft's charity hooped to humanity's worst
trade, and no minute of protest survives. **Office curdles into possession**:
Cuthbert Greig held the craft's own box hostage against his brethren in 1649.
**Pride of place curdles into litigation over shadows**: in 1776, with the
industrial age about to make their monopoly meaningless, the crafts spent years
and fortunes suing one another over who walked ninth in a procession. And
**belonging curdles into ordeal**: the trussing that welcomes a journeyman is,
seen coldly, the brotherhood filthying a man in front of his family and calling
it love — the initiation's warmth and its humiliation are the same act.

## Rivals & Kin (clashes, marriages, demarcation)

- **Kin: Masons and Wrights.** Born of the same 1551 composite seal of cause
  (with slaters, sawyers and quarriers). The Coopers left first (1569), the
  Wrights in 1600 — the Trades House still describes the three as "brother
  Craftsmen" ([Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/)).
  [extrapolated: demarcation friction between coopers and wrights over wooden
  vessels was common in Scottish burghs, but no Glasgow-specific dispute
  record was found online. In Aberdeen the two never separated at all — the
  "Wrights and Coopers" (1527) remain one trade to this day; in Edinburgh the
  coopers were folded into the wrights' and masons' Mary's Chapel from 1489
  [comparative].]
- **Rivals: the four premier crafts.** Hammermen, Tailors, Cordiners and
  Maltmen sued "the last ten trades" including the Coopers in the 1776
  precedence action — formalised condescension, answered in court
  ([Muir 1939](https://hammermenofglasgow.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/3.-Traditions-and-Customs-of-the-Hammermen-of-Glasgow-and-Insignia-and-Relics-%E2%80%93-of-the-The-Incorporation-of-Hammermen-1939-.pdf)).
- **Rivals: the country coopers and the unfree.** The 1665 acts against buying
  rungs "abrod aither in burghe or landwart" and the 1695 ratification against
  "country coopers" define the standing enemy: the man outside the hoop who
  works cheaper. Edinburgh's coopers complained in 1489 of exactly the same
  figure — "outlandis folkis … passand fra hous to hous mendand and spilland
  nychtbouris wirk and stuf, hafand nother stob nor stake within this toune"
  [comparative; [Colston](https://archive.org/details/incorporatedtrad00cols)].
- **Customers and co-dependents.** Maltmen (ale casks), Fleshers (salted meat),
  the fish-curers of the Clyde, and the tobacco and sugar aristocracy whose
  hogsheads and puncheons moved Glasgow's Atlantic wealth
  ([TheGlasgowStory — Trade and Communications](https://www.theglasgowstory.com/story/?id=TGSBD);
  [The Scotsman](https://www.scotsman.com/heritage-and-retro/heritage/how-scots-fishing-towns-boomed-from-sale-of-salted-herring-to-slave-plantations-2936978)).
- **The merchant class.** The standing structural rival of every craft: the
  1583 weaponschaw riot over "the ranking and placing of the merchants and
  craftsmen" preceded the 1605 Letter of Guildry settlement
  ([Electric Scotland — History of Glasgow](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/glasgow/glasgow2_14.htm)).
- **Shared estate.** From the crafts' purchase of the Gorbals lands the Coopers
  held a one thirty-one part share (ratified by Parliament 1661; sasine
  registered 1792) — bound to the other thirteen crafts in land as in
  procession ([Crawfurd](https://archive.org/details/asketchriseandp00crawgoog)).
- **Modern kin.** The Worshipful Company of Coopers of London and the other
  Scottish cooper incorporations; working cooperages (Speyside, distillery
  cooperages) receive the Incorporation's apprentice awards
  ([Trades House — Coopers](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-coopers/);
  [Incorporation — About](https://www.incorporationofcoopersofglasgow.org.uk/about.php)).

## Lexicon (period/craft words a Scots narrator could use)

1. **cowparis / cowperis** — the coopers, as spelled in the 1605–09 records.
   Beware the homonym: in the burgh records "couparis" can also mean *dealers*
   (as in horse-couper) — the 1622 Renfrew herring dispute is about
   fish-dealers, not barrel-makers.
2. **the gedge / jadge** — the gauge; lawful measure of a barrel ("not the full
   gedge"; "the jadge and mesour of Leyth").
3. **bind** — a barrel's statutory build and capacity ("the barell bind of
   salmond suld kepe & contene … xiiij gallonis").
4. **regraitit and compleanit** — "complained of" (the 1653 entry's opening).
5. **seal of cause** — a craft's founding charter from the magistrates.
6. **upset** — the entry fee paid to set up as a master; the 1630 penance was
   "ane new upsett" paid to the craft's poor.
7. **decayed brethren** — members fallen into poverty, owed relief.
8. **the Box** — the craft's chest: funds, charters, relics; "lodged in the
   box"; refused to "rander ye box" (1649).
9. **deacon / collector / late deacon** — the craft's elected officers.
10. **the aithe of obedience** — the entrant's oath binding him to his deacon.
11. **essay** — the trial masterpiece proving a man's hand. [extrapolated for
    Glasgow; documented for Aberdeen's coopers, down to deacon-issued
    "goadges"]
12. **croze** — tool cutting the groove that seats the cask-head; now the
    graduation gift of the trade.
13. **adze, bick iron, belly knife, scullup, shiv, swarth** — cooper's tools,
    named differently on Scotland's east and west coasts.
14. **stave, hoop, chime** — the anatomy of a cask. [general trade usage]
15. **stappis, rungis, stingis** — the imported barrel-wood of the 1638
    decreet; "ventour guidis" — goods brought in on venture (speculation).
16. **cavell / kavill** — a lot or allotted share; "each brother to have his
    cavell" of the Broomielaw timber.
17. **kirne** — a churn; "ane wnsufficient kirne" was seized in the 1630
    search.
18. **halfett** — the side of the head; where Gabriel Allasoun struck his
    deacon.
19. **wnlawis (unlaws)** — fines; what the coopers were rebuked for drinking
    in 1643.
20. **hogshead, puncheon, firkin, last** — capacities; "six lasts and two
    barrels of sufficient herring" was Glasgow's 1561 duty to the Crown.
21. **firlot and peck** — the lawful dry measures, themselves coopered
    vessels; part of the Aberdeen cooper's essay. [comparative]
22. **trussing-in / the blackening** — the barrel-rolling initiation of a
    finished apprentice.
23. **Pompey juice** — the foul steam-cleaning goo used in trussing "muck".
24. **at far hand** — entry with no claim by blood or marriage.
25. **weaponschaw** — the armed muster where precedence riots broke out; the
    deacons' muskets "with staf and bandeleiris" served the same civic duty.
26. **the water bailyie** — the burgh officer who policed the quay and the
    gauge.
27. **crown brand** — the fishery officer's stencil that made a barrel of
    herring trustworthy across Europe.
28. **pass and search and visit** — the Saturday inspection formula of 1569.
29. **druckin humor** — drunken temper; Gabriel Allasoun's confessed state.
30. **be sound of drum** — how burgh law reached the coopers' ears (1613).

## Question Seeds (Dostoyevsky-grade dilemmas rooted in this craft's lore)

1. **The Saturday Search.** It is your week to "pass and search and visit all
   men's work." In your oldest friend's shed you find herring barrels a
   half-inch under the gedge — his wife is ill, his entry fines unpaid, and
   confiscation ruins him; silence cheapens the brand every cooper's family
   eats by. Do you mark the barrels? — *Choosing to mark them reveals HONEST
   MEASURE above mercy; choosing silence reveals BROTHERHOOD above the craft's
   name.* (Rooted in the 1569 search articles and the 1653 gauge
   confiscations.)
2. **The Kirne.** You are Deacon Snype, your cheek still hot where your sworn
   brother struck you at the search — drunk, ashamed, and now standing in the
   House's judgment. The full penance is yours to press: homage at the mercat
   croce before the whole town, and a new upset that will beggar him. Soften
   it, and every future searcher is a little less safe at a stranger's door.
   Press it, and you have used the craft's whole machinery to crush a drunk
   man's worst afternoon. — *Pressing reveals PEER ACCOUNTABILITY as the
   craft's spine, the office above the man; softening reveals BROTHERHOOD that
   remembers the man inside the offender — and quietly teaches the next fist
   its price is negotiable.* (Rooted verbatim in the 1630 Snype–Allasoun case.)
3. **The Cavell.** The decreet has just forced the timber back into common
   bargain: every brother his lot, poor and rich alike. You are one of the
   "specialls" it was aimed at. At the division a poor brother — idle these
   six months, his cavell in his hands and no money to work it — offers you
   his lot at a price that feeds his family tonight and rebuilds your corner
   by spring, clause by lawful clause. Do you buy? — *Buying reveals the
   letter of the law wielded against its soul, THRIFT curdling back into the
   syndicate; refusing reveals EQUALITY AT THE DIVISION as a thing you keep
   even when the rules would let you drop it.* (Rooted in the 1638 decreet,
   which itself legalised the poor brother's right to sell his kavill.)
4. **The Banquet or the Box.** It is 1569 and the vote is called: keep the old
   entry banquet — one roaring night that makes every new man a brother by
   morning — or abolish it and turn the money into the poor-fund for decayed
   brethren you may never meet. Which is the truer fellowship? — *The banquet
   reveals FELLOWSHIP-IN-THE-FLESH; the box reveals BROTHERHOOD AS
   INFRASTRUCTURE, care outlasting the feast.* (Rooted verbatim in the 1569
   charter's substitution.)
5. **The Drum.** 29 May 1613: the drum has cried it through every street —
   from today, Glasgow barrels must be of the gauge of Leith, and yours, made
   honestly to the measure your father taught you, are contraband by
   sundown. A curer downriver, where the drum has not yet been heard, will
   take the whole stock at full price tomorrow. Sell fast, or stave them in?
   — *Selling reveals the letter of honesty (they were true when you made
   them) split from its spirit; staving them reveals HONEST MEASURE as a
   thing owed to the buyer who hasn't heard the drum yet, paid for out of
   your own children's winter.* (Rooted in the 1613 proclamation and the
   1653 confiscations.)
6. **The Plantation Contract.** It is 1798. The curer offers a contract that
   would keep every journeyman in your yard working two years: barrels for the
   West India fleet — herring to feed enslaved people on the plantations. The
   poor-box is nearly empty and winter is coming. Take it? — *Taking it
   reveals DUTY TO YOUR OWN, the hoop drawn tight around the craft; refusing
   reveals HONEST MEASURE extended to the whole world, the belief that a tight
   barrel in a rotten trade still leaks.* (Rooted in the documented 84,782
   barrels of 1798.)
7. **The Country Cooper.** 1695: Parliament has discharged "all country
   coopers to interfere with the trade," and the town itself protested the
   privilege you now hold. The gauger finds a landward man's casks in your
   own sister's curing yard — good work, full gauge, half your price; her
   widowhood runs on the difference. The law is yours to invoke. — *Invoking
   it reveals ADAPTIVE SURVIVAL, the craft defending the wall its poor-box
   is built on; staying silent reveals the suspicion that a privilege even
   your own town protested cannot be justice — and each silent day feeds
   your brethren's children to the cheaper man.* (Rooted in the 1695
   ratification and the town's protest.)
8. **Ninth Place.** The premier crafts have gone to law to fix you ninth in
   every procession until the end of time. Fighting costs the craft's fund
   money that could pension three widows; yielding means every cooper's son
   watches his father walk behind the tailors forever. — *Fighting reveals
   PRIDE OF PLACE as the guardian of the craft's dignity; yielding reveals
   USEFULNESS WITHOUT GLORY, the cooper's knowledge that the barrel doesn't
   care who walks first.* (Rooted in the 1776 Decreet of Declarator.)
9. **The Trussing.** Your apprentice has served his seven years. The yard
   waits with the soot, the feathers, the Pompey juice; his mother is watching.
   He is a proud, fragile man and you could spare him — hand him the croze
   quietly in the office. But no cooper here was ever made journeyman dry and
   clean. — *Rolling the barrel reveals BELONGING THROUGH ORDEAL, initiation
   as love; sparing him reveals the conviction that DIGNITY outranks custom —
   and quietly severs him from every man who went through it.* (Rooted in the
   documented trussing-in/blackening ceremony.)
10. **The Proxy's Patience.** You are David Shearer in 1605, sent to stand in
    another man's name at the new House, unnoticed and unthanked. You could
    force yourself forward now — or serve, season by season, for nineteen years
    until the chair comes to you. — *Seizing reveals AMBITION; the long service
    reveals TIME-SERVED PATIENCE, the cooper's faith that staves bent slowly
    don't split.* (Rooted in the 1605 record and the 1624 convenership;
    same-man identification [extrapolated].)
11. **The Empty Election.** It is the plague autumn of 1645 and the roll will
    say only "Not given." To be deacon is to hold the box, sit the House, and
    walk into sick men's houses with the craft's pension in your hand. No one
    has stood. You are the obvious name. — *Standing reveals BROTHERHOOD AS
    INFRASTRUCTURE carried at the body's own risk, the office as a duty that
    does not pause for death; declining reveals the honest arithmetic of
    self-preservation — and leaves the craft's poor to face the plague year
    with no one's name on it.* (Rooted in the deacon roll's 1645–46 blank;
    the deacon's visiting duties in plague-time are [extrapolated] from the
    craft's documented pension-visitation practice.)
