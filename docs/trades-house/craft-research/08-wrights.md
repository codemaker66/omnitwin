# Incorporation of Wrights

## Essence (the craft's soul in one paragraph)

The Wrights are the joiners — of wood, and of people. Their motto, *"Join all in one,"* is both a workshop instruction and a constitution: this was never one trade but a confederation of them — carpenters, joiners, arkwrights, cartwrights, wheelwrights, boat-wrights, shipwrights, glasswrights, painters, bowyers, sawyers — dovetailed under a single deacon in 1600, and the dovetail joint is literally cut into the partition line of their coat of arms. They are the craft of *fit*: the essay window measured to the half-foot, the four-throw lock on the Essay House door, the workshop inspections "for detecting bad work." And they are the craft that accompanied Glaswegians from cradle to grave — the Wright made the cradle, the roof-tree, the marriage bed, the fire-engine, the steamboat, and at the end the coffin, hired out the mortcloth, and walked as undertaker at the head of the funeral. The largest of Glasgow's fourteen crafts, and the one that keeps its memory in a locked box: a flag said to have flown at Langside, the original Seal of Cause, and a Gold Box whose opening is a secret taught only at the opening itself.

## History (founding, seal of cause date, key episodes)

- **The legend of 1057.** The modern Trades House states that "King Malcolm III of Scotland granted the Craftsmen a Royal Charter in 1057" in recognition of the craftsmen building Glasgow Cathedral ([Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/); [Incorporation of Wrights — Heritage](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/heritage.php)). **Caution:** the Incorporation's own detailed 1900 history and the 2025 Bryce history treat the craft's documented life as beginning far later; the 1057 charter is origin-myth territory, not archive fact. Treat it as the story the craft tells about being as old as the Cathedral's timber roof.
- **Before independence.** The Wrights were "joined together in one Letter of Deaconry" with the Masons and Coopers. Scots law spent a century trying to crush craft deacons — a statute of Queen Mary, c. 1555, ordered "that all deakins sall ceas for a year at least (**especially masonis an wrichtis**)" — the wrights and masons singled out by name as the most fractious. Mary repealed the prohibition on 16 April 1556 on the advice of the Queen Regent, Mary of Guise ([Bryce, *The History of the Incorporation of Wrights in Glasgow*, 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Seal of Cause, 3 May 1600.** Granted by the Provost and Magistrates of Glasgow on the petition of "the Deacon, Headmen, and Masters of the Wright Craft… and also Glazing-wrights, Boat-wrights, Painters, Bowyers, and Sawyers." The petition's stated reason for divorcing the Masons is the craft's creed in one line: *"the said masons could not judge upon our work, nor we upon theirs perfectly."* The Seal enacted that no one work the craft in Glasgow "until he be first made burgess and freeman… and be examined by three or four masters of the said crafts if he be a sufficient workman" ([1900 book, full text](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt); [Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **1605 and eighth place.** When the Letter of Guildry created the Trades House in 1605, the Wrights chose not to join as a senior craft, and so rank "eighth in line of seniority" among the fourteen ([Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/)). The order of precedence was finally fixed by the Court of Session's "Grand Decerniture" of 28 February 1777, after the Wrights and nine other crafts sued for equal representation; Bryce notes drily that "the reason for the precedence has been lost in time" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **The Covenant minute, 22 February 1650.** The earliest surviving Register of Acts opens in the shadow of the Solemn League and Covenant: the Wrights decreed "all in ane uoyce" that every meeting open and close with prayer, and that their Deacon be "ane able man most qvalified and worthie, of known affectiovne vnto, and approved fidelitie in the cavse of God… neither gviltie of scandovlovs sines, nor processit and sencvrit for any svch" ([Electric Scotland — Register of Acts](https://electricscotland.com/history/wrights.htm); [Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Trades Hall, 1791–1794.** The Wrights had a proprietary hand in the crafts' great house: the Glassford Street ground was bought from William Horn, Wright (Deacon 1769); the Wrights' own James Jaffray (Deacon 1780) drew the first plans — "the one with a spire was preferred" — before the Masons pressed for other architects and **Robert Adam's design was viewed as the best**. The Hall was completed in 1794 at £7,207; the Wrights' subscription rose from £356 to £681 10s. 3d., and their first meeting there was 19 September 1794 ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf); [1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **The end of monopoly, 14 May 1846.** The Burgh Trading Act swept away every exclusive privilege. The Wrights voted not to wind up and divide the funds, but "to channel the energy and enterprise of the Craft into the work of benevolence" — which remains its work today, with almost 2,000 members, the largest of the fourteen ([Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/); [Wrights — Heritage](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/heritage.php)).
- **Modern marks.** Arms matriculated with the Lord Lyon, 17 March 1924. Awards scheme for young carpenters instituted 1979. First female Deacon, Jenny McIntyre, 2015 ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf); [Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/)).

## Legends & Episodes

- **Saving the Cathedral, 1579.** The defining legend. When Mr. Melville, Principal of the College, "assembled a crowd by tuck of drum, to pull down the Cathedral," the Wrights and Masons — not yet even separated — ran to arms and told him "that if any person presumed to pull down a single stone of the church, he should, at that moment, be buried under it." Only the Magistrates' intervention saved Melville and his adherents from being put to death ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). The craft that frames roofs was ready to kill to keep one standing.
- **The Langside flag.** Inside the Deacon's Box, beside the original Seal of Cause, lies "a flag or sash, which is said to have been carried at the battle of Langside" (1568) — where 600 Glasgow men helped the Regent Moray break Mary Queen of Scots' army ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt); [Wikipedia — Battle of Langside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Langside)). Provenance unproven — which is exactly what makes it a relic.
- **The deacon as war-captain.** In the old burgh order the Deacon "chose the Craft's quota for national military service and led the craftsmen in battle with his Collector as second in command," and answered for their arms at the statutory Wappenschaw ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). Francis Crawfurd, Wright and Deacon Convener 1765 — a man with twenty children — "carried the colours of the Glasgow Volunteers at the battle of Falkirk, on 17th January 1746"; when he died in office all the corporations "walked in procession, each trade by itself" behind his coffin. In 1778 the craft gave £100 "For raising Glasgow Regiment," and on 2 August 1803, against Napoleon, it backed "The Trades' Battalion of Glasgow Volunteers," six hundred craftsmen strong.
- **The fire of 1652 and the last glasswright.** After "the lait suddent fyre" of 1652 gutted a third of Glasgow, the town discovered that every glasswright in the burgh "wer depairted this lyfe" except one, James Elphanstoune — and had to beg glaziers from Edinburgh, Linlithgow and Aberdeen. Into that vacuum stepped the brothers James and John Corssis, freemen wrights, who undercut the "great and exorbitant pryce tackin for the foot of glas" from five shillings to three shillings and eightpence, and offered "to have thair wark tryed to the full" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). Price war as public service, with a quality-inspection clause.
- **James Colquhoun's fire-engine.** Deacon 1634, "he made the first fire-engine used in Glasgow" and was the Town Council's man for everything from re-roofing the Cathedral to "mending the letters of the hail horologis for the better knowing of the houris" ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)). The Colquhoun name recurs in the deacons' roll for a century — a dynasty of wrights.
- **Henry Bell made the essay.** The pioneer of steam navigation in Europe — builder of the *Comet* — joined the Incorporation on 20 October 1797 "and made the usual Trade Essay" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). No exemption for genius: the man who would change shipping proved himself on a window and a footstool like everyone else.
- **The 296.** In 1786, shortly before a Clerk's election, 296 new members were suddenly admitted — "it is too apparent that the object was to create votes." The scandal produced the rule (1790) that no entrant may vote until "a year and a day" had passed, the Deacon warning of "the great evil which arises to Corporations by admitting members in the view only of having their vote on a canvass in the trade" ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **Robert Dickie's slander, 1673.** For calling the Deacon and "other honest men of the trade bot pendicles, with severall other railling, vilifieing, and threatening expressions," Dickie was fined twenty merks Scots and barred from office forever — and the court banned the whole craft from ever calling a member a "pendicle" again, on pain of five pounds Scots ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). In this craft, the deadliest insult was to say a man had never made his essay.
- **Even the Deacon pays.** 1790: "Deacon Charles Pirrie was fined one guinea for granting precepts inconsistent with the acts or customs of the Trade" ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)). The law of the craft outranked its own chair.
- **The widow's ten days, 1657.** The town granted "the relict of vmquhill James Rodger… licence to keipe and deteine the twa strangers wrightes that shoe hes, for ten dayes or thairby, for compleiting of hir house" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)) — a monopoly bending, briefly and exactly ten days' worth, for a widow.
- **Wrights in their carriages.** Allan Dreghorn, son of Deacon Robert Dreghorn, bought the estate of Ruchill and in 1752 "drove in his own carriage, the first private carriage known in Glasgow" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). Timber money built half of polite Glasgow.
- **The moderns.** Sir William Arrol (joined 1882), builder of the Forth Bridge and Tower Bridge; Sir William Burrell, who gave Glasgow £1m in art treasures; John Keppie (joined 1889, Deacon 1906), partner of Charles Rennie Mackintosh; James Sellars, architect of St Andrew's Halls, known as "The Wee Troot"; Dr James Cleland, statistician and Superintendent of Public Works ([Wrights — home](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/); [Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).

## Patron, Arms & Motto

- **Motto:** *"Join all in one."* No Latin, no French — a carpenter's plain imperative, doubling as the constitutional principle of a craft that absorbed a dozen trades ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt); [Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Pre-1924 arms (used for centuries, never matriculated):** "Argent: a chevron engrailed sable, between two Wrights' compasses expanded in chief, and a square and compasses conjoined in base, proper; on a chief or, on a pale of the second between two roses gules an escallop of the first" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). Compasses and square: the tools of measure, not of cutting — the Wrights blazoned *judgment*, not force.
- **1924 arms (Patent from the Lord Lyon, 17 March 1924):** "Parted per fess **dovetailed** Gules and Or, in chief an axe and ancient Scots nail hammer in saltire, surmounted of a Wrights' chisel edge downwards paleways, and in base the half-length figure of **St. Kentigern** affrontee, vested and mitred, his right hand raised in the act of benediction… and in an Escrol below the shield this motto: 'Join all in one'" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). The shield is literally *joined* by a dovetail — heraldry as carpentry joke and creed at once.
- **Patron saint:** The arms and the modern seal carry **St Kentigern (Mungo)**, Glasgow's own saint — the seal shows him with crozier, Celtic bell, the salmon and ring, an oak sapling with the robin, and a hawthorn, the withered-branch miracle read as the craft's charity: "just as St. Mungo by his words and love revived the broken branch… so does the Incorporation by its help and care revivify and cherish those who may be broken by misfortune" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)). The face of the saint on the seal is a portrait of Deacon Samuel Stevenson, and the salmon a joke about his angling. **[extrapolated]** No source found names the Wrights' pre-Reformation altar saint; Bryce confirms each craft kept an altar to "the particular saint whom a Craft had chosen" but not which. St Joseph the Carpenter is the generic patron of the trade across Christendom and may be assumed for color only, not asserted as Glasgow fact.

## Rituals & Customs

- **The Essay (masterpiece).** By minute of 24 February 1792: "a window, not less than two and one-half feet square… six lozens and beeded. A bound shutter four feet high… A knife box, fifteen inches long at the top, and bevelled both ways… A footstool sixteen inches square; and if a bound door, six feet high… not less than three panels. **All to be made from rough wood in the Essay Room.**" ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt))
- **The Essay House.** To prevent fraud, in 1781 the court ordered its door "secured with a new lock of four throws, with a key to each throw; and that each of the four Essay Masters be possessed of one of the said keys," with windows and vent-heads grated ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)). Four masters, four keys, one door: no single man could let a cheat in.
- **Freeman and Pendicle.** A Freeman had paid his freedom fine, taken "the oath *de fideli*," and "made and reported a sufficient essay." A Pendicle had not made the essay and could not practise until he did. Pendicle admission ended in 1876; today every applicant makes an essay "or pays a fine of five pounds in lieu thereof" ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **"A penny win ye."** On taking the oath and signing the roll, the entrant shakes hands with every member of the Master Court, each of whom says: *"I wish you a penny won,"* or *"A penny win ye"* — a blessing of honest earnings ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Entry at the Near Hand / Far Hand.** Sons and sons-in-law of members enter cheaply "at the near hand"; strangers "at the far hand" pay more ([Trades House — Wrights](https://www.tradeshouse.org.uk/crafts-wrights/)). Some families trace membership "back continuously for seven or eight generations" ([Wrights — Heritage](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/heritage.php)).
- **Choosing Day and the Choosing Dinner (early October).** Election day was once "the day of days in the Craft year… Drums rattled, trumpets blared, and pipes skirled, while much strong ale, aye and French wine, disappeared down the 'craigis' of the craftsmen." The modern Choosing Dinner keeps the fellowship of "The Auld Hoose"; the Deacon Convener formally visits ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Prayer, since 1650.** Master Court meetings open and close with prayer, by the Covenant-era decree ([Electric Scotland](https://electricscotland.com/history/wrights.htm)).
- **The Deacon's Box and its keys.** Two locks: the "Big Key" (Trade's Key) and the "Wee Key" (Deacon's Key), the keepers once called "Gowdie" — Keepers of the Gold. Inside: the original Seal of Cause, the Langside flag, and the **Gold Box**, "the art of opening it is supposed to be a secret to all who have not been at an opening." At the annual **Box Opening Dinner** the Deacon deposits a memento of the year — the box holds everything from a piece of 500-year-old oak from the Cathedral roof to a ribbon from the Riverside Museum opening ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf); [1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **Chain and Queen.** The Deacon's chain (1851) carries "gold links bearing the name of every Deacon since that time" — extended in 2025 to keep the unbroken record. Since 1963 the Deacon's consort — the **"Queen of the Wrights"** — wears the original 1936 badge on her own chain ([Wrights — Heritage](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/heritage.php); [Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **The Master Court bell.** Presented 2017 by Deacon Dorothy Newlands of Lauriston, rung to call the Court to order, "and once a year it makes the journey to London" ([Wrights — Heritage](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/heritage.php)).
- **Kirking of the House.** The Sunday after the Deacon Convener's Choosing Day, all fourteen crafts process behind the Mace into Glasgow Cathedral for divine service — the Wrights' arms are carved on Cathedral pews the crafts gifted in the 1950s ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).
- **Mortcloths and deid bells.** Renting the funeral pall was serious revenue — cloths in three sizes ("bairns'," "youths'," "meikle") and qualities; a joint Mortcloth Company with the Tailors and Weavers from 1774 (the Wrights held nine shares). Two "Deid Bells" survive (1809, 1824), rung to announce deaths ([1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **Quality patrol.** Until the Lammas Court of 1830, the Incorporation "regularly inspect[ed] all Wrights' workshops within the Royalty… for the purpose of detecting bad work," and prosecuted "cardowers" — unfree botchers — for fines ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf); [1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt)).
- **The Piper's Dram.** A pewter quaich (given 1993–94) intended for the piper's whisky at Choosing Dinners; Lord Forte's silver goblet for the Wrights' Deacon has carved wooden staves for its shaft ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf)).

## Sensory World

Oak above all — cathedral-roof oak five centuries old, kept as a relic; oak for the modern Deacon's box "with dovetailed corners." Ash for the funeral handspokes, "about two and a half inches square… rounded to the ends to afford an easy grip." Rough wood in the Essay Room becoming lozens, beeded shutters, three-panel bound doors under saw, chisel, axe and the "ancient Scots nail hammer" of the arms. Sawpits and timber yards off the Candleriggs; glue-pots and shavings; the shriek of the pit-saw and the knock of the mallet. The tuck of the drum — drummers paid when the Deacon was chosen (1701), the mob summoned "by tuck of drum" to pull down the Cathedral (1579), ordinances "intimat be touk of drume." The clatter of the Crafts' Hospital bell "out a requiem," and the deid bell in the street. Velvet mortcloths spread "with much ceremony… as a mantle" over coffins; kid gloves laid out at the kistin; whisky from a wooden cuttie and ale from a capie cogie; "strong ale, aye and French wine" going down craftsmen's craigis on Choosing Day. The reek of the 1652 fire, and glass at four shillings the foot. Later: iron and rivets on the Forth Bridge, steam off the *Comet*'s boiler, varnish on a Sellars façade — the wright's world widening from the bench to the Clyde ([Bryce 2025](https://www.tradeshouselibrary.org/uploads/4/7/7/2/47723681/final_combined_pdf_history.pdf); [1900 book](http://archive.org/stream/incorporationofw00inco/incorporationofw00inco_djvu.txt); [Wrights — home](https://www.wrightsinglasgow.org.uk/)).

## Values

1. **Union — "Join all in one."** The motto, the dovetailed shield, and the craft's whole structure: glaziers, painters, bowyers, sawyers, boat-wrights petitioning as one body in 1600, and a Trades Hall built on a Wright's land to house all fourteen crafts. The Wrights' identity *is* confederation.
2. **Proof by work.** The essay made "from rough wood in the Essay Room," Henry Bell making "the usual Trade Essay" despite being Henry Bell, and the rule that no one holds office "unless he has made an Essay." Your standing rests on what your hands have demonstrably done.
3. **Right judgment by peers.** The founding grievance of 1600: "the said masons could not judge upon our work, nor we upon theirs perfectly." Only those who know the craft may judge the craft — the Seal of Cause requires examination "by three or four masters."
4. **Guardianship of the standard.** Workshop inspections "for detecting bad work," cardowing fines, the four-keyed Essay House against fraud, the Corssis brothers inviting the town to "have thair wark tryed to the full." Quality is policed, not presumed.
5. **Care from cradle to grave.** The Trades' Hospital contributions from 1605, mortcloths and the Wright-as-undertaker, the 1846 refusal to cash out — choosing "the work of benevolence" instead — and today GalGael, veterans' woodwork, and the *Glenlee* apprenticeships. The craft buries and provides for its own, and for the city.
6. **Memory in joinery.** A chain carrying every Deacon's name since 1851 (extended in 2025 rather than break the record), the Gold Box accreting one relic per Deacon per year, seven-generation families, 500-year-old Cathedral oak kept like a saint's bone. Continuity is a made object, maintained like a joint.
7. **Law above the chair.** Deacon Pirrie fined a guinea by his own court in 1790; the 1650 decree that a Deacon must be blameless before he may lead; the year-and-a-day rule to purge vote-packing. The craft's rules bind its most powerful first.

## Shadow

Every Wright virtue is a joint that can be forced. **Union curdles into faction**: "Join all in one" became, in 1786, jamming 296 warm bodies onto the roll to steal an election — union as arithmetic instead of fellowship. **Guardianship curdles into persecution**: the same court that policed "bad work" hunted "cardowers" — poor unfree men working for bread — and metered the Gorbals wrights to two days a week in the city for a fee, then litigated the contract's corpse in 1780. **Judgment-by-peers curdles into demarcation war**: Archibald Reid fined ten pounds in 1610 for "outstreking ane void" — cutting an opening in a wall — because a hole is *masons'* property; the work stood unfinished while the courts decided whose hole it was. **Proof-by-work curdles into a toll**: the essay you may now escape for a five-pound fine — the test become a fee, the roll filling with hands never tried. **Memory curdles into ancestor worship**: a craft that keeps a flag it cannot prove flew at Langside, and names on gold links, can come to love its box of relics more than the living apprentice at the bench. The Wright's shadow is the closed shop: a fellowship of joiners who joined everything — except the people outside the door.

## Rivals & Kin

- **The Masons — the great other.** One Letter of Deaconry until 1600; the divorce was filed on grounds of mutual incomprehension ("could not judge upon our work, nor we upon theirs"). Then decades of demarcation war: the 1607 ruling that wrights Petegrue and Reid could finish but not begin "slating and pointing" work; the 1610 *outstreking ane void* fine. Yet in 1579 they stood "side by side" with drawn weapons to save the Cathedral, and it was the Masons who pushed the Trades Hall commission toward Robert Adam over the Wrights' own architect. Sibling rivalry in the fullest sense: the two crafts of the building shell, forever disputing the seam where wood meets stone.
- **The Coopers — the one that got away.** Fellow members of the old joint deaconry who "separated themselves from us… and obtained a separate Letter of Deaconry" first, providing the precedent the Wrights cited in 1600.
- **The absorbed kin.** Glazing-wrights, boat-wrights, painters, bowyers, sawyers signed the 1600 petition, but "do not appear… to have submitted themselves at once": the Painters held out **ninety-six years**, finally submitting on 17 September 1696 ("the only painters now in Glasgow" — all three of them), the plasterers following a week later. Glaziers were "a pendicle" of the craft; in 1804 William Cairney was admitted as glazier only, "debarred from exercising any other branch… until he should have made and reported a sufficient essay as a Wright."
- **The Wrights of Gorbals — the tolerated outsiders.** By a 1683 contract, Gorbals and Calton wrights could bring work into the city "upon two days each week only," at 15s Scots a year. The agreement died around 1780 amid mutual accusations of breach.
- **Partners in death.** The Mortcloth Company of 1774, jointly with the **Tailors** and **Weavers** — the three crafts that dressed, draped and boxed the Glasgow corpse, incorporated together.
- **The unfree.** Cardowers, "stranger wrights," journeymen without the freedom — the permanent underclass against whom the monopoly was enforced, fined, and occasionally, as for James Rodger's widow, mercifully waived.

## Lexicon

1. **Wright** — a maker; a worker in wood; the root of arkwright, cartwright, shipwright, wheelwright.
2. **Essay** — the masterpiece test-piece; "made and reported a sufficient essay."
3. **Essay Room / Essay House** — the locked workshop where the essay was made under the four Essay Masters' four keys.
4. **Pendicle** — a member who never made the essay; also the deadliest insult in the craft (see Dickie, 1673).
5. **Cardower / cardowing** — an unfree botcher; to work clumsily without the freedom. "Received fines for cardowing John Mills, 0/18."
6. **Lozen** — a window pane. **Beeded** — beaded, finished with a moulding.
7. **Outstreking ane void** — striking out an opening in a wall; the phrase that cost Archibald Reid ten pounds.
8. **Touk of drume** — beat of the drum; how ordinances (and mobs) were raised.
9. **Wappenschaw** — the statutory weapon-showing at which the Deacon mustered his armed craftsmen.
10. **A penny win ye** — the Master Court's handshake blessing to a new entrant: honest earnings.
11. **Near hand / far hand** — entry as kin of a member / as a stranger.
12. **Freedom fine** — the entry payment that made a man free of the craft.
13. **Mortcloth** — the hired funeral pall; in **bairns'**, **youths'** and **meikle** (big) sizes.
14. **Deid bell** — the handbell rung through the streets to announce a death.
15. **Saulies** — hired mourners in sable, with black batons, heading the procession to the **Hie Kirkyeird**.
16. **Kistin** — the coffining ceremony; **deid-meit** — the funeral refreshments, "mostly of a spiritual nature"; **streetchin brod** — the stretching-board the corpse was laid on; **handspokes** — the ash carrying-poles.
17. **Phial / fial** — wages, salary: "Joseph Crombie (the Clerk), his Phial £5 5 0."
18. **Tail-shaking** — the Collector's understood treat after a Court meeting, spent on refreshment.
19. **Gowdie / Wee Key / Big Key** — the keepers and keys of the Deacon's Box; "Keepers of the Gold."
20. **Relict of umquhill** — widow of the late; **merk** — two-thirds of a pound Scots; **Choosing** — the annual election; **the Auld Hoose** — the Trades House fellowship; **Chain Gang** — the deacons of all fourteen crafts on their round of Choosing Dinners.

## Question Seeds

1. **The Cardower's Bairns.** You are an Essay Master. You catch a man "cardowing" — unfree, unexamined, nailing up botched shutters for half-price in a back close — and his children are visibly starving. The fine you must levy goes to the Crafts' Hospital that feeds *members'* widows. Prosecute him, as the standard and your oath demand, or walk past and let bad work stand? — *Choosing prosecution reveals GUARDIANSHIP OF THE STANDARD; choosing to walk past reveals CARE, and exposes whether the candidate's charity extends beyond the brotherhood's walls.*
2. **Outstreking ane Void.** The job cannot be finished unless the wall is opened, and the wall, by charter, belongs to the Masons. Send for a mason and lose the week and the fee — or take the ten-pound fine like Archibald Reid and cut the hole yourself, because the work must be *finished right*? — *Waiting for the mason reveals RIGHT JUDGMENT BY PEERS (respect for the seam); cutting reveals PROOF BY WORK curdling toward its shadow — completion worshipped above the compact.*
3. **The 296.** The election is close, and your side is right — you are certain of it. The rolls are open; you could book two hundred journeymen at the near hand tonight, every admission legal, every vote yours. The craft's cause wins, and the craft's soul takes the wound. Do you fill the roll? — *Refusing reveals UNION held as principle; filling it reveals union held as arithmetic — the shadow's own signature.*
4. **A Single Stone.** 1579. Melville's crowd is at the Cathedral door with drums and crowbars, and the law — the Reformation, the College, perhaps God — is arguably on their side. Your grandfather framed that roof. Do you take up arms and promise a man he'll be "buried under" the first stone he pulls, or stand aside for the magistrates and the age to decide? — *Taking arms reveals MEMORY IN JOINERY (the dead's work defended like kin); standing aside reveals LAW ABOVE THE CHAIR — order held even against your own heart.*
5. **The Widow's Ten Days.** James Rodger died with his house half-built, and his widow begs leave to keep his two "stranger wrights" — unfree men — ten days more to close the roof before winter. Every exception gnaws the monopoly that feeds the hospital. Grant it? For ten days only? And what do you say to the freeman who was refused that same week? — *Granting reveals CARE FROM CRADLE TO GRAVE; refusing reveals GUARDIANSHIP — and the candidate must feel what the monopoly costs the merciful.*
6. **Five Pounds in Lieu.** You may make the essay — window, shutter, knife box, footstool, "all from rough wood" — or pay five pounds and never be tried. Your hands are good; you know it; no one else ever will. The roll reads the same either way. Which do you choose, and why does it matter if nobody checks? — *Making the essay reveals PROOF BY WORK as identity, not compliance; paying reveals the toll-taker's soul — the shadow of a craft that once locked its Essay House with four keys.*
