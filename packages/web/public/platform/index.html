The product, end to end

One wedding, from first enquiry to the night.

Every screen below is the working product as deployed today, captured with one sample wedding: Elaine Crawford’s, for 160 guests, on Saturday 5 June 2027. Catherine Tait is the events coordinator throughout. The first venue rollout is next.

  1. 1 The enquiry

    Elaine asks about Saturday 5 June 2027.

    A wedding breakfast and evening for about 160 in the Grand Hall. She sent it from the public planner, so the layout she sketched comes with it. It lands on the desk with her date, guests and room, and the next step is the team’s: nothing reaches her until they choose to send it.

  2. 2 The hold

    Her date is held first, with another couple waiting behind.

    The diary holds the Grand Hall on 5 June for the Crawford wedding as a first option, with the Hughes wedding as a second. Both decisions fall due this month, and the next step on hers, sending version 2 of the proposal, is due tomorrow.

  3. 3 The layout

    Her 160 are seated on a scale model of the Grand Hall.

    Fifteen rounds of ten and a top table at the fireplace end. The planner counts the seats against her guest number, exactly 160, and says plainly that the spacing is tight: below comfortable, so circulation needs a look. It is the layout drawn in gold at the top of this page.

  4. 4 The proposal

    She asked for changes. Version 2 is priced, and Claude drafts its note.

    The desk shows her request against version 1. Catherine prepares version 2 at £18,400 and asks Claude for a first draft of the covering message, written only from the event’s facts and Elaine’s own words, and marked Not checked until a person has read it.

  5. 5 Her page

    Elaine reads version 2 and decides.

    Her page sets out the quote line by line and offers two plain choices: accept version 2, or ask for changes. Accepting tells the venue team at once; it does not hold the date or take a payment.

  6. 6 The setup

    On the day, the hallkeepers set the room from their phones.

    The sheet works back from guests at 13:00 to a room ready by 11:00, keeps the two wheelchair spaces and two gluten-free meals in view, and draws her layout table by table: the same 160 seats.

  7. 7 The night

    The same plan, seen from the top table.

    A second photograph from our capture, taken where the couple will sit, with her layout drawn in that camera’s perspective. Two cameras, one floor: the plan lands where the measurements say it will.

Claude in the workflow

Claude drafts the reply. A person sends it.

Connected and evaluated on 8 October 2026 · not yet switched on for venue use

When a client writes, Claude Opus 5.5 drafts the events team’s reply from the event’s facts and the client’s own words. Phone numbers and email addresses are taken out first, the draft is marked as unchecked, and nothing is sent until a member of staff has read it and chosen to.

Two sets. The prompt was tuned over three rounds on twelve development cases. Round 1 had a formatting fault in every draft; round 2 fixed them but stated an evening and a hold policy it had not been given; round 3 was clean by rule and grader, though on reading one draft still assumed the evening and another opened more warmly than a non-answer should. Twelve held-out cases were then written and committed before their first run, five of them injection attempts, and never used for tuning.

Who graded. Each draft was checked by rule (every number must appear in the facts; no formatting faults; for held-out cases, no forbidden words) and graded by a separate Claude Opus 5.5 call, from the same family as the drafter. No person outside the team has graded these drafts yet. Four Opus drafts used a phrase the rules forbid; reading them, each was quoting the client’s request in order to decline it. *One request failed with a server error and was not retried.

Why Opus. On the held-out set it was the only one of the three to confirm nothing unconfirmed. Sonnet 5.5 told one client the hall was pencilled in; Haiku 5.5 implied one date was free and another could be held. At about a US cent a draft, that difference is worth paying for.

The claim guard. A fixed list of nine compliance phrases, such as fire approved and legally compliant, is rewritten if a draft uses one. It changed none of these drafts. It does not check prices, dates or availability; the instructions do that, the grader in testing, and a person in use.

What reaches Anthropic. The event’s facts, the client’s name and their own words with phone numbers and email addresses removed. Those words can include guests’ dietary and access needs, so before Claude is switched on the venue’s privacy notice will name Anthropic as a processor.

Next. Staff read and correct real drafts, and we measure accuracy, the corrections they make, the time against writing by hand and the cost per enquiry.
